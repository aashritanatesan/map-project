# Chapter Twenty-Three

The Hollow lives underground.

I don't understand it at first. The old woman leads us down off the rocks above the cave, slowly, leaning on her staff, and the others close in behind us with their spears held loosely, not pointed at anything, not quite pointed away. Nobody speaks. We walk for half an hour through the steaming grey wilderness. Then the old woman stops beside a sinkhole like any other, a black round mouth in the ground with a funnel of scree running down into it, and simply starts down.

There are steps. Cut into the scree, hidden under a skin of loose stones. Then there's a ledge, and an opening in the rock wall of the sinkhole, and beyond the opening, light.

It's a town. A small one, in a cave so big I can't see its roof. Lamps burn in niches in the rock. Hot springs steam in channels cut into the floor, and the air is warm and wet and smells of sulphur and woodsmoke and cooking. There are houses, built of stacked slate against the cave walls, with doors of hide. There are children. There are old people sitting by the springs. There are perhaps fifty souls in all, and every one of them stops and turns as we come in, and looks at us.

They look like Highwood. Black hair, brown skin, amber eyes. They look like Highwood the way a drawing looks like the thing it's drawn from: the same lines, but thinner, harder, scraped down to the bone.

Some of them are wrong.

I see a man by the nearest spring with a face that's almost a face: the jaw too long, the nose too broad, the ears too high and too pointed, and grey fur growing down his cheeks to his throat. I see a girl, no older than Bet, whose hands are long and dark and tipped with curved black claws. I see a woman whose spine curves forward in a long hunch under her coat, as if she's stopped halfway through bending down to the ground and never stood up again.

I remember the arm on the cart. *Halfway between one thing and another.*

"The half-shifted," Mara says, very low, beside me. Her voice is strange. "They get stuck. If they try to turn on ground that isn't theirs. Sometimes they just stay like that."

"For how long?"

"For ever."

The girl with the clawed hands is staring at my chain. When I look at her, she hides her hands behind her back.

---

The old woman sits us down by a spring at the back of the cave, on a ledge worn smooth by years of sitting. Someone brings bowls: broth, thin and hot and tasting of roots and something gamey. Someone else brings water. Nobody brings spears. They've vanished, along with most of the people who carried them.

Two things are laid at our feet, without a word.

The first is the pry bar. Its tip is still scraped bright.

The second is Mara's bow, its cut string dangling, and her quiver, refilled with every red-fletched arrow she dropped.

Mara stares at them. Then up at the old woman.

"The hunters' camp," says the old woman. "They ran in the night. They left everything." Her mouth twitches. "Your pony took most of it with him, surveyor. My grandsons found what was left."

"He's not my pony," I say faintly.

"No," she agrees. "He was the Office man's. And the Office man walked out of the Hollow yesterday at noon, south and west, toward the city, on foot, swearing." She settles herself on the ledge across the spring from us, leaning on her staff. "Not very fast. He's not a walker. But he'll get there."

"How far ahead?"

"Two days. Three, if he's as soft as he looks." She studies me. "You'll want to be quicker."

"We're going back to the line," says Mara. "To Nineteen."

"I know." The old woman's eyes haven't left me. "I'll send a boy with you. There's a way up out of the Hollow that comes out above Twenty. It'll save you half a day." A pause. "In exchange, you'll tell me something."

"What?"

"How long?"

It's what Ruth asked. The exact same words, in the exact same tone.

I look at Mara. Mara is looking at the old woman with an expression I can't read.

"Five days," I say. "No. Ten, now. At the ravine."

The old woman closes her eyes and breathes in. The same long slow breath as at the mouth of the cave.

"Ten days," she says. "And you're still alive. Both of you." She opens her eyes. "That's a good start."

---

"You knew," says Mara. "On the rocks. Before you saw us. You *smelled* it."

"Of course I smelled it." The old woman sounds almost amused. "You smell of each other right through. Like two loaves baked in the same oven." She tilts her head. "Your mother would have smelled it in a minute, Ruth's girl. Did she?"

Mara goes stiff. "You know my mother?"

"Everyone in the Hollow knows Ruth of Highwood." Something in the old woman's voice changes. It goes drier. Older. "Her grandmother put three of my family out of the hold under Clause Twelve. For marrying across packs. Did she ever tell you that?"

Mara doesn't answer.

"No," says the old woman. "I didn't think so." She looks away, across the warm dim cave, at the man by the spring with grey fur down his cheeks. "Most of us here were put out of somewhere. Under some clause or other. Twelve, or Six, or Twenty. For marrying wrong, or speaking wrong, or being born to the wrong mother. The treaty has a great many clauses, surveyor. It doesn't have any ground for us."

I think about the floor of the map room in Stonebridge. The blank grey patch with its little round pits, like pumice. No ochre. No lines. No numbers.

"I'm sorry," I say.

"Don't be sorry. Be a surveyor." She turns back to me. "Your survey. The true line. You're going to file it."

"Yes."

"In front of the Registrar."

"In front of everyone."

"Good." She nods, slowly. "Then they'll come for you. Not hunters. Not men with pry bars." Her eyes are very steady. "Law. Ledgers. Clauses. That's what they'll come with. The worst things in the world are written down."

"I know," I say.

"No, you don't. Not yet." She leans on her staff. "But you will."

---

We sleep in the Hollow that night, in a little stone house near the back of the cave, on a heap of furs that smell of smoke. Mara lies curled against me with her bandaged wrists tucked under her chin, so still and quiet that I think she must be asleep. I'm not. I lie awake and listen to the springs, and the soft sounds of fifty people sleeping in the dark, and think about clauses.

Before she sleeps, though, Mara says something.

We're lying in the dark in the little stone house, under the smoky furs, and she's quiet for so long I think she's already gone. Then she says, to the ceiling: "I didn't know."

"About Clause Twelve?"

"About any of it. My great-grandmother. The three she put out." Her voice is flat and careful, the way it gets when she's carrying something heavy. "We sing the border songs every full moon. Every cairn we ever lost. Every mile. We carved them on the posts in the hall so we'd never forget them." A breath. "Nobody ever carved a song for the ones we threw out."

I find her hand under the furs.

"That girl," she says. "By the spring. With the hands." Her fingers tighten on mine. "She's maybe nine. She'll never run. Not once. Not on any ground. Not ever. And I've been so angry, my whole life, about the Long Wood." She laughs, a short, ugly sound. "Four miles. I was angry about four miles."

"You're allowed to be angry about four miles."

"I know. I just—" She stops. "I thought we were the ones being robbed. I never thought about who we'd robbed."

I don't have an answer for that. I don't think there is one. So I just hold her hand, and after a while I feel her breathing slow, and the knot of shame in the tether loosen just a little, and she sleeps.

I lie in the dark beside her and think: *It doesn't have any ground for us.*

I'm a surveyor. Ground is what I do.

Some time in the night, I get up and go out.

The old woman is still sitting by the spring at the back of the cave. I don't think she's moved. Above her, high up, there's a crack in the roof of the cave, a long ragged slit of sky, and through it, so faint I almost miss it, the light of the moon. Waning now. Not full any more. But still bright enough to lay a thin silver stripe across the floor of the cave, right where the old woman sits.

She looks up when I come. I step into the silver stripe without thinking.

She sees my eyes.

I see her see them. I see her breath catch. And I know what she's seeing, the thin gold ring, faint now, a few nights past full, but there.

"You said two," I say quietly. "On the rocks. *Two in one lifetime.* You said the first one was a miracle."

The old woman doesn't answer for a long time. The spring talks to itself in its stone channel.

"Who was the first?" I ask.

"Sit down, surveyor."

I sit.

"I'm old," says the old woman. "I've seen a great many things. I've seen wolves stuck halfway for thirty years. I've seen children born with claws. I've seen Highwood throw its own out into the snow for loving the wrong person." She folds her hands on top of her staff. "And once, a long time ago, I saw a human catch the tether. I didn't think I'd ever see it again."

My heart is going very fast.

"With who?" I ask. "With someone from the Hollow?"

The old woman looks at me. Her face gives away nothing. It's the face of someone who has kept a great many secrets for a very long time, and knows exactly how much each one weighs.

"I don't say the names of my people to the Office," she says. "Not even to a surveyor I like."

"I'm not the Office."

"Not tonight." She almost smiles. "Ask me again after you've filed your survey. We'll see what you are then."

---

In the morning, before we leave, she gives me something.

She does it quietly, at the foot of the hidden steps, while Mara is a little way off talking to the boy who's going to guide us, a thin solemn creature of twelve or so with a sling at his belt. The old woman takes my hand, my good one, and turns it over, and presses something into my palm, and closes my fingers over it.

It's small and cold and hard. When I open my hand, it's a link.

A chain link. Brass. A long loop of wire bent at both ends, worn smooth by years of being dragged over stone, exactly like every one of the hundred links in the chain on my shoulder.

Except that one end has been opened, and never closed again.

And scratched very small along its side, in a slanted hand I'd know anywhere now, are two letters.

*J.M.*

I look up at the old woman. My mouth is open. Nothing comes out.

"Someone left that here," she says. "A while ago. For whoever came next." Her amber eyes are steady. "I think that's you."

Then she turns, and leans on her staff, and goes slowly back down the hidden steps into the dark.

The link is still cold in my fist when we climb out into the white morning. I don't put it away. I hold it the whole way up.
