# Chapter Ten

Mara binds my hand at the bottom of the ravine, kneeling in the shingle, and every time she touches the cut, she flinches.

Not me. Her.

I watch it happen. She wets a strip of cloth in the stream and lays it across my palm, and the cold goes into the wound like a blade, and I hiss. And at exactly the same moment, Mara's own hand jerks back, fingers curling, as if she's touched a hot stove. She stares at her own palm. There's nothing on it.

"You felt that," I say.

She doesn't answer. She picks up the cloth again and finishes binding my hand, fast and tight, her jaw clenched the whole time. When she's done she sits back on her heels, and I reach for her arm, the one the shard opened, where blood is still running in a thin dark line down toward her bandaged wrist.

"Let me."

"No."

"You're bleeding."

"It'll stop."

"Mara, I can *feel* it." I hold up my right arm. There's not a mark on it. Under the skin, in exactly the place where hers is cut, there's a hot, narrow ache, as clear as if I'd been sliced myself. "Right here. Tell me I'm wrong."

She looks at my arm. Then at her own.

Then she gets up and walks away down the stream bed without a word, and kneels at the water's edge with her back to me, and washes the cut herself.

I sit in the shingle with my bound hand in my lap and watch her go.

And here is the thing, the thing I can't stop noticing, the thing that sits in the back of my mind like a stone in a boot. I don't need to watch her go. I could close my eyes. I could turn around and face the cliff. I would still know exactly where she is. Fifteen paces downstream, a little to the left, crouched low. I can feel it the way I can feel which way is down. She's just *there*, a fixed point, the place every other part of the world now seems to arrange itself around.

I close my eyes to test it.

Fifteen paces. A little to the left. Moving now, standing, turning back.

I open my eyes. She's standing, turning back.

My hands are shaking. I put them between my knees so she won't see.

---

We climb out of the ravine by the goat path on the far side, toward Cairn Eleven. It's worse going up than coming down. My hand throbs with every handhold, and every time it throbs, I see Mara's shoulders tighten ahead of me on the path. I try to use my left hand less. It doesn't help much.

At the top, she doesn't stop at the cairn. She goes straight past it, along the rim, to the place above the scar where I saw something move. She crouches there for a long time. I wait by Cairn Eleven and set up the theodolite, because it's something to do with my hands, and I don't ask what she's looking at.

I don't have to. I can feel her go still, a long way off, the way a compass needle stops trembling.

While she's gone, I look at the marks on Cairn Eleven.

They're where they always are: third stone down, facing the line. The circle with its line through it, seven years old. Beneath it, June's slanted *J.M.* and this spring's date. So she got this far. She crossed the ravine, alive, and climbed up here and marked this cairn, which means the report is wrong about that too, or at least very carefully vague. *Rockfall in the ravine below Cairn Eleven, during a survey walk.* Not on the way here. After.

I lean closer. The wind has dried the slate dust on my face into a crust, and my eyes are sore, and at first I think I'm imagining it.

I'm not. There's something else scratched into the stone, very small, tucked into the angle where the J of June's initials curls down. Two figures. So faint and fine that anyone who wasn't nose to nose with the cairn would take them for lichen.

*13.*

I sit back on my heels and look at it for a long time. Then I look north along the ridge, toward Twelve, and beyond it, out of sight, Thirteen.

A surveyor doesn't scratch numbers on cairns for no reason. A surveyor who writes notes about the wind in her margins, who loses a page from her file, who sends her escort away so she can measure something alone, definitely doesn't.

I copy it into my field book, at the back, on the private page, beside my sketch of the toppled Nine. I don't know what it means yet.

I know it means something.

When she comes back, she's carrying something: a long iron bar, nearly as tall as she is, flattened to a wedge at one end. A pry bar. Its tip is scraped bright.

She drops it at my feet. It rings on the stone.

"It was jammed in a crack," she says. "Up on the edge. Right over the slab that came down." She wipes her hands on her coat as if the iron dirtied them. "There are boot marks. One person. Heavy. They went off north, along the rim, and down the other side."

I look at the bar. I think about a fresh pale gouge in a toppled cairn at Nine, exactly the width of my thumb. I think about it very carefully.

"River?" I ask.

"No." She shakes her head. "Human. Not hunters, either. No pitch." Her nostrils flare, once, as if the smell is still in them. "Lamp oil. And paper."

"Paper doesn't smell."

"It does to me."

Lamp oil and paper. Ledgers. A long corridor lined with them, floor to ceiling, going back a hundred years.

"Someone just tried to kill us," I say. It comes out very calm. I don't feel calm. "Someone stood up there with that thing and waited until we were underneath, and tried to kill us. Like they killed June."

Mara looks at me. Her face is grey with dust and set like the slate.

"Yes," she says.

"Why?"

"You know why. Or you're close to it." Her eyes narrow, just slightly. "You've been close to it since Nine."

I have nothing to say to that. So I bend and pick up the pry bar, which is heavy and cold and still faintly gritty with rock dust, and I lash it to the side of my pack. It's evidence. It's the first real thing I've got.

Mara watches me do it. She doesn't help. She stands three paces away, then four, then five, as if every inch closer to me costs her something.

---

That night she makes camp a long way from me.

She doesn't say she's doing it. She just builds the fire in the lee of a boulder near Cairn Eleven, sets out my bedroll beside it, and then takes her own blanket and her bow and goes and sits on the far side of a stand of larches, thirty yards off, with her back to me.

I eat my supper alone. The venison is gone; it's oatcake and the last of the cheese. I sit by the fire with my bound hand in my lap, and I can feel her out there in the dark, a fixed point thirty yards away, as clearly as if she were sitting next to me. Clearer, maybe. I can feel her heart beating. I can feel the ache in her forearm. I can feel the deep, slow, grinding burn of the silver in her wrist, which I didn't know was still hurting that much, and which I feel now under my own skin.

She's been carrying that for four days and never said a word.

I think about what she said at the bottom of the ravine. *Not you. Not now.* And the look on her face. Horror. That's the only word for it.

Whatever this is, she knows what it is. And she's horrified that it's me.

It shouldn't hurt as much as it does. I tell myself that firmly, staring into the fire. I've known her a week. A week, and most of that she's spent looking at me as if I were a particularly irritating kind of insect. She doesn't owe me anything. She saved my life today. She threw herself on top of me under a falling mountain. That's more than enough. That's more than anybody has ever done for me.

It still hurts.

I lie down by the fire and pull my blanket up and close my eyes, and I try very hard not to think about the fact that thirty yards away, Mara is awake too, and her heart is going much too fast, and I can feel every beat.

---

I don't know what wakes me.

It's late. The fire is down to embers. The sky through the larches is clear and black and full of stars, and there's a moon, a fat lopsided moon a few nights off full, standing high over the far ridge and laying silver over everything. The cairn, the boulder, the needles on the ground.

Something hurts.

Not my hand. Somewhere else. Somewhere outside me and inside me at the same time: a sharp, raw, twisting ache, high in my chest, like grief. It's not mine. I know it's not mine. I haven't got anything to grieve about, not right now, not at this exact second.

Thirty yards away, behind the larches, it gets worse.

I'm on my feet before I've decided to be. I don't need a lamp. I don't need the moon. I just walk toward the ache, the way you'd walk toward a fire in the dark because you can feel the heat on your face. Through the larches. Over the soft needles. Out the other side, onto a bare shoulder of rock above the ravine, where the moonlight is so bright it throws shadows.

Mara is there.

She's sitting on the rock with her knees drawn up and her arms wrapped around them, and her face pressed into her knees. Her braid has come half undone. Her shoulders are shaking.

She's crying. Silently, completely, the way you only cry when you think nobody can hear.

"Mara," I say.

She goes rigid. Her head comes up.

"Go back."

"No."

"*Cass*. Go back to the fire."

"I can't." I take another step, out of the shadow of the trees, into the full silver light. "I can feel it. Whatever you're feeling. It woke me up. I can feel exactly where you are, all the time, and I can feel your arm and your wrist and your—" My voice catches. "And I can feel *this*. Whatever this is. It hurts. It hurts so much, Mara, and I don't even know what it's about."

She turns her face toward me.

The moon is full on it. On her wet cheeks, her set mouth, her eyes.

Her eyes.

They're amber, the way they always are. Except they're not. Around the edge of each iris, where the amber meets the white, there's a ring. A thin, perfect, shining circle of gold, bright as a new coin, catching the moonlight and throwing it back. It wasn't there this morning. It wasn't there an hour ago by the fire. I'd swear to it. I've spent a week looking at her eyes, whether or not I wanted to admit it, and I would have seen that.

"Your eyes," I whisper.

She doesn't answer. She's staring at me. At *my* face. And her own face is doing something I've never seen it do: crumpling, coming apart, all at once.

"Yours too," she says.

I put my hand up to my face, as if I could feel it. As if a ring of gold were something you could touch.

"What is it?" My voice is very small. "Mara. What's happening to us?"

She closes her eyes. The gold disappears. When she opens them again it's still there, shining, in the light of a moon that's four nights off full.

"It's called the tether," she says. "And it's going to get one of us killed."
