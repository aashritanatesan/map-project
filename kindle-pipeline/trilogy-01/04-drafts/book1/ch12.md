# Chapter Twelve

Strictly business lasts until roughly nine o'clock the next morning.

It starts well. I'll give us that. We wake on opposite sides of the larches, and we eat on opposite sides of the cold fire, and we don't look at each other while we pack. When I take the theodolite out at Cairn Eleven to sight Twelve, Mara goes and stands at the far end of the chain, sixty-six feet away, and holds the brass handle at exactly the right height without being asked, and doesn't say a word.

Sixty-six feet. I find myself thinking about it as I take the bearing. It's the length of the chain. It's the closest we're allowed to be, and the furthest we can get, and still do the job.

"Chaining," I call.

"Fine," she calls back.

We chain. One. Two. Three. I pin and call the lengths, and she walks on, and the chain clinks over the needles between us. It's all perfectly professional. It's all perfectly, absolutely fine.

At length seven, I stub my toe on a root.

It's not a bad stub. I've stubbed my toes on every root in the Reach by now, or at least it feels that way. I hop once and say a word my mother would wash my mouth out for.

Sixty-six feet away, Mara says the same word, at the same moment, and hops.

We both freeze.

Very slowly, she turns around. She looks at me. I look at her. She's holding one foot off the ground with an expression of profound betrayal.

"Sorry," I say.

"Watch," she says through her teeth, "where you are *walking*."

"I *was*. The root moved."

"Roots don't move."

"This one did. It was a very aggressive root."

She puts her foot down, glares at me across the length of the chain, and turns back around. Her shoulders are stiff with offence. I can feel the throb in her toe, perfectly in time with mine, and something about it is so ridiculous that I have to bite the inside of my cheek very hard to stop myself laughing.

She feels that too. I know she does, because her shoulders get even stiffer.

---

By mid-morning it's worse.

It's not the pain. The pain I could manage. A stubbed toe here, a scraped knuckle there, the deep slow burn of the silver in her wrist that's always there now, under everything, like a low note under a song. Those are just facts. You can stand on facts.

It's the knowing where she is.

I can't stop. Every time she's in front of me, I know it. Every time she goes ahead to scout the next stretch of line, and I lose sight of her among the larches, the compass needle inside me swings round after her and points and won't come back. I take a bearing on Cairn Twelve and find myself looking, not at the red speck in the eyepiece, but at a stand of trees thirty degrees to the left where I know, *know*, that she's crouched by a stream filling her water bottle.

I write down the wrong angle. I have to cross it out.

I never write down the wrong angle.

"You're distracted," Mara says, when she comes back.

"I'm not."

"You've been staring at the same tree for a minute."

"It's a very interesting tree."

"You're staring at it," she says, "because I was behind it."

There's no answer to that. I don't try to make one. She looks at me with an expression I can't quite read, somewhere between exasperation and something much softer, and then she makes a small frustrated sound and turns away.

"It's not just you," she says, to the next cairn. "I keep looking back. At the theodolite. At *you*. Every time you breathe in sharp I think you've fallen." She rubs her burned wrist with the heel of her other hand. "When I drew my bow this morning, at a hare, you winced. From twenty yards. I felt you wince. I missed the hare."

"I'm sorry."

"Stop saying sorry. You didn't do it."

"Neither did you."

She turns her head and looks at me then. The daylight is clear and cold and ordinary, and there's no gold in her eyes, only amber. You'd never know. You'd never in a hundred years know.

"No," she says quietly. "Neither of us did it."

Something in the way she says it makes my chest hurt, and this time it's mine.

---

The climb to Twelve goes over a long rib of bare rock, and halfway up it, the rib narrows to a ledge with a drop on one side that I decide, firmly, not to have an opinion about.

I make it most of the way. Then a flake of slate comes loose under my boot, and my weight goes sideways, toward the drop, and my stomach goes with it.

Mara's hand closes on my wrist.

She's sixty-six feet ahead. She was sixty-six feet ahead. I don't know how she's here. I don't think she knows either; she's staring at her own hand around my wrist as if it belongs to somebody else. She must have felt it, the lurch, the moment my foot went, and moved before she'd decided to.

She hauls me back onto the ledge. I sit down hard against the rock. My heart is going like a drum, and so is hers, I can feel both of them, two drums out of step and then, slowly, settling into the same beat.

She doesn't let go of my wrist.

"Strictly business," I say, a little breathlessly.

"Shut up," says Mara.

Her thumb is on the inside of my wrist, right over the pulse. I don't think she knows it's there. She's looking at the drop. Then at me. Then at her thumb, and I watch her realise, and I feel it go through her like a flinch and a flame at the same time.

She lets go. She stands up. She walks back up the ledge and picks up the brass handle where she dropped it, and stands at the far end of the chain again, sixty-six feet away, exactly where she's supposed to be.

I sit against the rock and rub my wrist, where her thumb was.

"It's not working," I say, quietly, mostly to myself. "Whatever we're doing. It isn't working at all."

Sixty-six feet away, without turning around, Mara says, "I know." Of course she heard. Wolf ears. I should have known that, too.

---

We reach Twelve at noon, and Thirteen in the middle of the afternoon, after a long climb up a shoulder of the ridge where the larches thin out and the wind comes hard and cold off the north.

Thirteen is a big cairn: old, broad-based, much bigger than the others, built on a flat ledge of bare rock with a view so vast it makes my stomach drop. The whole Reach is spread out below. The gold forest pours down into River's green valleys. The grey gash of the ravine cuts back south behind us. And far off, at the bottom of everything, the smoky smudge of Stonebridge sits in its gorge like something at the bottom of a well.

I don't look at the view. I look at the cairn.

*13.*

"What are you doing?" Mara asks.

I'm crouched at the base of it, running my good hand over the stones. "June scratched a number on Eleven," I say. "Tiny. Next to her initials. Thirteen."

Mara goes very still.

"I didn't tell you," I say. "Yesterday. I'm sorry. I was going to. Then the ravine happened, and—" I don't finish. "But a surveyor doesn't scratch a number on a cairn for no reason. She meant someone to come here. She meant *the next surveyor* to come here."

Mara doesn't answer. But she crouches down beside me, at the other end of the cairn, and starts running her hands over the stones too.

We go round it twice. The marks on the third stone are where they should be, the old circle and June's initials. Nothing else. No numbers. No arrows. No more scratches. Just grey slate, cold and rough and furred with lichen in the cracks.

I sit back on my heels, frustrated. "Maybe I'm wrong. Maybe it meant something else."

"No," Mara says.

She's at the north side of the cairn, the side facing away from the line, the side nobody would ever look at. She has her face close to the stones, and her nostrils are flared.

"Here," she says. "It smells different."

"Stones don't smell different."

She gives me a look.

"Right," I say. "Sorry. Paper. Go on."

"Not paper. Wax. And—" She inhales again, slowly. Something changes in her face. "Her. It smells like her. June."

I get up and go round the cairn to her, and kneel at her shoulder, and I'm close enough now that the tether hums under my skin like a plucked string. I ignore it. I look at where she's looking.

At the very bottom of the cairn, where the lowest course of slabs sits on the bare rock of the ledge, there's a stone that doesn't quite match. It's the same grey, the same lichen. But the lichen on it has been disturbed: scraped away in a thin line along one edge, then pressed back down, not quite carefully enough.

I get my fingers into the gap. My bad hand screams. I use it anyway.

The stone shifts. It's heavy, but not as heavy as it looks, and after a moment of grunting and swearing and Mara swearing in sympathy, it slides out of the cairn and thumps down onto the ledge.

There's a hollow behind it. A small dry space in the heart of the stack, just big enough for two hands.

And in it, wrapped in oilcloth and sealed along every seam with dark red wax, is a package about the size of a book.

---

I don't open it straight away.

I sit on the ledge with it in my lap, and my hands are shaking, and I'm not counting, for once. I'm not doing anything. Just holding it. It's heavier than I expected. The wax seal on the top is stamped with a little slanted *J.M.*, the same as on the cairns.

Mara is sitting beside me. Not sixty-six feet away. Right beside me, close enough that our shoulders almost touch. She doesn't seem to have noticed. I'm not going to point it out.

"Open it," she says.

I break the seal with my thumbnail. I unfold the oilcloth, layer by layer.

Inside is a field book. Not an Office one, with the stamped cover and the numbered pages. A small, battered, leather-bound notebook, the kind you'd buy in any stationer's in the lowlands. Its cover is soft with use. The edges of the pages are grey from thumbs. When I open it, the first page is covered in June's small, slanted, precise hand: bearings, distances, sketches of cairns, all in neat columns, exactly the way I'd do it. Exactly the way I *am* doing it, at the back of my own field book, under my spare socks.

Tucked inside the front cover there's a folded sheet of paper. Office paper: I'd know that grain anywhere. Surveyors on long lines send their field pages down to the Office by runner every few cairns, and keep a duplicate for themselves. This is June's duplicate. It's in her hand, and in the top corner, where the clerks number the pages, she's written the number herself.

*41. Duplicate.*

Mara draws in a sharp breath beside me.

And folded inside that, on a scrap torn from the back of the leather notebook, there's a note. Written fast, the slant leaning harder than usual, the ink blotted in places as if her hand was not quite steady.

I read the first line, and the wind on the ledge goes cold right through me.

*To the next surveyor. If you've found this, they've already tried to kill you.*
