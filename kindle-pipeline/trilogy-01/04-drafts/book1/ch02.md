# Chapter Two

The innkeeper has opinions about the weather, and he shares them with me over porridge whether I want them or not.

"First snow on the high ridge," he says, setting down a pot of tea that has been stewing since roughly the founding of the city, "comes when it likes. Could be three weeks. Could be tomorrow."

"The Office said six weeks."

"The Office," he says, with enormous dignity, "has never once been up there."

I look out the window. The Highwood ridge is cut sharp against a sky so clear and pale it looks scrubbed. The gold of the larches has spread overnight. Yesterday it was a smudge along the crest. This morning it runs halfway down the slope, like someone has tipped a pot of honey over the mountain and let it ooze.

"It's beautiful," I say.

"So's a bear," says the innkeeper, and takes my bowl away before I've finished.

---

The road out of Stonebridge climbs the way everything here climbs: steeply, and without apology. I leave the last houses behind within the hour and follow a cart track up the side of the gorge, switchback after switchback, the river shrinking below me to a white thread. I have my instrument case strapped on my back, the satchel across my chest, and the chain looped over my shoulder, and by the fifth switchback I've started to have feelings about all three of them.

I don't look down. That's the trick. I look at my boots, at the track, at the rock wall on my left. I count my steps in sets of a hundred, the same as links on a chain, and every hundred I allow myself one glance at the view. One glance only. The view is enormous and blue and sickening, and I love it, and I'd love it much more from a chair.

Halfway up, the track passes the quarries.

I smell them before I see them, a raw mineral bite in the back of my throat, sharper than the wet-slate smell of the city. Then the hillside opens on my right into a vast grey wound. Terraces of cut stone step down into the earth. Men swarm the ledges with picks and wedges. A crane creaks somewhere. Slabs of slate the size of doors sit stacked by the track, waiting for carts, their faces split so clean they shine.

There's a sign at the quarry gate: *STONEBRIDGE MINING GUILD. TRESPASSERS PROSECUTED.*

Beyond the quarry, past a line of wind-bent trees, the land rises again into scrub and boulders. Someone has been busy there too. A row of wooden stakes marches up the slope, each one topped with a strip of yellow cloth. I count fourteen before the slope hides the rest. Two men with a measuring rope are setting another.

I stop. I shouldn't. I do anyway.

"Excuse me," I call. "What's that ground?"

One of the men straightens up. He looks at my chain, and his face does something careful. "Guild claim, miss."

"Whose land was it?"

"Nobody's." He shrugs. "Lapsed in spring. Open ground. Guild bought the mineral rights off the Office last month."

The east spur. The four hundred acres. I look up the slope at the yellow flags snapping in the wind and think of the arm on the cart, and the paper tag tied to one finger.

"Thank you," I say, because I'm polite. And then, because I'm a surveyor, I take out my notebook and write down the number of stakes, the bearing of the line they're setting, and the date.

The man watches me do it. He doesn't say anything. He doesn't have to.

---

The cart track gives up an hour past the quarries and becomes a path, and the path becomes a sort of determined suggestion, and the larches begin.

I've never been in a larch forest. Nobody warned me it would be like walking into a lantern. The trees are tall and straight and widely spaced, and every one of them is gold, needle to needle, so the light that comes through is gold too. It lies on the ground in warm drifts. The needles that have already fallen are soft underfoot, so soft that my boots stop making any sound at all, and for the first time since I left the lowlands it is properly quiet. No river. No sluices. No men shouting in quarries. Just wind moving high up in the gold, and the faint sticky smell of resin, and my own breathing.

I like it immediately. I distrust it almost as fast.

The Highwood gate is not a gate. It's two larches the width of a cart apart, a cairn daubed so red it looks freshly bled on, and a woman leaning against one of the trees like she's been carved there to frighten surveyors.

It's working.

"Cass Reed," I say, and stick out my hand. "Survey Office. I'm your problem for the next six weeks."

She looks at the hand. She looks at the chain coiled over my shoulder, the silver links winking every tenth loop. Then she looks back at my face, and I understand for the first time why the lowland manuals call Highwood eyes *amber*. It isn't a colour. It's a warning.

"Mara," she says. She doesn't take the hand.

I put it in my pocket, where it can be embarrassed in private. "Great. Lovely. So, the route. I've got June's field notes, the Office copy of the line, and a deadline of first snow, which the man at the inn says is either three weeks away or tomorrow, depending on how much you've paid him."

"Did Voss send you?"

"The Office sent me."

"That's a yes." She pushes off the tree. She's taller than me, which isn't hard, but she's also taller than the trees seem to want her to be, as if the forest leans back to make room. "Last surveyor walked this line in spring. She came back in a sack."

"She didn't come back at all," I say. "I read the report."

Something moves in her face. Not softness, exactly. More like a door that's been shut so long it's surprised to find it has hinges.

She has a bow across her back and a quiver at her hip, the fletching dyed the same red as the cairn. Her hair is black and braided in one long rope that hangs nearly to her waist. Her coat is worn leather, patched at the elbows, buttoned to the throat even though the afternoon is mild. Her hands are long and brown and callused across the first two fingers of the right, where a bowstring would bite. She's maybe thirty. She looks like she's been angry for about twenty-nine of those years and has got very efficient at it.

I remember the satchel. "I'm supposed to give you this." I fish out the sealed letter with the Registrar's mark and hold it out.

She takes it this time. Not my hand: the letter, held between two fingers like something that might be wet. She breaks the seal with her thumbnail and reads. It's short. I watch her eyes go down the page once, then back to the top, then down again.

Then she laughs. It's not a nice laugh. It's barely a laugh at all; it's the sound a person makes when something confirms exactly what they expected.

"What?" I ask.

She turns the letter so I can see. The handwriting is a clerk's, but the signature at the bottom is Voss's: small, neat, perfectly upright.

*The Office trusts the Highwood pack to extend every courtesy to its surveyor. The Office reminds the pack that the surveyor is under its protection for the length of the walk. Should any harm come to the surveyor before the line is chained and filed, the Office will be obliged to regard the line as unwalked.*

I read it twice. "Unwalked."

"Unwalked," Mara says. "Lapsed. Open ground." She folds the letter in half, then in half again, very precisely. "If you fall off a mountain, we lose the forest."

There's a long, airy silence. Somewhere high above us a bird says something rude.

"Well," I say. "Then I'd strongly recommend not letting me fall off a mountain."

She looks at me for a long moment. I can't tell what she's looking for. Whatever it is, I don't think she finds it.

"How many surveys have you walked?" she asks.

"Including this one?"

"Not including this one."

"Practice lines," I say. "Lots. In the lowlands. They're very flat." I pause. "Extremely flat. You could survey them lying down."

"So none."

"So none that count," I admit. "But I did six years of training, I graduated first in triangulation, and my chain errors are the lowest in my year. I can measure a mile to within a hand's width. I'm good, Mara. I'm just new."

"June was good," she says.

It comes out flat. Not cruel. Just a fact, set down between us like a stone. And for a second there's that door again, behind her eyes, opening a crack on something dark and closing just as fast.

I want to ask. I don't. I've been in the Reach for one day and I already know there are questions you don't get to ask people with bows.

She tucks the folded letter inside her coat. When she does it, her collar shifts and I see the edge of a scar along her collarbone: three pale, parallel lines, old and silvered, like something with claws once tried very hard to open her up.

She sees me see it. She buttons the collar back up.

"Walk behind me," she says. "Don't touch the cairns until I say. Don't swing that thing near me."

"It's a measuring chain. It doesn't *swing*. It's sixty-six feet of extremely calm brass."

"It's silver."

"It's *ten per cent* silver."

She's already walking. The larch needles swallow her footsteps whole, so the only sound on the path is me: my boots, my breath, and the chain clinking softly against my hip like it's laughing at me.

I follow her between the two larches, past the red cairn, and onto Highwood ground.

It doesn't feel different. I don't know what I expected. Some shiver in the air, some line I could feel the way I can feel a slope under my feet. There's nothing. Just more gold, more quiet, the same soft needles. But I count my steps anyway, out of habit, and I'm at forty when I notice the stream.

It's tiny: a bright thread of water slipping over moss between two stones. I'm thirsty and hot from the climb, so I crouch beside it and cup my hands and drink.

It tastes of iron. Of iron and something else, warm and metallic, like when you bite your own tongue.

I spit it out before I can stop myself. Mara has stopped on the path ahead. She's watching me with an expression I can only describe as deeply, profoundly unsurprised.

"That's normal," she says. "It's the stone."

"It tastes like *blood*."

"Yes." She turns away. "Welcome to Highwood."

---

We walk until the light starts to slant. Mara doesn't speak. I don't either, at first, because I'm busy breathing, and then because I'm busy being offended about not being spoken to, and then because the path has started to climb again and I need all my attention for my feet.

It's nearly an hour before I try again.

"Where are we stopping tonight?"

"Cairn One."

"And is Cairn One near a bed, or—"

"It's near a rock."

"A comfortable rock?"

She doesn't dignify that. A squirrel the colour of rust streaks across the path, stops dead, takes one look at Mara, and flees up a trunk so fast it nearly falls off the far side. I know exactly how it feels.

"Can I ask you something?"

"You will anyway."

That's fair. "Do you always escort the surveyors? Is it a job, like, an official Highwood post? Does it come with a hat?"

The path narrows between two boulders furred with moss. She goes through first, sideways, without touching either one. I go through after her and touch both, and get moss on my coat, and pretend I meant to.

"No," she says.

"No to the hat, or—"

"No, I don't always escort them." A pause, three steps long. I count them. "My mother usually sends someone else."

"So why you, this time?"

She doesn't look back. Her braid swings once against her spine, a black pendulum. "Nobody else would."

It lands somewhere under my ribs and stays there. Because I know that answer. I gave it, in my own head, the day Agnes Bell called me into her office and told me the Highwood post was mine if I wanted it. *Why me?* And the honest answer, the one she was too kind or too tired to say out loud: because nobody senior would take a dead woman's route.

"Well," I say, to Mara's back. "That makes two of us."

She doesn't answer. But her step changes, just slightly: shorter, a fraction slower. As if she's no longer trying quite so hard to leave me behind.

Up ahead, the trail tilts toward a drop I refuse to look at. The air already tastes of iron.

I tell the theodolite, quietly, that we're going to be fine.

It doesn't believe me either.
