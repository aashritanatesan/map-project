# Chapter Seven

The storm catches us halfway between Nine and Ten, and it doesn't so much rain as fall on us all at once.

One moment the sky is bruised yellow. The next it's black, and the larches are roaring, and water is coming down so hard it bounces knee-high off the path. I've been rained on before. I'm from the lowlands; rain is practically our national pastime. But lowland rain is soft and grey and apologetic. This rain has a grudge. It comes in sideways on a wind that tears at my coat and my pack and the chain on my shoulder, and within a minute I'm soaked to the skin and so cold I can't feel my face.

"This way," Mara shouts, and grabs my sleeve.

It's the first time she's touched me on purpose. I don't have time to think about it. She drags me off the line, downhill, through a stand of young larches that whip at my face, and across a stream that has turned from a trickle into something with opinions. I lose my footing on a wet stone. She hauls me up by the collar like a kitten. Thunder breaks directly overhead with a crack so loud I feel it in my teeth.

And then there's a wall in front of us. A real one: dry stone, waist-high, then a low roof of turf, then a door.

Mara shoulders the door open and shoves me through it.

---

It's a bothy. One room, barely big enough to lie down in, with a beaten earth floor and a stone hearth at one end and a wide wooden shelf built along the back wall for sleeping on. The air smells of old smoke and sheep. It is, without question, the most beautiful room I've ever stood in.

"Goat herders," Mara says, dumping her bow and quiver by the door. Water streams off her braid onto the floor. "Highwood keeps goats on the high pastures in summer. They leave peat."

There is peat. There's a neat stack of it by the hearth, and a tin box on a shelf with a flint and some dry kindling inside, and another tin, smaller, that turns out to hold tea leaves. I could weep. I nearly do.

Mara gets the fire going while I stand in the middle of the floor dripping and trying to make my fingers work well enough to unbuckle my pack. They won't. They're white and stiff and shaking, and the buckle keeps sliding out of them.

"Here." She's beside me. She doesn't ask. She just reaches out, flicks the buckle open with one hand, and lifts the pack off my shoulders. Then the chain, carefully, by its leather strap, not touching any of the links. Then she looks at me, and frowns.

"Take your coat off."

"I'm fine."

"You're blue. Take your coat off."

I take my coat off. Underneath it my shirt is soaked through and clinging, and I am suddenly, extremely aware of how small the room is and how there's nowhere in it to stand that isn't within arm's reach of her. She seems to become aware of it at the same moment. She turns away, abruptly, and goes and rummages in a wooden chest under the sleeping shelf, and comes back with two coarse grey blankets that smell of goat.

She holds one out without looking at me.

"Change," she says. "I won't look."

"You're soaked too."

"I'll change after."

"That's ridiculous. You'll freeze. We'll both turn around."

A pause.

"Fine," she says.

So we stand back to back in the firelight in the tiny stone room, and peel off our wet clothes, and wrap ourselves in blankets that smell of goat, and I spend the entire time staring very hard at the wall and listening to the rustle of cloth behind me and thinking about absolutely nothing at all. Especially not about how close she is. Especially not about the sound of her unbraiding her wet hair.

---

We hang our clothes on pegs by the fire. They steam. Outside, the storm pounds on the turf roof like it wants to be let in.

Mara makes tea in a blackened pot. We drink it out of the same tin cup, passing it back and forth, because there's only one. It's stewed and bitter and gritty with leaves, and it's the best thing I've ever drunk. I tell her so.

"You said that about the venison."

"I meant it about the venison."

"You say that about everything."

"I *mean* it about everything. Everything up here is either trying to kill me or the best thing I've ever had. Sometimes both." I wrap my hands around the cup. "Mostly both."

She sits on the edge of the sleeping shelf with her blanket around her shoulders and her knees drawn up. Her hair is loose now, black and heavy and still damp, falling over her shoulders almost to her waist. I've never seen it unbraided. It makes her look younger. It makes her look like someone who might, under other circumstances, laugh.

She isn't laughing. She's looking at the fire.

"My mother is dying," she says.

I don't say anything. I've learned that much. I hold the cup and wait.

"She's had the cough two years. It's worse every winter. The healers say this one, or the next." She says it plainly, the way I'd read off a bearing. "When she dies, I'm alpha. Highwood is mine. All of it. Every cairn, every acre, every wolf." She picks at a loose thread on the blanket. "And every mile we've lost. That's mine too. Two miles in my lifetime, four in hers. I told you." She pulls the thread. It doesn't break. "She's going to die and leave me a pack that's smaller every time someone like you walks through it."

"Someone like me."

"A surveyor." She looks at me. "No offence."

"Some taken." I pass her the cup. "For what it's worth, I'm trying very hard not to be someone like me."

That earns me a look I can't read at all.

"Why are you here?" she asks. "Really. Not *the Office sent me*. You. Why did you want this?"

And I could give her the Office answer. I nearly do. It's sitting right there on my tongue, smooth and neat: *because it's an honour, because it's important work, because the Reach needs surveyors.* All true. All useless.

"I grew up on a farm," I say instead. "In the lowlands. Very flat, very small. Our field went down to a stream, and there was a fence at the bottom of it, and on the other side of the fence was our neighbour's land. He wanted the stream. He wanted it for years. And one spring, when I was nine, a surveyor came out from town and walked the boundary again, and when he'd finished the fence was on our side of the stream instead of his."

Mara's hands have stopped moving on the blanket.

"My father went to the magistrate. The magistrate looked at the survey and said the survey was the law. My father said the survey was wrong, and the magistrate said, *Do you have a surveyor who says otherwise?* And we didn't. Nobody we knew could afford one." I look at the fire. "We lost the stream. Then we lost the field, because without the water nothing would grow. Then we lost the farm. My father died two years after that. My mother says it was his heart. I think it was the fence."

The storm hammers the roof. The peat fire breathes and settles.

"I found out later the neighbour paid the surveyor," I say. "Not much. Enough. And I decided, when I was eleven, sitting in a rented room in town, that I was going to be the one with the chain. That I was going to be the one person in the room who could say *no, that's wrong, I measured it, here's the number.*" I laugh a little. It doesn't come out right. "So. That's why. I wanted to be the surveyor nobody could pay."

For a long moment Mara doesn't say anything at all.

Then she says, very quietly: "Did you find something at Nine?"

And I want to tell her. I want it so badly my chest aches. I can feel the words lining up behind my teeth: *the cairn was toppled, someone moved it, someone moved your whole border a quarter mile into your forest, and I'm almost sure.* Almost.

"I think so," I say. "I'm not sure yet. I don't want to tell you something that's wrong. Not about this. Not to you."

I wait for her to push. She doesn't. She just looks at me, steady, in the firelight, her eyes gone dark in the low light, just a glint of amber left in them.

"Then tell me when you're sure," she says. "Not before."

It's such an enormous thing to give somebody, that kind of patience, that for a second I can't speak.

---

Much later, when the fire is low and we're lying back to back on the sleeping shelf because it's the only place to lie, and the only way two people fit on it is close, she tells me about June.

She tells it to the wall. I listen to it with my eyes open in the dark.

"I was her escort. In spring. My mother sent me." Her back is warm against mine, through two layers of goat blanket. "She was easy to walk with. She talked too much. Like you." A breath. "She liked River. She liked their beds. I didn't like her liking them, and I told her so, and she laughed at me. She called me sullen. She wrote it down in her book. She showed me."

I close my eyes. *Escort sullen.*

"We got to Eight. She was strange there. Quiet. She kept looking at the marks on the stones. And then she told me to go back to the hold. She said she had something to measure on her own, and I was distracting her. I said no. She said it was her survey and her line and her decision, and if I didn't like it I could go and complain to my mother." A long silence. "So I went."

"Mara—"

"I was angry. I left her at Eight, on her own, with the light going. I walked half the night back to the hold. And in the morning they found her chain at the bottom of the ravine below Eleven." Her voice doesn't break. It just goes very, very quiet. "She sent me away, and I went. And then she was gone."

I don't think. I reach back in the dark, and find her hand where it lies on the blanket between us, and hold it.

She goes rigid. For a second I'm sure she'll pull away.

She doesn't. Her fingers stay where they are, cool and callused, under mine. After a long time they turn, just slightly, and hold back.

I don't count anything. I notice that, somewhere in the dark: that for once there's nothing in my head that needs to be lined up and numbered and made to stand still. There's just the rain, and the slow breath of the fire, and the weight of her hand, which is the only fixed point I need.

We lie like that, not speaking, while the storm wears itself out on the roof.

---

I wake in the grey before dawn to an empty shelf and a cold draught.

Mara is at the door. She's dressed again, her clothes still damp, her hair roughly braided. The door is open a crack, and she's standing with her face to it, breathing slowly, in, out.

"What is it?"

She doesn't look round.

"Smoke," she says. "On the wind. Down by the open ground past Ten."

"Goat herders?"

"Goat herders went down a month ago." She reaches for her bow. "And they don't burn their fires with pitch."

I don't know what that means. Her voice tells me enough.

"Hunters," Mara says.
