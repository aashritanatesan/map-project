# Chapter Sixteen

I tell myself I'm only going to stand in the doorway.

That's all. Just for a minute. Just long enough to see. The bar is right there by my foot, the shutters are latched, and the second anything comes near I'll step back inside and drop the beam into its brackets and that'll be that. I'm not breaking a promise. I'm bending one. Very slightly. In a precise and measured way.

I last about a minute in the doorway.

Then I'm on the step. Then I'm on the path. Then I'm standing at the edge of the guesthouse's small patch of trampled ground, with the door wide open behind me and the lamp spilling yellow out onto the needles, and the moon pouring white over everything else.

It's the brightest night I've ever seen. Brighter than some lowland days. The great larches stand up out of the bowl like pillars in a temple, silver on one side and black on the other, every needle on every branch picked out sharp. The hall is a long dark shape, its turf roof furred with light. The smaller buildings are shut and silent. Not a lamp anywhere. Not a single human sound.

But the bowl is full of movement.

They're everywhere. Shapes, flowing between the trees. Grey, mostly, and silver-grey, and dun, and one or two nearly white, moving fast and low and silent through the shadows and then bursting out into the moonlight in long, loping strides. I see two of them meet in a clearing and rise up on their hind legs, chest to chest, snapping and tumbling, and I think it's a fight, and then I realise it's play. I see a whole knot of smaller shapes, half-grown, yipping and tearing round the trunk of one enormous larch in circles, and I think of Bet and her gap tooth and her fierce solemn face, and I bite my lip to keep from laughing out loud.

And over it all, the singing. It never quite stops. One voice goes up, then another, from the far side of the bowl, from up on the ridge, from somewhere off in the dark of the forest. It weaves in and out, rising and falling. Here a rise, here a fall. A long straight run.

It's a border. They're singing their border.

I stand at the edge of the trampled ground with my arms wrapped around myself, and I cry, a little, and I don't care.

---

And then I feel her.

She's been out there all along. Of course she has. Off to my left, a long way, up on the slope above the bowl, moving fast. I've been feeling her the whole time, the way I always do, a fixed point. But now something has changed. She's turned. She's coming back.

And through the tether, suddenly, so strongly it nearly knocks me off my feet, I feel *her*.

Not her pain. Not her heartbeat. *Her.* What it's like, right now, to be her.

It's speed. That's the first thing. Speed like I've never known, like falling and flying at once, the ground pouring away under four feet that know exactly where every stone and root is without being told. It's strength, a deep easy power in shoulders and haunches, the kind that could run all night and never tire. It's the smell of the forest, a thousand smells, ten thousand, every one of them sharp and clear and full of meaning: deer here an hour ago, a fox, water, resin, the warm living smell of the pack all round.

And it's joy. Huge, uncomplicated, overwhelming joy. The joy of being exactly what you are, in exactly the place where you're allowed to be it.

*This,* I think. *This is what the chain takes away.*

I'm still standing there, gasping, my hands pressed to my chest, when the first wolf finds me.

---

It isn't Mara.

It comes out of the shadow of the guesthouse wall, close, very close, ten feet away. It's big. Bigger than the ones I saw on the spur. Its coat is dark grey, nearly black along the spine, and its ears are flat, and its lips are drawn back from teeth as long as my fingers.

It's looking right at me. At my face.

I know those eyes. Amber, with a dark rim. I saw them this afternoon on the path to the hall, bright and flushed and full of teeth.

*Don't open it for anything. Not for anything you hear.*

"Lena," I whisper.

The wolf takes a step forward. It's growling. It's a low sound, so low I feel it more than hear it, in my ribs and the soles of my feet. Its hackles are up along its spine in a stiff dark ridge.

I don't move. I don't look away. *Don't look away,* Mara said. *They'll think you're prey.*

I'm fairly sure it already thinks that.

Another step. The growl deepens. Its eyes are fixed on my face, and I don't know if it understands, if there's anything of Lena in there at all right now, or if it's just moon and instinct and the smell of a surveyor standing where no surveyor should be.

It gathers itself to spring.

Something black hits it from the side.

---

They go down together in a storm of snarling and needles. It's over in seconds. The dark grey wolf rolls, scrambles, comes up, and the black one is already there, standing over it, teeth bared, a sound coming out of her I've never heard from any living thing. It isn't a growl. It's lower. It's older. It's the sound the mountain made in the ravine just before it came down.

The grey wolf drops. Flat. Belly to the needles, ears back, tail tucked under.

The black wolf stands over it for a long moment. Then she steps back.

The grey wolf scrambles up and bolts. Off into the trees, low and fast, and gone.

And the black wolf turns around and looks at me.

She's huge. I knew she would be. Taller at the shoulder than I am at the waist, long-legged, deep-chested, her coat black as a starless sky, gleaming silver where the moonlight touches it. There's a pale old scar across her chest, three parallel lines, where the fur grows in white. Her right foreleg is held a little off the ground, just above the paw. Where a bandage would be, if wolves wore bandages.

Her eyes are amber. And around the edge of each iris, shining in the full white light of the moon like two rings of fire, is gold.

"Mara," I say.

She walks to me. She doesn't hurry. She comes across the trampled ground slowly, one step at a time, head low, ears forward, and stops in front of me, so close I can feel the heat coming off her, smell resin and woodsmoke and something wild and warm that I would know anywhere now, in the dark, with my eyes shut.

I put my hand out. My left hand. The cut one, still bandaged.

She pushes her head into it.

Her fur is coarse on top and impossibly soft underneath. Her skull is broad and hard under my palm. She's breathing in long slow breaths, and I can feel each one through my hand and through the tether at the same time, and I'm crying again, properly this time, and she makes a small sound in her chest, low and rough, and leans her whole weight against my legs until I nearly fall over.

I put my other arm around her neck. I bury my face in her fur.

"You saved me again," I say into it. "That's twice. I'm keeping count."

She huffs. I could swear it's a laugh.

---

I don't know how long we stand like that. Long enough for my arms to go numb. Long enough for the moon to climb a little higher over the bowl. Long enough that, when I finally lift my head, we're not alone.

There's a wolf at the edge of the trees.

It's small. That's what I notice first. Small and old, its coat pure white, thick and ragged, the white of the braid that hangs over Ruth's shoulder in the hall. It's standing very still in the shadow of the nearest larch, one forepaw raised, the way you'd stand when you're too tired to walk any further but too proud to sit.

It's looking at us.

At Mara, pressed against my legs. At my arm around her neck. And then, slowly, deliberately, it lifts its eyes to my face.

I know what it sees. I can't see it myself, but I know. I've seen it in Mara's eyes twice now, and I can feel it in mine, a warmth like candlelight behind them. The full moon is pouring down on my upturned face. My eyes are shining gold.

Like a lamp, Mara said. Anyone who looks at you will know.

The white wolf looks for a long time.

Then it looks at Mara. Mara has gone completely still against me. I can feel her heart, racing. I can feel her fear, sudden and enormous.

The white wolf doesn't move. It doesn't growl. It doesn't make any sound at all.

It simply turns, and walks away into the trees, slow and stiff and silent, and doesn't look back.

Mara makes a small, broken noise against my legs.

I put my hand on her head and keep it there.

"She knows," I whisper.

Mara doesn't answer. She doesn't need to. What comes back through the tether isn't a word, but it's as good as one: a great, cold, sinking drop of dread, like a stone going down into deep water.

---

I go back inside eventually. Mara walks me to the door. She stands on the step while I go in, and she doesn't leave until I've dropped the larch bar into its brackets with a heavy wooden thud. I sit on the bed with my back against the wall and listen to her pad away across the needles, and feel her go back out into the moonlight, back to the running and the singing and the joy.

I don't sleep. Not for a long time.

I lie on the narrow bed with the lamp turned low and my bandaged hand resting on my chest, and I think about what I felt out there. The speed. The strength. Ten thousand smells. The joy of being exactly what you are, in exactly the place where you're allowed to be it.

I've spent my whole life thinking of borders as lines. Ink on paper, ochre on stone, grooves in a slate floor. Things you could measure. Things you could get right or wrong, the way you get a sum right or wrong. I became a surveyor because I wanted to be the one who got it right.

I never once thought about what it's like to be on the other side of the line. To stand at the edge of a cairn on open ground and feel all of that, the speed and the strength and the joy, waiting for you three steps away. To know that a man in a map room with clean hands has moved it a quarter mile further off, and called it a slip, and written your mother a very polite letter.

Four miles. A quarter mile deep. I'd thought of it as acres.

It's this. It's this, every full moon, for every wolf in the hold, for as long as the line stays wrong.

I turn on my side and press my face into the pillow, which smells of larch and old wool, and I make myself a promise, and I don't say it out loud, because I don't need to. It's the same promise I made in a rented room in a lowland town when I was eleven. It's just bigger now.

Some of the joy comes back to me through the tether, all night long, from somewhere out among the great trees. It lulls me, in the end. Near dawn, I sleep.

In the morning, a knock comes at the door. Not Mara's.

"Surveyor," says a voice. A child's voice: Bet. Very solemn. "Ruth says you're to come to the hall. Now. On your own."
