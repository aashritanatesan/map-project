# Chapter One

There are fourteen locks between the bottom of the gorge and the city of Stonebridge, and by the ninth I've stopped pretending I'm not counting them.

"You'll wear the numbers out," the bargeman says. He leans on the tiller like a man who has done this climb ten thousand times and enjoyed none of them.

"Numbers don't wear out." I keep my eyes on the gate ahead, a black wall of oak streaming water from every seam. "That's the whole appeal."

The sluices open. The river comes in roaring, white and furious, and the barge begins to rise. It's the slowest kind of lift there is, an inch at a time, and I feel every inch in my knees. Cold spray beads on my coat, my eyelashes, the oilcloth over my instrument case. The walls of the lock are slate, sheer and wet and the colour of a bruise on its third day, and they slide downward past us until the sky widens and the next gate looms.

Ten.

Six weeks ago I was in the lowland capital being handed a brass chain and a sworn oath and a pat on the shoulder. *First posting,* my instructor said. *The Reach. Don't look down.* I thought she was joking. I've since learned that the Reach is a place where everything is either up or down and nothing is in between, and that Agnes Bell has never made a joke in her life.

"First time up?" the bargeman asks.

"Is it that obvious?"

He looks pointedly at my hands, which are holding the rail tightly enough to leave marks. "Bit."

At eleven, I see my first wolf.

I don't see a wolf, exactly. I see a cart on the towpath above us, and a hunter walking beside it with a crossbow on his back, and in the bed of the cart, under a tarp that doesn't quite reach, an arm. A man's arm, bare and pale at the shoulder, then furred grey from the elbow down, the hand stretched long and wrong, its nails curved into black claws. Halfway between one thing and another. Someone has tied a paper tag to one of the fingers, the way you'd label a parcel.

"Open ground," the bargeman says, following my look. He spits over the side. "East spur of Highwood ground lapsed last spring. Separate line, walked in a different year. Four hundred acres. Hunters have been feasting."

My stomach turns over, and not from the lock. "That's legal?"

"That's the treaty." He shrugs. "Ground nobody's walked in seven years belongs to nobody. And on nobody's ground, a wolf's just a person who can't change. Can't run, can't fight, can't do much but die." He jerks his chin at the case at my feet. "You're the new surveyor."

It isn't a question, so I don't answer it. The cart rattles out of sight. The tarp flaps once and is still.

"Highwood'll love you," he adds, in a tone that suggests they won't.

---

Stonebridge is a city built by people who refused to accept that a gorge is not a good place for a city.

It climbs. Houses stack on houses, stairways run straight up between them, and everywhere there is water: channelled, sluiced, spilling over little stone weirs in the middle of streets and vanishing under bridges no wider than a doorway. The air smells of wet slate and coal smoke and something sharp and green underneath that I don't recognise until a gust comes down off the mountain. Pine, except not pine. Something resinous and cold.

"Larch," says a woman selling chestnuts at the top lock, when I ask. She looks me up and down. "You'll smell a lot more of it where you're going."

"How do you know where I'm going?"

She nods at my case, then at the chain I've looped over my shoulder because I don't trust anyone else to carry it. Sixty-six feet, a hundred links, every tenth one silver. In the grey morning light the silver links look like someone has threaded small moons onto a necklace.

"Only one kind of person walks around with that," she says. "And they don't usually walk around for long."

I buy chestnuts from her anyway, because I'm cold and because my mother raised me to be polite to rude people, a habit I've never managed to break. The chestnuts burn my fingers through the paper. I eat them on the stairs. There are two hundred and twelve stairs from the top lock to the Survey Office, and I count every one, and I'm only slightly out of breath when I reach the top.

Slightly.

---

The Survey Office sits at the head of the gorge like a judge at the end of a long table. It's grey, of course. But it's a cleaner grey than the rest of the city, the slate dressed smooth and the windows tall and many-paned, so that from inside you can see the whole Reach unrolling below: the gorge, the river, the ridges stacked behind one another in paler and paler blues, and on the highest of them, a smudge of gold.

"The Highwood forest," says the clerk who meets me at the door. He's young, nervous, inked to the wrists. "The larches are turning. They do it all at once, up there. One week green, the next gold, then bare."

"How long until they're bare?"

"Six weeks, if the weather's kind." He hesitates. "It isn't, usually."

He takes me down a corridor lined with ledgers. Not shelves of ledgers: walls of them, floor to ceiling, their spines stamped with dates going back a century. I want to stop and read every one. The clerk walks faster, and I make myself keep up.

At the end of the corridor is a pair of double doors, and in front of the doors is a bench, and on the bench is a row of shoes.

"Boots off," the clerk says, already unlacing his own.

"I'm sorry?"

"No boots on the map."

I look at him. He looks back with the earnest face of a man reciting a rule he has never once questioned. So I sit on the bench, unlace my boots, and set them neatly beside a pair of polished black shoes that are much too expensive to belong to a clerk. Then I follow him through the doors in my socks.

And stop.

The map room is the size of a chapel, and the entire floor is the Reach.

It's carved into slate: every ridge, every river, every ravine, cut into the stone by hands that must have worked for years. The hills are raised in shallow relief. The rivers are channels with real water trickling through them, fed from somewhere under the walls, so the whole floor glints and murmurs. And over all of it, painted into the grooves in red ochre, are the borders. Thin lines, thick lines, lines with numbers stamped beside them. Cairn by cairn, pack by pack, the whole fragile peace of the Reach, laid out flat for anyone to walk across in their socks.

"Beautiful, isn't it?"

The man who says it is standing on the far side of the room, on the gold-painted crown of the Highwood ridge. He's in his fifties, I'd guess, and very neat: grey coat, grey waistcoat, spectacles with thin silver rims. He walks toward me across the map without looking down once, stepping over rivers and ridges as if he knows exactly where each one is. I suppose he does.

"Registrar Voss," he says, and offers his hand.

I shake it. It's dry and cool and perfectly clean. In a building where the clerks are inked to the elbow, that's the first thing about him I notice, and I can't stop noticing it.

"Cass Reed," I say. "Sworn surveyor, first class, first posting. I've brought my papers and my oath card and my chain, and I'd like it noted that I came up all fourteen locks without being sick."

The corner of his mouth moves. "Noted." He lets go of my hand. "We were very glad to receive you, Miss Reed. The Highwood line has been difficult to staff."

"Since June."

The name drops into the room like a stone into one of the little channels at our feet. For half a second there is no sound but the water.

"Since June Marsh," Voss agrees gently. "You've read the report."

"On the barge. Twice." The report was four pages long. A rockfall in the ravine below Cairn Eleven, late spring, during a survey walk. Her chain was found. Her instrument case was found, crushed. She wasn't. *Body not recovered. Presumed buried under the fall.* I read it the way I read everything, looking for the number that doesn't fit, and I didn't find one, and I didn't like that either.

"A tragedy," Voss says. "The Reach moves, Miss Reed. Rain cuts new ravines. Sinkholes open overnight. That's why we walk the lines at all." He turns, and his clean hand sweeps out over the gold crown of Highwood. "Which brings me to your work."

He walks me across the map. I follow carefully, stepping where he steps, trying not to put a sock in a river. Up close, the Highwood border is a long red seam along the high ridge, studded with numbered cairns. Thirty-one of them. I count without meaning to.

"The Highwood line was last walked and chained seven years ago," he says. "The survey was filed on the day of first snow. Under the Cairn Treaty, if it isn't walked and chained again by a sworn surveyor before this year's first snow, the line lapses."

"And it becomes open ground."

"Every acre of it." He says it with what sounds like real regret. "The Highwood pack would lose its oldest forest. Its cairns. Its right to change on that land." A small, delicate pause. "You saw the cart on the towpath, I imagine."

I did. I'm going to see that arm for a long time. "So I walk the line before the snow."

"You walk it, chain it, mark each cairn, and file it here." He taps the floor with one polished toe. "On this map. Your survey becomes the law of the Reach the moment it's carved."

It shouldn't make my pulse quicken. It does anyway. Six years of training, and nobody ever said it so plainly: *you make the law with a chain and a pencil.*

Then I look down at the seam under my feet, and something snags.

The slate along the Highwood line is a different grey.

It's subtle. In the low light you would never see it unless you'd spent six years staring at stone. But the ochre groove here is sharper-edged than the borders around it, and the slate on either side is paler, newer, as if the stone had been dressed and the line cut again. And the line doesn't run quite where the ridge wants it to. Between Cairns Nine and Fourteen it bends inward, away from the forest edge, a long shallow curve that eats into the gold.

"This section's been recut," I say.

Voss doesn't look down. "The ravine." He says it so easily. "June's last survey notes recorded the collapse, and we amended the map. Ground that falls into a ravine can't hold a cairn."

That's true. It's exactly what I'd expect. It's also a quarter of a mile, at least, between Nine and Fourteen, and I've never heard of a rockfall that size.

"Of course," I say, because I'm on my first posting, standing in my socks in front of the most powerful man in the Reach, and I have not yet learned when not to be polite.

---

He gives me everything in a leather satchel: the Office copy of the Highwood line, June's survey notes from the Office files, my letter of commission, and a sealed paper with the Registrar's mark that I'm to hand to my escort.

"Escort?"

"The Highwood pack will send someone to meet you at their gate. It's customary. They have an interest in making sure you're safe." He pauses. "And in making sure you measure generously."

"I measure accurately."

"I'm very glad to hear it." He holds out one more thing: a small card, edged in red. My oath card. I already have one; every sworn surveyor carries one. But he holds this one out to me anyway, and waits until I take it.

"A formality," he says. "We remind every surveyor going up to the packs. You'll know it by heart."

I do. I read it anyway, because he's watching.

*Clause Nine. No wolf of any pack shall bond, marry, or lie with a sworn surveyor of the Reach. The wolf shall be put to death. The surveyor shall lose the hand that holds the chain, and every survey she has signed shall be void.*

I look up. "With respect, Registrar, I've been in the Reach for about three hours and the only wolf I've seen was on a cart. I don't think you need to worry."

"I'm sure I don't." He smiles. It's a very good smile, warm and practised and perfectly clean. "Clause Nine has never once been invoked, Miss Reed. It's there to remind us that a surveyor belongs to no one. Not to a pack, not to a city. Only to the line."

---

The inn at the top lock has a room with a slanted ceiling, a bed that sags in the middle, and a window that looks straight up at the Highwood ridge. I sit on the bed with the satchel open beside me and eat bread and cheese and read June Marsh's notes by candlelight.

She had beautiful handwriting. Small and slanted and precise, every figure crossed and dotted, the kind of hand I've spent my whole life trying to have. She chained the first eight cairns of the Highwood line in four days. Her notes are full of tiny asides in the margins: *wind from the north, cold. Larch needles on the chain, sticky with resin. Escort sullen.* I laugh at that one, then feel bad about laughing.

Then I reach Cairn Nine, and the notes stop.

Not just stop. There's a final page, half-written, then nothing. I turn to the back, to the index that every Office file carries, the record of each page entered by the clerks. *Highwood line, J. Marsh, field notes: forty-one pages.*

I count.

Then I count again, because I'm me.

Forty.

Outside, far up on the ridge, something begins to sing. It's a long, rising note, and then another voice under it, and then another, until the whole mountainside is ringing with it. The candle flame shivers. I don't think it's the draught.

Somewhere between Cairn Nine and the ravine, the last page of a dead woman's survey has gone missing.

And in the morning, I'm walking up there to find out why.
