# Chain and Cairn

*Moonbound, Book 1 of 3*


# Chapter One

There are fourteen locks between the bottom of the gorge and the city of Stonebridge, and by the ninth I've stopped pretending I'm not counting them.

"You'll wear the numbers out," the bargeman says. He leans on the tiller like a man who has done this climb ten thousand times and enjoyed none of them.

"Numbers don't wear out." I keep my eyes on the gate ahead, a black wall of oak streaming water from every seam. "That's the whole appeal."

The sluices open. The river comes in roaring, white and furious, and the barge begins to rise. It's the slowest kind of lift there is, an inch at a time, and I feel every inch in my knees. Cold spray beads on my coat, my eyelashes, the oilcloth over my instrument case. The walls of the lock are slate, sheer and wet and the colour of a bruise on its third day, and they slide downward past us until the sky widens and the next gate looms.

Ten.

Six weeks ago I was in the lowland capital being handed a brass chain and a sworn oath and a pat on the shoulder. *First posting,* my instructor said. *The Reach. Don't look down.* I thought she was joking. I've since learned that the Reach is a place where everything is either up or down and nothing is in between, and that Agnes Bell has never made a joke in her life.

"First time up?" the bargeman asks.

"Is it that obvious?"

He looks pointedly at my hands, which are holding the rail tightly enough to leave marks. "Bit."

At eleven, I see my first wolf.

I don't see a wolf, exactly. I see a cart on the towpath above us, and a hunter walking beside it with a crossbow on his back, and in the bed of the cart, under a tarp that doesn't quite reach, an arm. A man's arm, bare and pale at the shoulder, then furred grey from the elbow down, the hand stretched long and wrong, its nails curved into black claws. Halfway between one thing and another. Someone has tied a paper tag to one of the fingers, the way you'd label a parcel.

"Open ground," the bargeman says, following my look. He spits over the side. "East spur of Highwood ground lapsed last spring. Separate line, walked in a different year. Four hundred acres. Hunters have been feasting."

My stomach turns over, and not from the lock. "That's legal?"

"That's the treaty." He shrugs. "Ground nobody's walked in seven years belongs to nobody. And on nobody's ground, a wolf's just a person who can't change. Can't run, can't fight, can't do much but die." He jerks his chin at the case at my feet. "You're the new surveyor."

It isn't a question, so I don't answer it. The cart rattles out of sight. The tarp flaps once and is still.

"Highwood'll love you," he adds, in a tone that suggests they won't.

---

Stonebridge is a city built by people who refused to accept that a gorge is not a good place for a city.

It climbs. Houses stack on houses, stairways run straight up between them, and everywhere there is water: channelled, sluiced, spilling over little stone weirs in the middle of streets and vanishing under bridges no wider than a doorway. The air smells of wet slate and coal smoke and something sharp and green underneath that I don't recognise until a gust comes down off the mountain. Pine, except not pine. Something resinous and cold.

"Larch," says a woman selling chestnuts at the top lock, when I ask. She looks me up and down. "You'll smell a lot more of it where you're going."

"How do you know where I'm going?"

She nods at my case, then at the chain I've looped over my shoulder because I don't trust anyone else to carry it. Sixty-six feet, a hundred links, every tenth one silver. In the grey morning light the silver links look like someone has threaded small moons onto a necklace.

"Only one kind of person walks around with that," she says. "And they don't usually walk around for long."

I buy chestnuts from her anyway, because I'm cold and because my mother raised me to be polite to rude people, a habit I've never managed to break. The chestnuts burn my fingers through the paper. I eat them on the stairs. There are two hundred and twelve stairs from the top lock to the Survey Office, and I count every one, and I'm only slightly out of breath when I reach the top.

Slightly.

---

The Survey Office sits at the head of the gorge like a judge at the end of a long table. It's grey, of course. But it's a cleaner grey than the rest of the city, the slate dressed smooth and the windows tall and many-paned, so that from inside you can see the whole Reach unrolling below: the gorge, the river, the ridges stacked behind one another in paler and paler blues, and on the highest of them, a smudge of gold.

"The Highwood forest," says the clerk who meets me at the door. He's young, nervous, inked to the wrists. "The larches are turning. They do it all at once, up there. One week green, the next gold, then bare."

"How long until they're bare?"

"Six weeks, if the weather's kind." He hesitates. "It isn't, usually."

He takes me down a corridor lined with ledgers. Not shelves of ledgers: walls of them, floor to ceiling, their spines stamped with dates going back a century. I want to stop and read every one. The clerk walks faster, and I make myself keep up.

At the end of the corridor is a pair of double doors, and in front of the doors is a bench, and on the bench is a row of shoes.

"Boots off," the clerk says, already unlacing his own.

"I'm sorry?"

"No boots on the map."

I look at him. He looks back with the earnest face of a man reciting a rule he has never once questioned. So I sit on the bench, unlace my boots, and set them neatly beside a pair of polished black shoes that are much too expensive to belong to a clerk. Then I follow him through the doors in my socks.

And stop.

The map room is the size of a chapel, and the entire floor is the Reach.

It's carved into slate: every ridge, every river, every ravine, cut into the stone by hands that must have worked for years. The hills are raised in shallow relief. The rivers are channels with real water trickling through them, fed from somewhere under the walls, so the whole floor glints and murmurs. And over all of it, painted into the grooves in red ochre, are the borders. Thin lines, thick lines, lines with numbers stamped beside them. Cairn by cairn, pack by pack, the whole fragile peace of the Reach, laid out flat for anyone to walk across in their socks.

"Beautiful, isn't it?"

The man who says it is standing on the far side of the room, on the gold-painted crown of the Highwood ridge. He's in his fifties, I'd guess, and very neat: grey coat, grey waistcoat, spectacles with thin silver rims. He walks toward me across the map without looking down once, stepping over rivers and ridges as if he knows exactly where each one is. I suppose he does.

"Registrar Voss," he says, and offers his hand.

I shake it. It's dry and cool and perfectly clean. In a building where the clerks are inked to the elbow, that's the first thing about him I notice, and I can't stop noticing it.

"Cass Reed," I say. "Sworn surveyor, first class, first posting. I've brought my papers and my oath card and my chain, and I'd like it noted that I came up all fourteen locks without being sick."

The corner of his mouth moves. "Noted." He lets go of my hand. "We were very glad to receive you, Miss Reed. The Highwood line has been difficult to staff."

"Since June."

The name drops into the room like a stone into one of the little channels at our feet. For half a second there is no sound but the water.

"Since June Marsh," Voss agrees gently. "You've read the report."

"On the barge. Twice." The report was four pages long. A rockfall in the ravine below Cairn Eleven, late spring, during a survey walk. Her chain was found. Her instrument case was found, crushed. She wasn't. *Body not recovered. Presumed buried under the fall.* I read it the way I read everything, looking for the number that doesn't fit, and I didn't find one, and I didn't like that either.

"A tragedy," Voss says. "The Reach moves, Miss Reed. Rain cuts new ravines. Sinkholes open overnight. That's why we walk the lines at all." He turns, and his clean hand sweeps out over the gold crown of Highwood. "Which brings me to your work."

He walks me across the map. I follow carefully, stepping where he steps, trying not to put a sock in a river. Up close, the Highwood border is a long red seam along the high ridge, studded with numbered cairns. Thirty-one of them. I count without meaning to.

"The Highwood line was last walked and chained seven years ago," he says. "The survey was filed on the day of first snow. Under the Cairn Treaty, if it isn't walked and chained again by a sworn surveyor before this year's first snow, the line lapses."

"And it becomes open ground."

"Every acre of it." He says it with what sounds like real regret. "The Highwood pack would lose its oldest forest. Its cairns. Its right to change on that land." A small, delicate pause. "You saw the cart on the towpath, I imagine."

I did. I'm going to see that arm for a long time. "So I walk the line before the snow."

"You walk it, chain it, mark each cairn, and file it here." He taps the floor with one polished toe. "On this map. Your survey becomes the law of the Reach the moment it's carved."

It shouldn't make my pulse quicken. It does anyway. Six years of training, and nobody ever said it so plainly: *you make the law with a chain and a pencil.*

Then I look down at the seam under my feet, and something snags.

The slate along the Highwood line is a different grey.

It's subtle. In the low light you would never see it unless you'd spent six years staring at stone. But the ochre groove here is sharper-edged than the borders around it, and the slate on either side is paler, newer, as if the stone had been dressed and the line cut again. And the line doesn't run quite where the ridge wants it to. Between Cairns Nine and Fourteen it bends inward, away from the forest edge, a long shallow curve that eats into the gold.

"This section's been recut," I say.

Voss doesn't look down. "The ravine." He says it so easily. "June's last survey notes recorded the collapse, and we amended the map. Ground that falls into a ravine can't hold a cairn."

That's true. It's exactly what I'd expect. It's also a quarter of a mile, at least, between Nine and Fourteen, and I've never heard of a rockfall that size.

"Of course," I say, because I'm on my first posting, standing in my socks in front of the most powerful man in the Reach, and I have not yet learned when not to be polite.

---

He gives me everything in a leather satchel: the Office copy of the Highwood line, June's survey notes from the Office files, my letter of commission, and a sealed paper with the Registrar's mark that I'm to hand to my escort.

"Escort?"

"The Highwood pack will send someone to meet you at their gate. It's customary. They have an interest in making sure you're safe." He pauses. "And in making sure you measure generously."

"I measure accurately."

"I'm very glad to hear it." He holds out one more thing: a small card, edged in red. My oath card. I already have one; every sworn surveyor carries one. But he holds this one out to me anyway, and waits until I take it.

"A formality," he says. "We remind every surveyor going up to the packs. You'll know it by heart."

I do. I read it anyway, because he's watching.

*Clause Nine. No wolf of any pack shall bond, marry, or lie with a sworn surveyor of the Reach. The wolf shall be put to death. The surveyor shall lose the hand that holds the chain, and every survey she has signed shall be void.*

I look up. "With respect, Registrar, I've been in the Reach for about three hours and the only wolf I've seen was on a cart. I don't think you need to worry."

"I'm sure I don't." He smiles. It's a very good smile, warm and practised and perfectly clean. "Clause Nine has never once been invoked, Miss Reed. It's there to remind us that a surveyor belongs to no one. Not to a pack, not to a city. Only to the line."

---

The inn at the top lock has a room with a slanted ceiling, a bed that sags in the middle, and a window that looks straight up at the Highwood ridge. I sit on the bed with the satchel open beside me and eat bread and cheese and read June Marsh's notes by candlelight.

She had beautiful handwriting. Small and slanted and precise, every figure crossed and dotted, the kind of hand I've spent my whole life trying to have. She chained the first eight cairns of the Highwood line in four days. Her notes are full of tiny asides in the margins: *wind from the north, cold. Larch needles on the chain, sticky with resin. Escort sullen.* I laugh at that one, then feel bad about laughing.

Then I reach Cairn Nine, and the notes stop.

Not just stop. There's a final page, half-written, then nothing. I turn to the back, to the index that every Office file carries, the record of each page entered by the clerks. *Highwood line, J. Marsh, field notes: forty-one pages.*

I count.

Then I count again, because I'm me.

Forty.

Outside, far up on the ridge, something begins to sing. It's a long, rising note, and then another voice under it, and then another, until the whole mountainside is ringing with it. The candle flame shivers. I don't think it's the draught.

Somewhere between Cairn Nine and the ravine, the last page of a dead woman's survey has gone missing.

And in the morning, I'm walking up there to find out why.



# Chapter Two

The innkeeper has opinions about the weather, and he shares them with me over porridge whether I want them or not.

"First snow on the high ridge," he says, setting down a pot of tea that has been stewing since roughly the founding of the city, "comes when it likes. Could be three weeks. Could be tomorrow."

"The Office said six weeks."

"The Office," he says, with enormous dignity, "has never once been up there."

I look out the window. The Highwood ridge is cut sharp against a sky so clear and pale it looks scrubbed. The gold of the larches has spread overnight. Yesterday it was a smudge along the crest. This morning it runs halfway down the slope, like someone has tipped a pot of honey over the mountain and let it ooze.

"It's beautiful," I say.

"So's a bear," says the innkeeper, and takes my bowl away before I've finished.

---

The road out of Stonebridge climbs the way everything here climbs: steeply, and without apology. I leave the last houses behind within the hour and follow a cart track up the side of the gorge, switchback after switchback, the river shrinking below me to a white thread. I have my instrument case strapped on my back, the satchel across my chest, and the chain looped over my shoulder, and by the fifth switchback I've started to have feelings about all three of them.

I don't look down. That's the trick. I look at my boots, at the track, at the rock wall on my left. I count my steps in sets of a hundred, the same as links on a chain, and every hundred I allow myself one glance at the view. One glance only. The view is enormous and blue and sickening, and I love it, and I'd love it much more from a chair.

Halfway up, the track passes the quarries.

I smell them before I see them, a raw mineral bite in the back of my throat, sharper than the wet-slate smell of the city. Then the hillside opens on my right into a vast grey wound. Terraces of cut stone step down into the earth. Men swarm the ledges with picks and wedges. A crane creaks somewhere. Slabs of slate the size of doors sit stacked by the track, waiting for carts, their faces split so clean they shine.

There's a sign at the quarry gate: *STONEBRIDGE MINING GUILD. TRESPASSERS PROSECUTED.*

Beyond the quarry, past a line of wind-bent trees, the land rises again into scrub and boulders. Someone has been busy there too. A row of wooden stakes marches up the slope, each one topped with a strip of yellow cloth. I count fourteen before the slope hides the rest. Two men with a measuring rope are setting another.

I stop. I shouldn't. I do anyway.

"Excuse me," I call. "What's that ground?"

One of the men straightens up. He looks at my chain, and his face does something careful. "Guild claim, miss."

"Whose land was it?"

"Nobody's." He shrugs. "Lapsed in spring. Open ground. Guild bought the mineral rights off the Office last month."

The east spur. The four hundred acres. I look up the slope at the yellow flags snapping in the wind and think of the arm on the cart, and the paper tag tied to one finger.

"Thank you," I say, because I'm polite. And then, because I'm a surveyor, I take out my notebook and write down the number of stakes, the bearing of the line they're setting, and the date.

The man watches me do it. He doesn't say anything. He doesn't have to.

---

The cart track gives up an hour past the quarries and becomes a path, and the path becomes a sort of determined suggestion, and the larches begin.

I've never been in a larch forest. Nobody warned me it would be like walking into a lantern. The trees are tall and straight and widely spaced, and every one of them is gold, needle to needle, so the light that comes through is gold too. It lies on the ground in warm drifts. The needles that have already fallen are soft underfoot, so soft that my boots stop making any sound at all, and for the first time since I left the lowlands it is properly quiet. No river. No sluices. No men shouting in quarries. Just wind moving high up in the gold, and the faint sticky smell of resin, and my own breathing.

I like it immediately. I distrust it almost as fast.

The Highwood gate is not a gate. It's two larches the width of a cart apart, a cairn daubed so red it looks freshly bled on, and a woman leaning against one of the trees like she's been carved there to frighten surveyors.

It's working.

"Cass Reed," I say, and stick out my hand. "Survey Office. I'm your problem for the next six weeks."

She looks at the hand. She looks at the chain coiled over my shoulder, the silver links winking every tenth loop. Then she looks back at my face, and I understand for the first time why the lowland manuals call Highwood eyes *amber*. It isn't a colour. It's a warning.

"Mara," she says. She doesn't take the hand.

I put it in my pocket, where it can be embarrassed in private. "Great. Lovely. So, the route. I've got June's field notes, the Office copy of the line, and a deadline of first snow, which the man at the inn says is either three weeks away or tomorrow, depending on how much you've paid him."

"Did Voss send you?"

"The Office sent me."

"That's a yes." She pushes off the tree. She's taller than me, which isn't hard, but she's also taller than the trees seem to want her to be, as if the forest leans back to make room. "Last surveyor walked this line in spring. She came back in a sack."

"She didn't come back at all," I say. "I read the report."

Something moves in her face. Not softness, exactly. More like a door that's been shut so long it's surprised to find it has hinges.

She has a bow across her back and a quiver at her hip, the fletching dyed the same red as the cairn. Her hair is black and braided in one long rope that hangs nearly to her waist. Her coat is worn leather, patched at the elbows, buttoned to the throat even though the afternoon is mild. Her hands are long and brown and callused across the first two fingers of the right, where a bowstring would bite. She's maybe thirty. She looks like she's been angry for about twenty-nine of those years and has got very efficient at it.

I remember the satchel. "I'm supposed to give you this." I fish out the sealed letter with the Registrar's mark and hold it out.

She takes it this time. Not my hand: the letter, held between two fingers like something that might be wet. She breaks the seal with her thumbnail and reads. It's short. I watch her eyes go down the page once, then back to the top, then down again.

Then she laughs. It's not a nice laugh. It's barely a laugh at all; it's the sound a person makes when something confirms exactly what they expected.

"What?" I ask.

She turns the letter so I can see. The handwriting is a clerk's, but the signature at the bottom is Voss's: small, neat, perfectly upright.

*The Office trusts the Highwood pack to extend every courtesy to its surveyor. The Office reminds the pack that the surveyor is under its protection for the length of the walk. Should any harm come to the surveyor before the line is chained and filed, the Office will be obliged to regard the line as unwalked.*

I read it twice. "Unwalked."

"Unwalked," Mara says. "Lapsed. Open ground." She folds the letter in half, then in half again, very precisely. "If you fall off a mountain, we lose the forest."

There's a long, airy silence. Somewhere high above us a bird says something rude.

"Well," I say. "Then I'd strongly recommend not letting me fall off a mountain."

She looks at me for a long moment. I can't tell what she's looking for. Whatever it is, I don't think she finds it.

"How many surveys have you walked?" she asks.

"Including this one?"

"Not including this one."

"Practice lines," I say. "Lots. In the lowlands. They're very flat." I pause. "Extremely flat. You could survey them lying down."

"So none."

"So none that count," I admit. "But I did six years of training, I graduated first in triangulation, and my chain errors are the lowest in my year. I can measure a mile to within a hand's width. I'm good, Mara. I'm just new."

"June was good," she says.

It comes out flat. Not cruel. Just a fact, set down between us like a stone. And for a second there's that door again, behind her eyes, opening a crack on something dark and closing just as fast.

I want to ask. I don't. I've been in the Reach for one day and I already know there are questions you don't get to ask people with bows.

She tucks the folded letter inside her coat. When she does it, her collar shifts and I see the edge of a scar along her collarbone: three pale, parallel lines, old and silvered, like something with claws once tried very hard to open her up.

She sees me see it. She buttons the collar back up.

"Walk behind me," she says. "Don't touch the cairns until I say. Don't swing that thing near me."

"It's a measuring chain. It doesn't *swing*. It's sixty-six feet of extremely calm brass."

"It's silver."

"It's *ten per cent* silver."

She's already walking. The larch needles swallow her footsteps whole, so the only sound on the path is me: my boots, my breath, and the chain clinking softly against my hip like it's laughing at me.

I follow her between the two larches, past the red cairn, and onto Highwood ground.

It doesn't feel different. I don't know what I expected. Some shiver in the air, some line I could feel the way I can feel a slope under my feet. There's nothing. Just more gold, more quiet, the same soft needles. But I count my steps anyway, out of habit, and I'm at forty when I notice the stream.

It's tiny: a bright thread of water slipping over moss between two stones. I'm thirsty and hot from the climb, so I crouch beside it and cup my hands and drink.

It tastes of iron. Of iron and something else, warm and metallic, like when you bite your own tongue.

I spit it out before I can stop myself. Mara has stopped on the path ahead. She's watching me with an expression I can only describe as deeply, profoundly unsurprised.

"That's normal," she says. "It's the stone."

"It tastes like *blood*."

"Yes." She turns away. "Welcome to Highwood."

---

We walk until the light starts to slant. Mara doesn't speak. I don't either, at first, because I'm busy breathing, and then because I'm busy being offended about not being spoken to, and then because the path has started to climb again and I need all my attention for my feet.

It's nearly an hour before I try again.

"Where are we stopping tonight?"

"Cairn One."

"And is Cairn One near a bed, or—"

"It's near a rock."

"A comfortable rock?"

She doesn't dignify that. A squirrel the colour of rust streaks across the path, stops dead, takes one look at Mara, and flees up a trunk so fast it nearly falls off the far side. I know exactly how it feels.

"Can I ask you something?"

"You will anyway."

That's fair. "Do you always escort the surveyors? Is it a job, like, an official Highwood post? Does it come with a hat?"

The path narrows between two boulders furred with moss. She goes through first, sideways, without touching either one. I go through after her and touch both, and get moss on my coat, and pretend I meant to.

"No," she says.

"No to the hat, or—"

"No, I don't always escort them." A pause, three steps long. I count them. "My mother usually sends someone else."

"So why you, this time?"

She doesn't look back. Her braid swings once against her spine, a black pendulum. "Nobody else would."

It lands somewhere under my ribs and stays there. Because I know that answer. I gave it, in my own head, the day Agnes Bell called me into her office and told me the Highwood post was mine if I wanted it. *Why me?* And the honest answer, the one she was too kind or too tired to say out loud: because nobody senior would take a dead woman's route.

"Well," I say, to Mara's back. "That makes two of us."

She doesn't answer. But her step changes, just slightly: shorter, a fraction slower. As if she's no longer trying quite so hard to leave me behind.

Up ahead, the trail tilts toward a drop I refuse to look at. The air already tastes of iron.

I tell the theodolite, quietly, that we're going to be fine.

It doesn't believe me either.


# Chapter Three

Cairn One is, as promised, a rock.

It's a very large rock, to be fair: a heap of grey slabs stacked chest-high on a shoulder of the ridge, the top stone daubed red, the paint cracked into a fine web by seven winters. Someone has scratched a mark into the face of the third slab down: a small circle with a line through it, and a date. *Seven years.* The surveyor before June. And under it, newer, still sharp, a slanted little *J.M.* and this spring's date.

I touch the letters with one fingertip before I can think better of it.

"Don't," says Mara, from behind me.

"You said not until you said."

"I haven't said."

"Can you say?"

A long pause. She's sitting on a boulder a few yards off with her bow across her knees, watching the sunrise burn the mist off the valley as if it has personally wronged her. She slept, as far as I can tell, sitting up against a tree with her eyes half open. I slept on the promised rock, which was not comfortable, and woke with a crick in my neck and larch needles in places larch needles have no business being.

"Fine," she says. "Touch it."

I touch it. Nothing happens. I'm not sure what I thought would happen.

---

Here is what a survey is, at its simplest: you start at a fixed point and you measure your way to the next one, and the next, and the next, and at the end you have a line that everyone has agreed to believe in.

Here is what a survey is in the Reach: you start at a heap of rocks on a mountain, and you measure your way to another heap of rocks on another part of the mountain, over ground that is trying to kill you, while a woman with a bow watches you do it as if she's hoping you'll fail.

I set up the theodolite over Cairn One. Brass legs, brass body, a telescope no longer than my forearm, a bubble level I have to coax into the centre with tiny turns of the screws. My fingers are cold. The bubble wanders. I tell it, under my breath, to be reasonable, and it settles.

"You talk to it," Mara says.

"It listens better than most people."

I take my bearing on Cairn Two, which is a red speck on the next spur along, nearly a mile off, with what looks like a ravine in between. I write the angle in my field book. Then I unloop the chain.

And I realise, with the creeping horror of a person who has done every practice line of her life with a fellow student holding the other end, that I need someone to hold the other end.

I look at Mara.

Mara looks at the chain.

"No," she says.

"It's a two-person job."

"Then you should have brought two persons."

"The Office doesn't send two. The escort usually—" I stop, because I don't actually know what the escort usually does. The manuals were written in the lowlands. In the lowlands, nobody's escort is allergic to the equipment.

She's on her feet now, and I watch her look at the hundred links pooled on the needles at my feet, at the ten silver ones winking in the early light. Her mouth goes thin.

"The handles," I say quickly. "The handles are brass. Look." I pick up one end and hold it out: a ring of brass, worn smooth by a century of hands. "You only touch this. You never have to touch a link. I do all the work. You just walk to where I point and hold it still and keep it off the ground."

She doesn't move.

"Or," I say, "we can stand here for six weeks and let the snow decide."

That gets her. I see it hit. She crosses the needles in four long strides and takes the brass handle out of my hand. Her fingers don't touch mine. She's careful about that. I notice that she's careful, and I notice that I've noticed, and then I make myself stop noticing anything except the angle.

"Toward the red speck," I say. "Sixty-six feet. When the chain goes taut, stop, and hold it at the height of your waist. I'll come to you."

She walks. The chain uncoils behind her, clinking over the needles, brass and silver and brass. At the end of it she stops, without being told twice, and holds it at exactly the right height, and doesn't let it sag. I check the tension, mark the ground with a pin, and call the first length.

"One."

She doesn't answer. But she walks on when I wave, and holds the chain steady again, and stops.

"Two."

---

By mid-morning we've found a rhythm, and I've found out that I hate this mountain.

The line from Cairn One to Cairn Two does not go around the ravine. Lines don't go around things. They go straight, because the whole point of a line is that it's straight, and the treaty was drawn up by people who either didn't know or didn't care that a straight line across the Reach goes through some deeply unreasonable places.

This one goes over a scree slope.

It's not a cliff. I tell myself that firmly. It's a slope. A long fan of broken grey slate pouring down from a crag above us into the ravine below, every stone flat and loose and ready to slide. You can walk across it. People do. Mara does, right now, the chain handle in one hand, stepping from stone to stone as if she's walking across her own kitchen.

I put one foot on it. The slate shifts under my boot with a dry, clattering hiss, and a few small stones break away and go skittering down, and down, and *down*, and I hear them for much longer than I want to.

I take my foot back.

"Problem?" calls Mara. She's twenty yards out on the scree, holding the chain taut.

"No."

"You've stopped."

"I'm admiring the view."

"You're looking at your boots."

"They're very good boots." My heart is going so hard I can feel it in my teeth. I look at the chain, stretched out across the slope between us, and I think: *Sixty-six feet. One length. You can do one length.* I've done thousands.

I step onto the scree. I don't look down. I look at the chain, at the brass handle in Mara's fist, at the red speck of Cairn Two hanging on the far spur like a promise. I count my steps. Ten. Twenty. The slate slides and hisses and settles. Thirty. My knees are water. Forty.

I reach her. I mark the pin. I don't fall.

"Eleven," I say. My voice comes out reasonably normal, which I'm proud of.

Mara looks at me. Up close, in the full morning light, her eyes are paler than I thought, more gold than brown, with a darker ring around the edge. She's frowning slightly. Not in contempt, I don't think. More like someone who's just watched a dog walk on its hind legs and is trying to decide how impressed to be.

"You're afraid of heights," she says.

"I'm afraid of *falling*. It's different. Heights are fine. Heights are just numbers." I take the handle out of her hand, being careful not to touch her fingers, and wave her on. "Keep walking. We've got a mountain to measure."

---

Somewhere around length forty-three, I get careless.

It's early afternoon. We're past the scree and onto a long grassy shoulder, and I'm happy, really happy, the bright fizzing happiness of a job going well. The theodolite readings are clean. The chain lengths are clean. Cairn Two was exactly where the Office copy said it should be, to within the width of my thumb, and I'm already planning the neat column of figures I'll write tonight.

The chain kinks. It happens. Two links twist and catch, and the line goes slack in the middle. I'm thinking about my figures. I flick the chain to free the kink, the way I've done a thousand times on the flat lowland fields, a quick snap of the wrist that sends a ripple running down the whole length.

The ripple runs straight to Mara.

The chain jumps in her hand. A loop of it whips up and slaps across the back of her wrist, just above the brass handle, and one of the links that slaps her is silver.

She doesn't scream. I think I'd feel better if she screamed. She makes a sound like air leaving a punctured skin, very short, very quiet, and her whole body goes rigid. The handle drops. The chain slides off her wrist and falls into the grass.

Where it touched her, the skin has gone white. Then red. Then something worse than red, a raw, wet, blistered stripe exactly the width of a chain link, already rising.

There's a smell. I'm never going to forget the smell.

"Oh, gods," I say. I'm running, I'm already running, I'm across sixty-six feet of grass before I know I've moved. "Oh, gods, I'm sorry, I'm so sorry, I didn't—let me see—"

"Don't." She's cradling the wrist against her chest. Her face is grey under the brown. "Don't touch it."

"I've got salve. In my pack. It's for burns. It's for rope burns, but it's—"

"It won't help."

"You don't know that."

"*I know that.*" She bares her teeth when she says it. For a second I see them, and they're ordinary, human teeth, but there's something behind her eyes that isn't ordinary at all, and I take a step back without meaning to.

She sees the step. Something in her face closes.

"It heals," she says. Lower now. Flatter. "Slowly. Silver always does. Just get your chain."

I don't get my chain. I get my pack instead, and I dig out the little tin of salve, and I kneel in the grass beside her and hold it out. My hand is shaking. I don't try to make it stop.

"I'm not going to touch it," I say. "I'm just going to give you this. And you can throw it off the mountain if you want. But I did this, and I'm not going to just go and pick up the thing that did it and pretend I didn't."

For a long moment she doesn't move. The wind runs through the grass around us, bending it silver, then gold. Somewhere far below in the ravine a stream is talking to itself.

Then she reaches out with her good hand and takes the tin.

Her fingertips brush mine. Just barely. Just the edge of the nail against the pad of my thumb.

It's nothing. It's the smallest touch in the world. I feel it anyway, all the way up my arm.

She opens the tin, smells it, makes a face, and smears a thin layer over the burn with one finger. She doesn't thank me. She doesn't look at me. She closes the tin and puts it in her own coat pocket, which I'm fairly sure means I'm never getting it back.

"It doesn't swing," she says. "You said."

I wince. "I lied."

"Mm." She stands, wrist tucked against her ribs. She looks down at the chain in the grass, at the silver link lying there as innocent as a coin.

Then she bends, picks up the brass handle with her left hand, and holds it out to me.

"Forty-three," she says.

---

We reach Cairn Three just as the sun drops behind the far ridges and the whole forest goes from gold to copper to rust. I've counted every length. I've written every angle. It's been the best and worst day of my life, in roughly equal measure, and I am so tired my bones feel hollow.

I set the theodolite over the cairn to take one last bearing before dark. Mara stays back, at the edge of the trees.

Then she goes still.

It's a particular kind of still. Not the stillness of someone resting. It's the stillness of a deer that has heard a twig snap. Her head comes up. She turns her face into the wind that's coming up the valley from the south, and her nostrils flare, once, twice.

"What is it?" I ask.

She doesn't answer for a moment. When she does, her voice has gone low and quiet, and there's something in it I haven't heard before, not even when the silver burned her.

"River," she says.

I look south, down the long dusky slope toward the valleys. I see nothing but trees.

"Is that bad?"

Mara slides an arrow out of her quiver without looking at it, and sets it to the string.

"Get your things," she says. "We're not sleeping here."


# Chapter Four

I learn three things about walking a mountain in the dark.

The first is that there's no such thing as dark, not really, not when your eyes have had an hour to adjust. There's starlight, thin and silver, and the paler shapes of the larches against the black, and the faint glow of the needles on the path, which hold onto the day's gold even after the sun is gone.

The second is that none of that helps, because you still can't see your feet.

The third is that Mara doesn't need to.

She walks ahead of me at the same steady pace she kept all day, not hurrying, not slowing, turning left and right along a path I can't see at all. Every so often she stops and lifts her face into the wind, and I stop too, and hold my breath, and listen to absolutely nothing. Then she moves on. I follow the faint pale shape of her coat and the occasional whisper of her braid, and I trip over roots I'm fairly sure weren't there for her.

"How do you do that?" I whisper, after the fourth root.

"Do what?"

"See."

"I don't." She steps over something. I step on it. It's a branch. It snaps like a gunshot, and she turns her head and gives me a look I can feel even in the dark. "I smell."

"You smell the path?"

"I smell where the ground is soft and where it's hard. Where water's been. Where deer go." A pause. "Where you're about to fall over."

"I'm not about to—"

I fall over.

She doesn't catch me. She doesn't laugh, either, which is almost worse. She just waits while I pick myself up out of a drift of needles and check that the theodolite case hasn't cracked, and then she walks on.

---

We stop, finally, in a hollow under an overhang of rock, high above where we'd meant to camp. Mara crouches at the lip of it for a long time, looking south, breathing slow. Then she comes back in and starts gathering twigs.

"They've gone," she says.

"The River people?"

"Three of them. Scouts. They followed us from Cairn Two." She stacks the twigs into a small neat pyramid, no bigger than her two hands. "They're back on their own side now."

"Why were they following us?"

"Because River follows everything." She strikes a flint. A spark catches, and she cups it in her palms and breathes on it until it grows. In the first small light her face looks sharper, all cheekbones and shadow. "They trade with Stonebridge. Furs, mostly. Safe passage for the Guild carts. And news. Everything they see, your Registrar hears about by the end of the week."

"He's not my Registrar."

"He signed your letter."

"He signs everybody's letters. That's his whole job." I sit down on the other side of the fire, carefully, because my legs have stopped being reliable. "If it helps, he wasn't very nice to me either."

"Was he not?"

"He was perfectly nice. That's what I mean." I hold my hands out to the fire. It's tiny, but it's warm, and the warmth goes into my fingers like a drink. "He was very, very nice. With very clean hands."

Mara looks at me across the flames. I can't read her face. But after a moment she reaches into her coat and brings out a paper packet, and from the packet, two strips of something dark and dried, and holds one out to me across the fire.

"What is it?"

"Venison."

"Did you kill it?"

"No, it died of embarrassment." She keeps holding it out. "Eat it or don't."

I eat it. It's salty and smoky and tough as a boot, and I'm so hungry it's the best thing I've ever tasted. I say so. She says nothing. But I think, though I can't be sure in the firelight, that her mouth moves at one corner.

Emboldened, I chew, and swallow, and ask the thing I've been wanting to ask since the gate.

"Can you change? Into—you know."

"Into what?"

"Don't make me say it."

"You're a surveyor. You're supposed to be precise."

"Into a wolf," I say. "A large, alarming wolf. Can you just do it? Whenever you like?"

She tears off a piece of venison with her teeth and chews it for a long, slow, deliberate time.

"Not in front of surveyors," she says.

"Is that a rule?"

"It's a preference."

"Because I'd be very respectful. I'd look away. I'd take notes, obviously, but I'd take them *respectfully*—"

"Cass."

"Yes?"

"Eat your venison."

I eat my venison. It occurs to me, chewing, that I've been in this forest a day and a half and she's said more words to me in the last ten minutes than in all the rest of it put together. It also occurs to me that every single one of them has been a way of not answering my question.

I file that away, the way I file everything. In a neat column. For later.

---

Later, when the fire has burned down to a red eye and I'm pretending not to be too cold to sleep, I watch her unwrap her wrist.

She does it carefully, with her back half turned to me, as if she doesn't want me to see. The salve is still there, shiny in the coal-light. Underneath it the burn has crusted at the edges, but the centre is still raw and wet and angry, exactly the width of a chain link. It hasn't healed at all. If anything, it looks worse.

"You said it heals."

"Slowly." She doesn't look up. "Silver takes weeks."

"Weeks."

"Sometimes."

I think about the chain, coiled in my pack six inches from my head. I think about how I'm going to have to flick it, and drag it, and coil it, and hand her the brass handle, over and over, for thirty-one cairns.

"I'll be more careful," I say.

"You'll have to be." She rewraps the wrist in a strip of cloth and ties it off with her teeth. "If I lose that hand, I can't draw. If I can't draw, I can't keep you alive. And if I can't keep you alive—"

"You lose the forest. I know." I pull my coat tighter. "I read the letter."

She finally looks at me. Her eyes catch the last of the fire and throw it back, and just for a second they look less like eyes than like two small lamps.

"I'd lose the forest anyway," she says. "Eventually. Every survey, we lose a little. A cairn moves. A line bends. A stream changes course, and the Office decides the old line's wrong." Her voice is very even. "We've lost two miles of ground in my lifetime. My mother's lost four in hers. So no, Cass Reed, I don't think your clean-handed Registrar is going to give it back."

It's the first time she's used my name.

She says it like she's testing the weight of it. Like a stone you pick up at a river's edge to see whether it's worth skimming, or whether you should just put it back where you found it. I don't know which she decides. I'm not sure she does either.

The coal pops. Somewhere out in the dark an owl asks a question, and nothing answers.

I don't know what to say to the rest of it. So I don't say anything. I lie down with my head on my pack and look up at the underside of the rock, and think about a line on a slate floor bending a quarter mile inward between Cairns Nine and Fourteen, and the paler colour of the stone.

---

I wake to Mara's hand over my mouth.

It's barely dawn. Grey light, grey air, mist lying in the valleys below like milk in a bowl. She's crouched over me with one finger at her lips, and her other hand is pressed flat over my mouth, warm and dry and smelling of woodsmoke and resin. Her eyes are on something beyond the lip of the hollow.

I nod. She takes her hand away.

We crawl to the edge together on our bellies, and I look down.

Below us the slope falls away in long gold terraces of larch to a bare, scrubby shoulder of land that runs east along the bottom of the ridge. I recognise the shape of it. I've seen it twice now: once carved on the floor of the map room, once yesterday from the cart track, studded with yellow-flagged stakes.

The east spur. Open ground.

There are people crossing it.

Six of them, moving fast across the scrub in single file. They're bent low, keeping to the folds of the land, and even from up here I can see that they're afraid. They move the way you move across ice. One of them is limping. Two others are carrying something between them, long and heavy and wrapped in a blanket, and I know what it is without being told, and my throat closes.

"Highwood," Mara breathes, beside me. "My mother sent them down to the city. To bring Aled home."

"Aled?"

"The one on the cart." Her voice doesn't change at all. "The hunters sold him to the bounty office in Stonebridge. My mother bought him back." A pause. "The bounty office charges more for the body than it paid for the kill. The shortest way home is across the spur."

I don't say anything. There isn't anything.

The six figures reach the bottom of the slope, where the scrub gives way to larch. It's not a dramatic line. There's no wall, no fence, nothing to mark it but a single red-daubed cairn at the edge of the trees. If I hadn't been staring at that border on the map two days ago, I wouldn't know it was there.

The first of the six steps past the cairn, onto Highwood ground.

And changes.

It isn't like anything I've imagined. It's not a slow, grinding horror, not bones cracking, not screaming. It happens the way a wave breaks, all at once: one moment a woman in a grey coat, the next a shape of grey fur twice her size, long-legged and deep-chested, shaking out its pelt like a dog stepping out of a river. The coat falls off its shoulders and lies empty in the needles.

The second one crosses. Changes. Then the third, then the limping one, who changes and stops limping, and runs a few strides up the slope and then turns back and lifts its head and sings. A short, high note. Joy, or grief, or both.

The two carrying the blanket cross last. They lay it down gently in the needles. Then they change too, and stand over it, and lower their heads.

I realise I'm crying. I don't know when I started.

"Out there," I say. My voice is a mess. "On the spur. They couldn't—"

"No." Mara is watching them. Her face is wet too. She doesn't wipe it. "We can only change on our own ground. Ground the treaty says is ours. Out there, we're just people. Slow, soft, useless people. Anyone with a crossbow can walk up and—" She stops.

I look down at the single red cairn at the edge of the trees. The only thing between a wolf and a person with a tag on her finger.

I think about my chain. About my pins and my angles and my neat columns of figures. About what Voss said in the map room, his clean hand sweeping out over the slate. *Your survey becomes the law of the Reach the moment it's carved.*

I'd thought he meant land. Acres. Hunting rights. Things you could put a price on.

He didn't. He meant this. Every pin I push into the ground decides where these people get to be what they are.

"Mara," I say.

She turns her head. Her eyes are bright and wet and furious, and they're looking right at me, the way you'd look at the knife instead of the hand holding it.

"Now you know," she says, "what you're carrying."

Then she gets up, and slings her bow over her shoulder, and goes to fetch the brass handle of the chain.


# Chapter Five

We chain Cairns Four, Five and Six in a single long day, and neither of us says a word about the spur.

It's not that we're avoiding it. It's more that it's there, between us, the way the ravine was there between Cairns One and Two: something we have to keep crossing, over and over, without looking down. Mara holds the brass handle. I hold the other end. I don't flick the chain once. When it kinks, I walk the whole length of it and untwist the links with my fingers, and if she notices she doesn't say anything, but she stops holding her right wrist so far from her body.

The ridge bends south after Cairn Five. The ground falls away on our left into long green valleys, softer than anything I've seen in the Reach, with a river threading silver through the bottom of them and smoke rising from somewhere far down where I can't see the houses.

"River ground," Mara says, when she catches me looking.

"It's pretty."

"So's a snake."

"Everything up here is pretty in a way that wants to kill me." I take my bearing on Cairn Six. "It's a theme."

We eat at Cairn Six, sitting on opposite sides of it with our backs to the stones, like two people who have been seated at the same table at a wedding and are trying to make the best of it. Mara has more venison. I have the end of the innkeeper's bread, which has gone hard enough to drive pins with.

I'm working my way through it when she says, without looking around the cairn, "Why do you count?"

I stop chewing. "What?"

"Everything. Steps. Trees. You counted the stones in the stream yesterday. I heard you." A pause. "You count under your breath when you're frightened."

I hadn't known she could hear that. I'm not sure I'd known I did it out loud.

"It helps," I say, after a moment.

"With what?"

"With the—" I wave my bread at the general mountain. "The everything. When I was little, things kept changing without anyone telling me. Where we lived. Who was in charge of what. Whether the fence at the bottom of our field was ours or someone else's." I turn the crust over in my hands. "Numbers don't do that. Forty is forty on Tuesday and forty on Friday and forty on a scree slope with a wolf watching you. You can stand on a number."

Silence, from the other side of the cairn.

"That's stupid," Mara says eventually.

"Thank you."

"No." I hear her shift against the stones. "I mean it's stupid that it works. It shouldn't. A number's not a floor." Another pause, longer. "My mother sings when she's frightened. Under her breath. The old border songs. All the verses, start to finish, even the ones about cairns we lost before I was born." A breath. "I used to think that was stupid too."

I don't say anything. I don't think she wants me to. I sit with my back against the cold slate and listen to the wind come up out of the River valley, and on the other side of the cairn, so quietly I'm not sure I'm meant to hear it, someone hums three notes and stops.

Then she stands, and the brass handle clinks, and we go on.

---

Cairn Seven is wrong.

I see it from half a mile off, before I've even set up the theodolite. Every other cairn on the line has been daubed the same tired rust-red, the ochre weathered and cracked by seven winters. Cairn Seven glows. It sits on a knuckle of rock where the ridge splits, Highwood forest on one side, River valley on the other, and the red on its capstone is so fresh and bright it looks wet.

"Mara."

"I see it."

We walk to it together. Up close it's even stranger. The ochre isn't just fresh on top. It's been painted down the sides, in long careful strokes, and in the wet-looking red someone has drawn a mark I don't recognise: three wavy lines, one above another, like water.

"That's not a survey mark," I say.

"No." Mara's voice is very flat. "It's River's."

"Why would River paint—"

"Because they want you to see it." She's not looking at the cairn. She's looking past it, down into the valley, and her hand has gone to her bow. "Because they want *me* to see it."

"Very good," says a voice behind us. "Ten points to Highwood."

I turn so fast I nearly drop the theodolite.

There are three of them. They've come up out of the valley side so quietly that I didn't hear a thing, which shouldn't surprise me by now, but does. Two are big and silent and wear long fur-trimmed coats the colour of wet bark. The third is out in front, and he's smiling.

He's maybe thirty, red-blond, with a short beard and the kind of open, sunburned face that makes you want to tell it things. His coat is better than the others', with silver buttons. He has a pack on his back and a walking stick, not a bow, and he's holding the stick loosely, as if he's never once had to hit anyone with it.

"Theo," says Mara. It isn't a greeting.

"Mara. Always a joy." He doesn't come closer. He's very careful, I notice, to stay on the valley side of the cairn. Then he turns the smile on me, and it brightens, like a lamp turned up. "And you must be the new surveyor. Theo of River. I'd shake your hand, but I suspect Highwood would shoot me for it."

"I wouldn't waste the arrow," Mara says.

"See? Charming. She's always like this." He sweeps a hand at the cairn. "Do you like it? We tidied up. It was looking very shabby."

"You're not supposed to touch the cairns," I say. "Only a sworn surveyor marks a cairn."

"Oh, we didn't *mark* it." He looks wounded. "We'd never. We just decorated. It's a border cairn, half of it's ours. We thought it deserved a bit of colour." His eyes flick to Mara, and for just a second the warmth in them goes thin as ice. "Seeing as Highwood can't be bothered to look after its own."

Mara doesn't move. But I'm standing close enough to her to feel something change: a tightening in the air, like the moment before a thunderstorm when every hair on your arms lifts at once.

"Theo," I say, loudly and brightly, "it's very nice to meet you. Is there something we can do for you? Because we've got a lot of mountain left to measure."

He laughs. It's a good laugh, warm and easy, and I hate that I like it.

"Straight to business. I like her, Mara. Where did you find her?"

"Stonebridge," says Mara. "Same place your orders come from."

That lands. I watch it land. The smile stays on Theo's face, but it stops reaching his eyes.

"I'm here," he says to me, ignoring her, "to make you an offer. Purely neighbourly. You've got, what, twenty-four cairns left? Some of them are in terrible country. Sinkholes. Sheer drops. Hunters." He shrugs. "And your escort is, let's be kind, a little *distracted* these days. Her mother's ill. The pack's nervous. Highwood isn't what it was."

"Get to it," Mara says.

"River would be honoured to take over." He spreads his hands. "Three of our best, to see you safely to Cairn Thirty-One. Proper beds, down in the valley, every few nights. Hot food. Decent wine. Not"—he glances at Mara's camp-stained coat, my needle-studded hair—"whatever this is."

I'll be honest. For one second, the words *proper beds* go into me like a hook into a fish.

Then I think of a heap of slate on a mountain, painted with three wavy lines, on a cairn that is half Highwood's.

"That's very kind," I say. "But the treaty says the surveyor's escort comes from the pack whose line is being walked. And this is the Highwood line."

"The treaty says lots of things."

"Yes. That's sort of the point of it."

Theo studies me. His eyes are very blue and very sharp, and suddenly I'm aware that the easy smile and the walking stick and the sunburned face are all a sort of costume, and under it is someone who has been doing sums in his head since before I opened my mouth.

"Fair enough," he says lightly. "Offer stands, though. Any time." He turns to Mara, and his voice drops into something almost gentle, which is somehow worse than the ice. "She's better than the last one at saying no. I'll give her that."

Mara stops breathing.

"June said yes, you know," Theo goes on, softly. "To the beds. Twice. Lovely woman. Very curious." He tilts his head. "The last time I saw her, she was walking with you, Mara. Up toward Cairn Eight. And then she wasn't walking with anybody."

The silence after that has teeth.

Mara's fingers are white on her bow. I don't think she's breathing. I don't think, for a moment, that she's entirely in her own body. There's that door again, behind her eyes, and this time it isn't opening a crack. It's straining at the hinges, and whatever's behind it is not small, and it is not calm.

"Thank you, Theo," I say. I step between them, which is a stupid thing to do, which I do anyway. I keep my eyes on his face and my voice very polite. "We'll think about your offer. Good luck with your decorating."

For a moment he just looks at me. Then the smile comes back, real or not, and he sketches a little bow.

"Surveyor."

His two silent companions turn and start back down the valley side. Theo follows, unhurried, the walking stick swinging. Ten yards down the slope, he stops and looks back over his shoulder.

"Oh," he says. "One more thing. Professional courtesy." He nods at the theodolite in my arms. "Your Registrar asked me to keep an eye on you. Lovely man. Very clean hands." He smiles. "When you get to Nine, measure twice."

Then he's gone, down among the green, and the valley swallows him.

---

I turn around.

Mara is standing exactly where she was, by the bright-red cairn. She hasn't moved. Her bow is still in her hand. She's staring down at the place where Theo disappeared, and her face is the colour of the slate.

"Mara."

Nothing.

"Mara, what did he mean? About June?"

"Nothing." It comes out rough. "He meant nothing. He wanted to see what I'd do."

"He said you were walking with her. Up toward Eight."

She turns. And the look she gives me isn't angry. It's much worse than angry. It's the look of a person who has just been told that the thing she's been trying very hard not to think about is standing right behind her.

"Measure your cairn," she says. "We're losing the light."

I want to push. Everything in me that counts and checks and corrects wants to push. But I look at her hand on the bow, at the bandage on her wrist, at the bright-red ochre drying on the cairn behind her, and I think: *not now.* Not with Theo's voice still hanging in the air like smoke.

So I set up the theodolite over Cairn Seven. I level the bubble. I take my bearing on Cairn Eight. My hands are shaking, a little, and I have to do it twice.

*When you get to Nine, measure twice.*

Behind me, I hear Mara walk to the edge of the ridge and stand there, facing the valley. She doesn't hum this time. She doesn't make any sound at all. When I glance back, she's pressed the heel of her burned hand against her mouth, hard, as if there's something in there she can't afford to let out.

I look away. I give her that.

I write the angle in my field book. And underneath it, very small, in the margin where June used to write about the wind, I write:

*Why does River know about Nine?*


# Chapter Six

Cairn Eight is the last place June Marsh was seen alive, and Mara won't go within twenty yards of it.

She doesn't say so. She doesn't have to. We chain up to it from Seven through a long morning of thin cold sunshine, and with every length the brass handle in her hand gets a little lower, and her steps get a little shorter, until on the last length before the cairn she stops dead with the chain only half-taut and won't walk on.

"Mara."

"Here's fine."

"It's twelve feet short."

"Then measure the twelve feet."

I look at her. She's staring at the cairn, which is just a cairn: a grey heap on a rocky step above a long drop, daubed with tired red, a few tufts of grass growing out of the gaps in the stones. There's nothing about it to frighten anyone. But Mara looks at it the way I'd look at the scree slope, if the scree slope had personally killed someone I loved.

I don't argue. I walk the last twelve feet myself, with the chain's end trailing behind me, and pin the length, and set up the theodolite.

There's a surveyor's mark scratched into the third stone down, the same as on every other cairn. A circle with a line through it, the date seven years ago. And under it, *J.M.*, this spring.

I've been looking at these marks for five days. I've touched every one. It's only now, crouched beside Cairn Eight with my nose almost against the stone, that I really *see* one.

The line through the circle isn't decoration. It's not a flourish. It's straight and deep and deliberate, and it points.

I turn and look back down the ridge. From here I can just see Cairn Seven, a red speck far below on its knuckle of rock. The line on the previous surveyor's mark on Seven had pointed here. I'm almost sure of it. I never thought to check.

I check now. I lay my pencil along the scratched line on Cairn Eight's stone and sight along it, out across the slope. It points northeast, out over a long gold shoulder of forest toward the next spur.

Then I open the Office copy of the line, find Cairn Nine, and read off the bearing and distance from Eight.

*Bearing: 52 degrees. Distance: 38 chains.*

I sight it with the theodolite. Fifty-two degrees puts Cairn Nine not on the far spur but in a little dip much closer in, deeper among the trees.

The line on the old mark points somewhere else.

I sit back on my heels.

"What?" says Mara, from twenty yards away.

"Nothing." It comes out too fast. "Just checking the angle."

---

We chain toward Nine.

I follow the Office bearing, because that's what the Office copy says, and that's what I'm here to walk. Thirty-eight chains: a little under half a mile. I count every length out loud, and Mara holds the handle steady, and we go down off the rocky step into the forest and along a gentle slope where the larches grow close together and the light goes the colour of weak tea.

At thirty-eight chains, almost exactly, there's a cairn.

It's not like the others. It's smaller, for a start, and much neater, the slabs squared and fitted together as if somebody took care. The ochre on its capstone is red but not wet-bright like River's paint on Seven. It's a season old, maybe. There's no lichen on the stones. There's no grass growing out of the gaps. There's a surveyor's mark scratched into the face, but only one, and it isn't June's.

It's an Office stamp: a little square with the letters *S.O.* inside, and a date from this summer.

"Cairn Nine," says Mara.

"Is it?"

She frowns at me. "The Office moved it. After the rockfall." She nods northward, vaguely, toward the far spur. "There was a notice. My mother got a letter. The ground had slipped, they said. The old cairn went down with it. They sent a man up in summer to build this one."

"A surveyor?"

"A man with a hammer." She shrugs. "We weren't invited."

I look at the neat little cairn. At the letters *S.O.* and the summer date. At the absence of lichen.

*When you get to Nine, measure twice.*

"I want to close the angle," I say.

"What does that mean?"

"It means I'm going to take a sight back to Seven and Eight from here, and see if the triangle adds up." I'm already setting up the theodolite, my hands moving faster than my thoughts. "Three angles in a triangle have to make a hundred and eighty degrees. Always. If they don't, something's in the wrong place."

"Something like what?"

"Like me," I say. "Probably. It's probably me."

It isn't me.

I take the sight to Eight. I take the sight to Seven, which I can just catch through a gap in the trees, a red speck on the far knuckle. I do the sums in my field book, carefully, twice. Then I do them a third time, because a third time is what you do when the first two scare you.

The triangle doesn't close. It's out by more than three degrees. Over these distances, three degrees isn't an error. It's a different place.

I do some more arithmetic. It's not hard arithmetic. I could do it in my sleep. I just don't want to.

If the line through the old mark on Cairn Eight is right, then Cairn Nine, the real Cairn Nine, ought to be about a quarter of a mile further out. Northeast. Toward the far spur. Toward the edge of the forest, and the open slope beyond it.

A quarter of a mile. Twenty chains.

The same distance the line bends inward on the floor of the map room.

---

"I'm going to take a look at something," I tell Mara.

"At what?"

"Just the ground. Up there." I wave vaguely northeast. "For the ravine. I want to see where the slip happened."

It's not even a lie. I do want to see where the slip happened. I want to see it very much. I want to see it because I'm almost certain I won't find it.

She looks at me for a long, measuring moment. Then she slings her bow over her shoulder and follows.

I walk on the old bearing. I don't chain it; I just pace, counting under my breath, compass in one hand. The trees thin. The ground rises gently, firm and dry under the needles. There's no slip. There's no scar, no raw earth, no tumble of fallen rock. There's just more forest, gold and quiet, and the far spur getting closer.

At about nineteen chains, I nearly fall over it.

It's lying on its side under a fallen larch, half buried in needles and moss. A heap of grey slabs that used to be a stack, spilled out across the ground like dice from a cup. Moss has grown over most of them. Lichen, yellow and grey, crusts the rest. And on the largest, wedged under the trunk of the fallen tree, the faded rust-red of very old ochre.

I crouch. I brush away moss with my sleeve.

There's a mark scratched in the stone. A circle with a line through it, seven years old. Under it, *J.M.* and this spring's date.

And beside the mark, low on the stone where the slabs meet the earth, there's a fresh, pale gouge, about the width of my thumb. The kind of gouge you get when someone drives the point of an iron bar between two stones, and leans on it until the stack goes over.

I put my thumb in it. It fits.

Behind me, needles crunch.

I stand up, fast, and turn, and Mara is right there, three paces off, looking past me at the spilled stones. I can't tell how much she's seen. I can't tell what she's thinking. Her face is very still.

"That's old," she says. "That's from before the slip."

"Yes," I say.

"So it fell in the slip."

I look at the gouge. I look at the forest around us, dry and whole and untouched, for a quarter mile in every direction. I look at Mara, whose people have lost two miles of ground in her lifetime. Who sat by a fire three nights ago and told me she didn't think the clean-handed Registrar was going to give any of it back.

And I think: *if I tell her, what does she do?*

She goes back to her mother. Highwood marches down to River's cairns, or to Stonebridge, or straight to the Survey Office. And I'm a surveyor with five days' experience and one bad triangle, and no proof of anything except a gouge in a stone that anyone could have made at any time, and the line still lapses at first snow, and the hunters come up onto the ridge, and Aled is not the last person with a tag on his finger.

Or I'm wrong. I'm wrong, and I've just started a war over a bad sum.

"Probably," I say.

It's the worst thing I've ever said. It sits in my mouth like the iron taste of the stream at the gate.

Mara looks at me. Then she looks at the stones again, for a long time. Then she turns and walks back toward the neat little Office cairn, and I follow her, and neither of us says anything at all.

---

We pin the Office cairn as Nine. I write the Office bearing in my field book, and the Office distance, and the Office everything. And then, on a fresh page at the back, the page I keep for my own working, I write the old bearing, and the number nineteen, and a small careful sketch of a cairn spilled on its side under a fallen tree.

I don't show it to anyone.

It's not the same as lying, I tell myself. A survey isn't filed until it's carved. Between now and first snow I can walk this line a dozen ways, and keep a dozen pages of working, and the only page that becomes law is the one I lay on the slate floor in Stonebridge at the end. So I'll walk it both ways. I'll keep the Office's numbers and my own, side by side, the way you keep two columns of a sum you don't trust. And when I've got enough, when I've got more than one gouge and one bad triangle, I'll decide.

*A surveyor belongs to no one,* Voss said, with his clean hands folded. *Only to the line.*

The trouble is that I'm no longer sure which line he meant. The one on the floor of his map room, or the one scratched into a fallen stone under a larch, pointing northeast.

I close the field book and put it in the bottom of my pack, under my spare socks, where nobody would think to look. Then I feel ridiculous, and leave it there anyway.

By late afternoon the sky behind the far spur has gone a strange, bruised yellow, and the wind is coming the wrong way, hard and cold out of the north. Mara keeps looking up at it. She keeps looking at me, too, when she thinks I'm busy with the theodolite. I can feel it, every time, like a draught on the back of my neck.

We're packing up the chain when she finally says it.

"You found something."

"I found a fallen cairn."

"You found something else."

"Mara—"

"You count when you're frightened," she says. Her voice is low and level and absolutely certain. "I told you that. I didn't tell you the rest." She takes the brass handle, coils the last few links around it, and holds it out to me, and her eyes are the colour of the sky behind her, gold and darkening. "You count when you lie, too."

I take the handle.

I don't answer.

Above us, somewhere over the far spur, the first long roll of thunder comes rumbling down the ridge.


# Chapter Seven

The storm catches us halfway between Nine and Ten, and it doesn't so much rain as fall on us all at once.

One moment the sky is bruised yellow. The next it's black, and the larches are roaring, and water is coming down so hard it bounces knee-high off the path. I've been rained on before. I'm from the lowlands; rain is practically our national pastime. But lowland rain is soft and grey and apologetic. This rain has a grudge. It comes in sideways on a wind that tears at my coat and my pack and the chain on my shoulder, and within a minute I'm soaked to the skin and so cold I can't feel my face.

"This way," Mara shouts, and grabs my sleeve.

It's the first time she's touched me on purpose. I don't have time to think about it. She drags me off the line, downhill, through a stand of young larches that whip at my face, and across a stream that has turned from a trickle into something with opinions. I lose my footing on a wet stone. She hauls me up by the collar like a kitten. Thunder breaks directly overhead with a crack so loud I feel it in my teeth.

And then there's a wall in front of us. A real one: dry stone, waist-high, then a low roof of turf, then a door.

Mara shoulders the door open and shoves me through it.

---

It's a bothy. One room, barely big enough to lie down in, with a beaten earth floor and a stone hearth at one end and a wide wooden shelf built along the back wall for sleeping on. The air smells of old smoke and sheep. It is, without question, the most beautiful room I've ever stood in.

"Goat herders," Mara says, dumping her bow and quiver by the door. Water streams off her braid onto the floor. "Highwood keeps goats on the high pastures in summer. They leave peat."

There is peat. There's a neat stack of it by the hearth, and a tin box on a shelf with a flint and some dry kindling inside, and another tin, smaller, that turns out to hold tea leaves. I could weep. I nearly do.

Mara gets the fire going while I stand in the middle of the floor dripping and trying to make my fingers work well enough to unbuckle my pack. They won't. They're white and stiff and shaking, and the buckle keeps sliding out of them.

"Here." She's beside me. She doesn't ask. She just reaches out, flicks the buckle open with one hand, and lifts the pack off my shoulders. Then the chain, carefully, by its leather strap, not touching any of the links. Then she looks at me, and frowns.

"Take your coat off."

"I'm fine."

"You're blue. Take your coat off."

I take my coat off. Underneath it my shirt is soaked through and clinging, and I am suddenly, extremely aware of how small the room is and how there's nowhere in it to stand that isn't within arm's reach of her. She seems to become aware of it at the same moment. She turns away, abruptly, and goes and rummages in a wooden chest under the sleeping shelf, and comes back with two coarse grey blankets that smell of goat.

She holds one out without looking at me.

"Change," she says. "I won't look."

"You're soaked too."

"I'll change after."

"That's ridiculous. You'll freeze. We'll both turn around."

A pause.

"Fine," she says.

So we stand back to back in the firelight in the tiny stone room, and peel off our wet clothes, and wrap ourselves in blankets that smell of goat, and I spend the entire time staring very hard at the wall and listening to the rustle of cloth behind me and thinking about absolutely nothing at all. Especially not about how close she is. Especially not about the sound of her unbraiding her wet hair.

---

We hang our clothes on pegs by the fire. They steam. Outside, the storm pounds on the turf roof like it wants to be let in.

Mara makes tea in a blackened pot. We drink it out of the same tin cup, passing it back and forth, because there's only one. It's stewed and bitter and gritty with leaves, and it's the best thing I've ever drunk. I tell her so.

"You said that about the venison."

"I meant it about the venison."

"You say that about everything."

"I *mean* it about everything. Everything up here is either trying to kill me or the best thing I've ever had. Sometimes both." I wrap my hands around the cup. "Mostly both."

She sits on the edge of the sleeping shelf with her blanket around her shoulders and her knees drawn up. Her hair is loose now, black and heavy and still damp, falling over her shoulders almost to her waist. I've never seen it unbraided. It makes her look younger. It makes her look like someone who might, under other circumstances, laugh.

She isn't laughing. She's looking at the fire.

"My mother is dying," she says.

I don't say anything. I've learned that much. I hold the cup and wait.

"She's had the cough two years. It's worse every winter. The healers say this one, or the next." She says it plainly, the way I'd read off a bearing. "When she dies, I'm alpha. Highwood is mine. All of it. Every cairn, every acre, every wolf." She picks at a loose thread on the blanket. "And every mile we've lost. That's mine too. Two miles in my lifetime, four in hers. I told you." She pulls the thread. It doesn't break. "She's going to die and leave me a pack that's smaller every time someone like you walks through it."

"Someone like me."

"A surveyor." She looks at me. "No offence."

"Some taken." I pass her the cup. "For what it's worth, I'm trying very hard not to be someone like me."

That earns me a look I can't read at all.

"Why are you here?" she asks. "Really. Not *the Office sent me*. You. Why did you want this?"

And I could give her the Office answer. I nearly do. It's sitting right there on my tongue, smooth and neat: *because it's an honour, because it's important work, because the Reach needs surveyors.* All true. All useless.

"I grew up on a farm," I say instead. "In the lowlands. Very flat, very small. Our field went down to a stream, and there was a fence at the bottom of it, and on the other side of the fence was our neighbour's land. He wanted the stream. He wanted it for years. And one spring, when I was nine, a surveyor came out from town and walked the boundary again, and when he'd finished the fence was on our side of the stream instead of his."

Mara's hands have stopped moving on the blanket.

"My father went to the magistrate. The magistrate looked at the survey and said the survey was the law. My father said the survey was wrong, and the magistrate said, *Do you have a surveyor who says otherwise?* And we didn't. Nobody we knew could afford one." I look at the fire. "We lost the stream. Then we lost the field, because without the water nothing would grow. Then we lost the farm. My father died two years after that. My mother says it was his heart. I think it was the fence."

The storm hammers the roof. The peat fire breathes and settles.

"I found out later the neighbour paid the surveyor," I say. "Not much. Enough. And I decided, when I was eleven, sitting in a rented room in town, that I was going to be the one with the chain. That I was going to be the one person in the room who could say *no, that's wrong, I measured it, here's the number.*" I laugh a little. It doesn't come out right. "So. That's why. I wanted to be the surveyor nobody could pay."

For a long moment Mara doesn't say anything at all.

Then she says, very quietly: "Did you find something at Nine?"

And I want to tell her. I want it so badly my chest aches. I can feel the words lining up behind my teeth: *the cairn was toppled, someone moved it, someone moved your whole border a quarter mile into your forest, and I'm almost sure.* Almost.

"I think so," I say. "I'm not sure yet. I don't want to tell you something that's wrong. Not about this. Not to you."

I wait for her to push. She doesn't. She just looks at me, steady, in the firelight, her eyes gone dark in the low light, just a glint of amber left in them.

"Then tell me when you're sure," she says. "Not before."

It's such an enormous thing to give somebody, that kind of patience, that for a second I can't speak.

---

Much later, when the fire is low and we're lying back to back on the sleeping shelf because it's the only place to lie, and the only way two people fit on it is close, she tells me about June.

She tells it to the wall. I listen to it with my eyes open in the dark.

"I was her escort. In spring. My mother sent me." Her back is warm against mine, through two layers of goat blanket. "She was easy to walk with. She talked too much. Like you." A breath. "She liked River. She liked their beds. I didn't like her liking them, and I told her so, and she laughed at me. She called me sullen. She wrote it down in her book. She showed me."

I close my eyes. *Escort sullen.*

"We got to Eight. She was strange there. Quiet. She kept looking at the marks on the stones. And then she told me to go back to the hold. She said she had something to measure on her own, and I was distracting her. I said no. She said it was her survey and her line and her decision, and if I didn't like it I could go and complain to my mother." A long silence. "So I went."

"Mara—"

"I was angry. I left her at Eight, on her own, with the light going. I walked half the night back to the hold. And in the morning they found her chain at the bottom of the ravine below Eleven." Her voice doesn't break. It just goes very, very quiet. "She sent me away, and I went. And then she was gone."

I don't think. I reach back in the dark, and find her hand where it lies on the blanket between us, and hold it.

She goes rigid. For a second I'm sure she'll pull away.

She doesn't. Her fingers stay where they are, cool and callused, under mine. After a long time they turn, just slightly, and hold back.

I don't count anything. I notice that, somewhere in the dark: that for once there's nothing in my head that needs to be lined up and numbered and made to stand still. There's just the rain, and the slow breath of the fire, and the weight of her hand, which is the only fixed point I need.

We lie like that, not speaking, while the storm wears itself out on the roof.

---

I wake in the grey before dawn to an empty shelf and a cold draught.

Mara is at the door. She's dressed again, her clothes still damp, her hair roughly braided. The door is open a crack, and she's standing with her face to it, breathing slowly, in, out.

"What is it?"

She doesn't look round.

"Smoke," she says. "On the wind. Down by the open ground past Ten."

"Goat herders?"

"Goat herders went down a month ago." She reaches for her bow. "And they don't burn their fires with pitch."

I don't know what that means. Her voice tells me enough.

"Hunters," Mara says.


# Chapter Eight

The line from Nine to Ten runs straight through a place where Mara can't be a wolf.

I find this out at the worst possible moment, which is to say with the theodolite set up and the bearing taken and the chain already uncoiled across the needles. I've been so busy with the Office numbers, and my own numbers, and the two columns of figures I'm keeping in my head like two dogs that hate each other, that I haven't looked properly at the map.

I look now. I spread the Office copy out on a flat rock and trace the line from Nine to Ten with my finger.

Halfway along it, the east spur reaches up to meet the ridge.

It's only a narrow neck. On the paper it's a little tongue of open ground poking up from the lapsed land below, no more than four or five chains across. But the line goes straight through it, because lines go straight through everything, and on the far side of it, past another stretch of Highwood forest, sits Cairn Ten.

"We can go around," I say.

"You can't." Mara is standing at the edge of the trees, looking down the slope toward the gap. Her face is very calm, which I've learned means nothing good. "You have to chain it straight. You said."

"I can offset. Measure around it, then do the trigonometry—"

"And at the end, when your Registrar asks why there's a hole in your survey right where it crosses open ground, you'll tell him what?" She turns her head. "That your escort didn't want to walk on it?"

I open my mouth. I close it.

"Five chains," she says. "That's nothing. We go fast."

---

The smoke is still on the wind. I can smell it now too, faint and bitter, under the clean wet-forest smell the storm left behind. Pitch. Like burning tar.

"Where are they?"

"Down the spur. Half a mile, maybe more." Mara tests the wind with her face. "They camped in the hollow below the old pine. They'll have heard us yesterday, in the storm. They'll know where the line crosses."

"So they're waiting."

"Maybe. Maybe they're asleep. Maybe they're drunk." She slings her bow onto her shoulder and takes the brass handle from my hand. "Maybe I'm wrong about the pitch."

She isn't wrong. I can tell from the way her shoulders are set.

We go.

The last stretch of Highwood ground before the gap is a gentle slope of larch, needles soft and gold underfoot. At the bottom of it, where the trees stop, there's a cairn, small and very old, daubed red. Beyond it, the ground changes. It's not dramatic. The grass is coarser. There are fewer trees: scrubby birches, a few wind-bent pines, brown bracken knee-high and wet from the storm. It looks like the lower slopes of any mountain anywhere.

Mara stops at the cairn.

I watch her stand there for a moment with the chain handle in her fist. She closes her eyes. Breathes in. Breathes out. And then she steps past the cairn, onto open ground, and I swear I see something go out of her, like a lamp turned down. Her shoulders drop half an inch. Her stride gets shorter. Nothing you'd notice if you didn't know.

I know.

"Go," she says, over her shoulder. "Chain."

I chain.

One. She walks, I follow, I pin, I call it. Two. The bracken soaks my trousers to the knee. Three. The smell of pitch is stronger now, close and acrid. I don't look for its source. I look at the chain, the brass, Mara's back. Four.

At four and a half, a crossbow bolt hits the ground between us.

It goes into the turf with a sound like someone snapping a carrot. It stands there quivering, black-fletched, a foot from the chain. I stare at it. I can't make my mind connect it to anything.

"Stop there," says a voice.

---

There are three of them, and they've come up out of the bracken on our left so quietly that the first thing I see is the crossbows.

Three crossbows. Loaded. One of them, I'm almost sure, is the same crossbow I saw on the towpath on my first morning in the Reach, slung on the back of a man walking beside a cart.

The one in front is a woman. Broad, weathered, fifty maybe, with grey hair cropped close to her skull and a long coat stiff with old blood. She has her crossbow levelled at Mara's chest, and she's smiling the smile of someone who has just found a coin on the road.

"Well," she says. "Look at that. Highwood, on open ground, in broad daylight. And no fur on her at all."

Mara doesn't move. She's holding the brass handle in her left hand. Her bow is on her shoulder. She'd need two seconds to get it free. She'd need two more to nock an arrow. I can see her counting, the same way I would.

The bolt in the turf is still quivering.

"I'm a sworn surveyor," I say. My voice comes out much higher than I'd like. I make it come down. "Of the Survey Office of Stonebridge. This is a sworn survey of the Highwood line under the Cairn Treaty, and she's my chain-hand. You need to lower your weapons."

The woman's eyes flick to me. She looks me up and down: the chain, the theodolite case, the mud on my knees. Her smile gets wider.

"Office girl," she says. "Funny. Office told us there'd be no trouble on this stretch."

Something cold goes down my spine.

"Who in the Office?"

"Does it matter?" She shrugs, the crossbow steady. "Ground's open, sweetheart. Says so on your own map. No law up here but the bounty, and the Guild's paying double for Highwood this season." Her eyes slide back to Mara. "Double. For the heir? I'd guess triple. Wouldn't you, lads?"

The two men behind her laugh. One of them is already moving, circling wide through the bracken toward Mara's right. Toward her bow arm.

"She's on a sworn survey," I say again. Louder. "Interfering with a sworn survey is a hanging offence. It's in the treaty. Article Four. You can read it in any ledger hall in the Reach—"

"Can't read," says the woman cheerfully. "Never could."

The circling man is ten feet from Mara now. Eight.

I don't decide to do it. It's not that kind of thing. It's the kind of thing where your body has already done it by the time your mind catches up.

I step in front of her.

I put myself squarely between Mara and the woman with the crossbow, close enough to Mara that I can feel her breathing against my shoulder blades. I hold my arms out a little from my sides. I'm shaking so hard the chain on my shoulder rattles.

"Then you'll have to shoot through me," I say. "And I'm not on any bounty list. I'm Office. I'm sworn. You put a bolt in me, and that's not a wolf on open ground. That's murder. And they'll hang you for it in Stonebridge, on the top lock, where everyone can watch."

The woman's smile slips.

"Cass," says Mara, very low, behind me. "*Move.*"

"No."

"Cass—"

The circling man lunges.

He goes for Mara's right arm, the burned wrist, his hand reaching. And the chain is still in my right hand, the other brass handle, sixty-six feet of it coiled loose over my arm. I don't think. I swing it.

It's heavy. It's the heaviest thing I've ever swung. A hundred brass links, a lifetime of my training, and every tenth one silver. It goes out in a long, hissing arc through the wet air and wraps around the man's forearm like a whip around a post, and he screams and drops to his knees in the bracken, clutching his arm.

Behind me, I hear the creak of a bowstring.

Mara has her bow up. I didn't see her do it. I don't know how she did it. It's free from her shoulder and an arrow is nocked and drawn back to her cheek, and it's pointed past my ear, straight at the woman's throat.

Everyone goes very still.

"I can't change out here," Mara says. Her voice is perfectly calm. "But I can still shoot. Ask the last hunter who came up the spur after my cousin."

The woman looks at the arrow. Then at me. Then at the man on his knees in the bracken, cradling his arm and moaning.

"It's not even silver that hit him," I say. My voice cracks. "That's just brass. Imagine what silver would do."

For a long, long moment, nobody breathes.

Then the woman lowers her crossbow. Slowly. An inch at a time.

"Get him up," she says to the third man, without taking her eyes off Mara. "We're going."

"But the heir—"

"*We're going.*"

They go. They drag the moaning man up out of the bracken between them and back away down the slope, crossbows still half-raised. At the edge of the birches, the woman stops.

"Office girl," she calls.

I don't answer.

"You'll walk your line. You'll file your pretty survey. And then the snow comes, and you go back to the lowlands." She spits into the bracken. "And we'll still be here."

Then they're gone.

---

I don't remember the last half-chain to the Highwood cairn on the far side. I know Mara gets me there. I know I'm counting, out loud and not very steadily. *Five. Five and a quarter. Five and a half.* I know that when we step back onto Highwood ground she lets out a breath like she's been holding it for a mile, and her shoulders come up again, and the lamp comes back on. Behind us, the bracken of the open ground rustles in the wind, empty, and I can't stop looking at it. Five chains. Less than a quarter of a mile. That's all it takes, out here, to turn a person into a bounty.

And then my knees go.

I sit down hard in the needles, right there by the cairn, and put my head between my knees, and shake.

Mara stands over me. I can feel her there. She doesn't say anything for a long time.

"You stood in front of a crossbow," she says eventually.

"I know."

"For me."

"I *know*, Mara."

"Don't ever," she says, and her voice does something strange, wobbles and catches and comes back hard, "*ever* do that again."

I lift my head. She's looking down at me, and her face is furious, and her eyes are wet, and her hands are clenched so tight on her bow that the knuckles have gone white. And under all of that, there's something else, something I've never seen on her before. It takes me a second to recognise it.

She's frightened. Not of the hunters. Of me. Of what I just did.

"You'd have died," I say. "Out there. You can't change."

"That's my problem."

"No. You're my chain-hand. You're on my survey. That makes it my problem." I wipe my face with my muddy sleeve. "Also, the letter said if anything happened to me you'd lose the forest. I was thinking about the forest."

She stares at me.

"You weren't," she says.

"No," I admit. "I wasn't."

For a moment neither of us says anything. Then she crouches, slowly, in the needles beside me, and reaches out, and wipes a streak of mud off my cheek with her thumb. Her hand is warm and steady and not very gentle. It's the best thing I've ever felt.

I don't say so. For once, I don't say so.

"Tomorrow," she says, standing up, "we reach Eleven."

She looks past me, north along the ridge, to where the forest drops away into a long grey gash in the mountain, steep-sided and deep and full of shadow. The ravine. Where they found June's chain.

"Get some sleep," she says. "You'll need your feet."


# Chapter Nine

Cairn Ten is easy. That's the first thing that should worry me.

We reach it by mid-morning, after a short clean stretch of chaining through open larch. It's exactly where the Office copy says it should be. The triangle closes. The surveyor's mark on the third stone down points north, straight along the ridge, toward the place where the forest falls away into nothing.

I stand beside the cairn and look along the line, and my stomach turns over slowly, like a fish in a bucket.

The ravine isn't like the one between Cairns One and Two. That was a crease in the mountain. This is a wound. It cuts across the ridge from east to west as if someone took an axe to the Reach and didn't stop: a deep, narrow, grey-walled gash, maybe a hundred feet across at the top and much, much further down. Larches cling to the rim at angles that make my knees hurt. A stream runs along the bottom, white and loud. I can hear it from here.

On the far rim, small and red against the grey, sits Cairn Eleven.

"The line crosses that," I say.

"Yes."

"Straight across."

"Yes."

"Through the air."

Mara glances at me sideways. It's a new look, one I've seen a few times since yesterday. I think it might be her version of fondness, which on anyone else's face would look like mild irritation.

"There's a path down," she says. "On this side. Then up the other. Goat herders use it." A pause. "You don't chain the drop. You take the angle across, then you measure the width with your instrument. That's what June did."

I look at her. She's not looking at me. She's looking at the ravine, at the far cairn, at the white thread of water far below, and her jaw is set so tight I can see a muscle jumping in it.

"Mara," I say. "We don't have to go down."

"You want to," she says. "You've wanted to since Stonebridge. You want to see where they found her."

It's true. I hadn't said it. I'd barely said it to myself.

"I can do the angle from here," I say. "And the width. I don't need to go down."

"You need to see it." She shrugs her bow higher on her shoulder. "So do I."

---

I take the angle from Cairn Ten. I take the width across with the theodolite, twice, and write it down. Then we go down.

The path is a goat path, which is to say it isn't really a path at all. It's a series of suggestions, scratched into the side of the ravine by animals that have four feet and no fear. It goes down in steep, tight zigzags, sometimes over bare rock, sometimes over loose slate. I go down it on my backside more than once. Mara doesn't comment. She goes ahead of me, and every time I slip she stops, and waits, and doesn't look back. It takes me a while to understand that she's waiting where she can catch me if I fall.

I don't fall. Not quite.

The bottom of the ravine is a different world. The light is grey and dim and cold, the sky a long narrow ribbon very far above. The air is wet with spray from the stream, which roars and boils over boulders the size of carts. Everything is slate: the walls, the stones, the shingle we walk on. It smells of cold water and wet rock, and under that, faint and metallic, the iron smell of Highwood ground. Moss grows on everything. Ferns uncurl from cracks in the cliff. It's beautiful in the way a cathedral is beautiful, which is to say I feel very small and very much like I shouldn't be here.

"Where?" I ask. I have to raise my voice over the water.

Mara points.

Fifty yards upstream, the ravine wall on our right is scarred. A long fan of broken rock spills out from it into the stream bed: pale, sharp-edged, newer than the moss-furred stones around it. Above the fan, high up the wall, there's a raw grey bite out of the cliff where a slab has sheared away.

So the rockfall was real. Something did come down.

I walk toward it, slowly. I'm counting my steps. I don't stop myself.

The fan of rock is big, but not as big as I'd thought. The report said *buried under the fall*. I'd pictured half the mountain. This is a heap of broken slabs and shards that comes up to my shoulder, spread across maybe forty feet of stream bed. A person could be under it. A person could just as easily not.

At the downstream edge of it, someone has built a cairn.

It's tiny. Knee-high. Seven flat stones stacked carefully one on top of another, each a little smaller than the one beneath. There's no ochre on it. There's no surveyor's mark. On the top stone, someone has laid a single larch twig, gold needles gone brown and dry.

I look at Mara.

She's standing a few paces behind me, very still. She doesn't look at the little cairn. She looks at the stream.

"I came back," she says. "After. When they'd taken her chain away. I couldn't find her. I looked for two days. I moved the stones I could move." She lifts one hand, the burned one, and lets it fall. "So I built that."

I crouch beside the tiny cairn. I don't touch it. I look at the seven stones and the dry gold twig, and something in my chest pulls tight and hot and stays there.

"It's beautiful," I say. And then, because I can't help it: "It's very well built."

A sound comes out of her that might, in another world, be a laugh.

---

I'm looking at the rockfall, properly now, the way I look at everything. The way I looked at the toppled cairn at Nine, at the fresh gouge in the stone. I'm looking for the number that doesn't fit.

The slabs at the top of the fan are mossy. The ones at the bottom aren't. That makes sense: the newest fall would be on top. Except it's the other way round. The fresh, pale stones are underneath. The mossy ones sit on top of them, as if someone had taken old stones from the stream bed and piled them over the new ones.

To hide them? To make the fall look older, or bigger, or heavier?

"Mara," I say. "Come and look at—"

Something moves on the rim.

I see it out of the corner of my eye, high up, against the narrow ribbon of sky. A shape. A head, maybe, or a shoulder, there and gone, at the edge of the cliff directly above the raw grey scar. It might be a deer. It might be a goat. It might be a cloud.

Then I hear it. Over the roar of the stream, a sound I'll hear in my sleep for the rest of my life: a deep, grinding crack, like the mountain clearing its throat.

"*Cass.*"

Mara hits me from the side.

She doesn't push me. She *hits* me: full body, shoulder into my ribs, like a wolf taking down a deer. I go sideways off my feet and into the air, and we both slam down into the shingle at the foot of the cliff, tight against the base of the wall where it leans out a little over the stream. Her arm is around my head. Her body is over mine.

Then the world falls on us.

It's not like rain. It's not like anything. It's noise so huge it isn't noise any more. It's pressure, a fist the size of the ravine slamming down on the stream bed again and again. Stone hits stone and explodes. Shards whip past. Something huge hits the shingle a foot from my face and bounces and is gone. I can't breathe. There's dust in my mouth and my eyes and my nose, grey slate dust, and Mara's weight pressing me down into the stones, and her heart slamming against my back, hard and fast, and I can feel it, I can feel every beat of it—

Something sharp goes across my palm.

I've flung my hand out without meaning to, to brace myself or to grab something, and a shard of slate as thin and hard as a knife skids across the shingle and opens my left palm from the base of my thumb to my little finger. It doesn't hurt. Not yet. It's just hot, very hot, and wet.

The same shard, still moving, hits Mara's forearm where it's wrapped around my head. I hear her hiss. I feel her flinch.

And her hand, her right hand, burned and bandaged, closes over mine in the dust. Her cut forearm presses against my cut palm. Her fingers lock around my fingers and hold on.

The rocks stop falling.

---

The silence afterwards is louder than the noise was.

I lie in the shingle with Mara on top of me and stone dust settling over us both like grey snow. The stream is still roaring, somewhere, but it sounds very far away. My ears are ringing. My palm is starting to hurt now: a deep, pulsing, burning hurt, in time with my heart.

No. Not in time with my heart.

In time with *hers*.

I can feel it. Not her heart against my back; something else, deeper, inside me, as if a second pulse has started up under my own. It's faster than mine and stronger, and it's coming from somewhere just to my right, just behind me, and when I think about it I know, with absolute certainty, the way I know that forty is forty, exactly where she is. Not because I can feel her weight. Because something in me is pointing at her, steady and true, like a compass needle swinging round to north and stopping there.

And under the hot ache in my palm there's another ache, a sharper one, in my right forearm. In exactly the place where the shard hit Mara.

I don't have a cut on my right forearm.

I can feel one anyway.

And there's a smell. Through the stone dust, through the cold wet reek of the stream, I can smell *her*: woodsmoke and larch resin and something under them, warm and wild and particular, that I've been breathing for a week without noticing. I notice it now. It fills my head. It's suddenly the only thing in the ravine that makes sense, as if every other smell in the world has been tuned slightly wrong my whole life and this is the note they were all meant to match.

"Mara," I say. My voice is a croak. "Mara, something's—"

She rolls off me.

She does it fast, faster than I've ever seen her move, and she's on her knees in the shingle with her back to the cliff, staring at me. Her face is grey with dust. There's blood on her arm, running down over the bandage on her wrist. There's blood on my hand. There's blood on both of us, mixed together, a long red smear across my palm and her forearm where they were pressed together.

She looks at the blood. Then she looks at me.

I've seen Mara angry. I've seen her afraid, at the edge of the open ground, with crossbows on her. I've seen her grieving, by the little cairn of seven stones.

I've never seen her look like this.

"Did you feel it?" she whispers.

I don't have to ask what she means. I can feel it right now. The second pulse. The compass needle. The ache in an arm I didn't hurt.

"Yes," I say.

She closes her eyes. Something goes across her face that I can't name: horror, or grief, or something so far beyond both that it doesn't have a word yet.

"No," she says. Very quietly. To nobody. To the mountain. "No, no, no. Not you. Not *now*."

High above us, on the rim of the ravine, nothing moves at all.


# Chapter Ten

Mara binds my hand at the bottom of the ravine, kneeling in the shingle, and every time she touches the cut, she flinches.

Not me. Her.

I watch it happen. She wets a strip of cloth in the stream and lays it across my palm, and the cold goes into the wound like a blade, and I hiss. And at exactly the same moment, Mara's own hand jerks back, fingers curling, as if she's touched a hot stove. She stares at her own palm. There's nothing on it.

"You felt that," I say.

She doesn't answer. She picks up the cloth again and finishes binding my hand, fast and tight, her jaw clenched the whole time. When she's done she sits back on her heels, and I reach for her arm, the one the shard opened, where blood is still running in a thin dark line down toward her bandaged wrist.

"Let me."

"No."

"You're bleeding."

"It'll stop."

"Mara, I can *feel* it." I hold up my right arm. There's not a mark on it. Under the skin, in exactly the place where hers is cut, there's a hot, narrow ache, as clear as if I'd been sliced myself. "Right here. Tell me I'm wrong."

She looks at my arm. Then at her own.

Then she gets up and walks away down the stream bed without a word, and kneels at the water's edge with her back to me, and washes the cut herself.

I sit in the shingle with my bound hand in my lap and watch her go.

And here is the thing, the thing I can't stop noticing, the thing that sits in the back of my mind like a stone in a boot. I don't need to watch her go. I could close my eyes. I could turn around and face the cliff. I would still know exactly where she is. Fifteen paces downstream, a little to the left, crouched low. I can feel it the way I can feel which way is down. She's just *there*, a fixed point, the place every other part of the world now seems to arrange itself around.

I close my eyes to test it.

Fifteen paces. A little to the left. Moving now, standing, turning back.

I open my eyes. She's standing, turning back.

My hands are shaking. I put them between my knees so she won't see.

---

We climb out of the ravine by the goat path on the far side, toward Cairn Eleven. It's worse going up than coming down. My hand throbs with every handhold, and every time it throbs, I see Mara's shoulders tighten ahead of me on the path. I try to use my left hand less. It doesn't help much.

At the top, she doesn't stop at the cairn. She goes straight past it, along the rim, to the place above the scar where I saw something move. She crouches there for a long time. I wait by Cairn Eleven and set up the theodolite, because it's something to do with my hands, and I don't ask what she's looking at.

I don't have to. I can feel her go still, a long way off, the way a compass needle stops trembling.

While she's gone, I look at the marks on Cairn Eleven.

They're where they always are: third stone down, facing the line. The circle with its line through it, seven years old. Beneath it, June's slanted *J.M.* and this spring's date. So she got this far. She crossed the ravine, alive, and climbed up here and marked this cairn, which means the report is wrong about that too, or at least very carefully vague. *Rockfall in the ravine below Cairn Eleven, during a survey walk.* Not on the way here. After.

I lean closer. The wind has dried the slate dust on my face into a crust, and my eyes are sore, and at first I think I'm imagining it.

I'm not. There's something else scratched into the stone, very small, tucked into the angle where the J of June's initials curls down. Two figures. So faint and fine that anyone who wasn't nose to nose with the cairn would take them for lichen.

*13.*

I sit back on my heels and look at it for a long time. Then I look north along the ridge, toward Twelve, and beyond it, out of sight, Thirteen.

A surveyor doesn't scratch numbers on cairns for no reason. A surveyor who writes notes about the wind in her margins, who loses a page from her file, who sends her escort away so she can measure something alone, definitely doesn't.

I copy it into my field book, at the back, on the private page, beside my sketch of the toppled Nine. I don't know what it means yet.

I know it means something.

When she comes back, she's carrying something: a long iron bar, nearly as tall as she is, flattened to a wedge at one end. A pry bar. Its tip is scraped bright.

She drops it at my feet. It rings on the stone.

"It was jammed in a crack," she says. "Up on the edge. Right over the slab that came down." She wipes her hands on her coat as if the iron dirtied them. "There are boot marks. One person. Heavy. They went off north, along the rim, and down the other side."

I look at the bar. I think about a fresh pale gouge in a toppled cairn at Nine, exactly the width of my thumb. I think about it very carefully.

"River?" I ask.

"No." She shakes her head. "Human. Not hunters, either. No pitch." Her nostrils flare, once, as if the smell is still in them. "Lamp oil. And paper."

"Paper doesn't smell."

"It does to me."

Lamp oil and paper. Ledgers. A long corridor lined with them, floor to ceiling, going back a hundred years.

"Someone just tried to kill us," I say. It comes out very calm. I don't feel calm. "Someone stood up there with that thing and waited until we were underneath, and tried to kill us. Like they killed June."

Mara looks at me. Her face is grey with dust and set like the slate.

"Yes," she says.

"Why?"

"You know why. Or you're close to it." Her eyes narrow, just slightly. "You've been close to it since Nine."

I have nothing to say to that. So I bend and pick up the pry bar, which is heavy and cold and still faintly gritty with rock dust, and I lash it to the side of my pack. It's evidence. It's the first real thing I've got.

Mara watches me do it. She doesn't help. She stands three paces away, then four, then five, as if every inch closer to me costs her something.

---

That night she makes camp a long way from me.

She doesn't say she's doing it. She just builds the fire in the lee of a boulder near Cairn Eleven, sets out my bedroll beside it, and then takes her own blanket and her bow and goes and sits on the far side of a stand of larches, thirty yards off, with her back to me.

I eat my supper alone. The venison is gone; it's oatcake and the last of the cheese. I sit by the fire with my bound hand in my lap, and I can feel her out there in the dark, a fixed point thirty yards away, as clearly as if she were sitting next to me. Clearer, maybe. I can feel her heart beating. I can feel the ache in her forearm. I can feel the deep, slow, grinding burn of the silver in her wrist, which I didn't know was still hurting that much, and which I feel now under my own skin.

She's been carrying that for four days and never said a word.

I think about what she said at the bottom of the ravine. *Not you. Not now.* And the look on her face. Horror. That's the only word for it.

Whatever this is, she knows what it is. And she's horrified that it's me.

It shouldn't hurt as much as it does. I tell myself that firmly, staring into the fire. I've known her a week. A week, and most of that she's spent looking at me as if I were a particularly irritating kind of insect. She doesn't owe me anything. She saved my life today. She threw herself on top of me under a falling mountain. That's more than enough. That's more than anybody has ever done for me.

It still hurts.

I lie down by the fire and pull my blanket up and close my eyes, and I try very hard not to think about the fact that thirty yards away, Mara is awake too, and her heart is going much too fast, and I can feel every beat.

---

I don't know what wakes me.

It's late. The fire is down to embers. The sky through the larches is clear and black and full of stars, and there's a moon, a fat lopsided moon a few nights off full, standing high over the far ridge and laying silver over everything. The cairn, the boulder, the needles on the ground.

Something hurts.

Not my hand. Somewhere else. Somewhere outside me and inside me at the same time: a sharp, raw, twisting ache, high in my chest, like grief. It's not mine. I know it's not mine. I haven't got anything to grieve about, not right now, not at this exact second.

Thirty yards away, behind the larches, it gets worse.

I'm on my feet before I've decided to be. I don't need a lamp. I don't need the moon. I just walk toward the ache, the way you'd walk toward a fire in the dark because you can feel the heat on your face. Through the larches. Over the soft needles. Out the other side, onto a bare shoulder of rock above the ravine, where the moonlight is so bright it throws shadows.

Mara is there.

She's sitting on the rock with her knees drawn up and her arms wrapped around them, and her face pressed into her knees. Her braid has come half undone. Her shoulders are shaking.

She's crying. Silently, completely, the way you only cry when you think nobody can hear.

"Mara," I say.

She goes rigid. Her head comes up.

"Go back."

"No."

"*Cass*. Go back to the fire."

"I can't." I take another step, out of the shadow of the trees, into the full silver light. "I can feel it. Whatever you're feeling. It woke me up. I can feel exactly where you are, all the time, and I can feel your arm and your wrist and your—" My voice catches. "And I can feel *this*. Whatever this is. It hurts. It hurts so much, Mara, and I don't even know what it's about."

She turns her face toward me.

The moon is full on it. On her wet cheeks, her set mouth, her eyes.

Her eyes.

They're amber, the way they always are. Except they're not. Around the edge of each iris, where the amber meets the white, there's a ring. A thin, perfect, shining circle of gold, bright as a new coin, catching the moonlight and throwing it back. It wasn't there this morning. It wasn't there an hour ago by the fire. I'd swear to it. I've spent a week looking at her eyes, whether or not I wanted to admit it, and I would have seen that.

"Your eyes," I whisper.

She doesn't answer. She's staring at me. At *my* face. And her own face is doing something I've never seen it do: crumpling, coming apart, all at once.

"Yours too," she says.

I put my hand up to my face, as if I could feel it. As if a ring of gold were something you could touch.

"What is it?" My voice is very small. "Mara. What's happening to us?"

She closes her eyes. The gold disappears. When she opens them again it's still there, shining, in the light of a moon that's four nights off full.

"It's called the tether," she says. "And it's going to get one of us killed."


# Chapter Eleven

"Sit down," Mara says. "Before you fall down."

I sit. Not next to her: she hasn't asked for that, and I'm not brave enough to do it unasked. I sit a few feet off on the cold rock, in the moonlight, with the ravine breathing below us and my bound hand cradled in my lap. My legs are shaking. So is everything else.

She wipes her face with the heel of her hand, roughly, like she's angry at it. Then she takes a long breath and looks out over the drop.

"There are songs," she says. "Old ones. Older than the treaty. My mother knows all of them. She used to sing them to me when I couldn't sleep." Her voice is low and flat and careful, as if she's carrying something heavy across a stream. "Most of them are about borders. Who held what. Who lost it. But some of them are about the tether."

"What is it?"

"A bond." She doesn't look at me. "Between two people. It doesn't happen often. Once in a generation, maybe, in a whole pack. My grandmother had it. With my grandfather. My mother didn't." She pauses. "It catches through scent and blood. Both at once. You have to be close enough to breathe each other in, and your blood has to meet. A shared wound. That's all it takes. One." Her hand closes, briefly, over the fresh cut on her forearm. "And then it's done."

I think of the ravine. Her weight over me. Her heart against my back. Her forearm pressed against my open palm, the blood running together, and her smell filling my head like a song.

"Done," I say. "What does done mean?"

"It means it doesn't break."

"Ever?"

"Not while you're both alive."

The moon is very bright. I can see every needle on the larches behind us, every grain in the rock under my hand. I look down at my bandaged palm, and I feel, under the throb of my own cut, the deeper ache of hers. And under that the silver burn in her wrist, slow and grinding. And under that, a long way down, the twist of grief that woke me up.

"The pain," I say. "Your arm. I feel it."

"Yes."

"And you feel mine."

"Yes. Everything. If you're hurt, I'm hurt. If you're dying—" She stops. Starts again. "The songs say a lot of the tethered don't outlive each other by long."

"And the—" I touch the space between my eyes, as if I could point at the compass needle there. "I know where you are. All the time. I could find you with my eyes shut."

"That's the other part. You'll always know which way I am. However far." For the first time, she turns her head and looks at me. The gold ring in her eyes catches the moon. "My grandmother used to say it was so you'd never lose each other in the dark."

It's such a lovely thing to say, and she says it in a voice like a funeral, and for a moment neither of us can speak at all.

"And this?" I say eventually. I wave a hand at her face. At mine.

"The tell." She looks away again. "It's in the blood. It only shows under the moon. Indoors, in daylight, nobody can see it. You can hide it for weeks if you're careful." A pause. "Except at full moon. At full moon, it shines like a lamp. Anyone who looks at you will know."

I count. I can't help it. *Four nights.*

"Mara," I say slowly, "this was supposed to only happen between wolves."

"Yes."

"The songs. Your grandmother. Wolves."

"Yes."

"So how—"

"*I don't know.*" It comes out of her like a crack in ice. "I don't know, Cass. It's not supposed to be able to happen. Humans don't catch the tether. Everybody knows that. It's in every song. Your *treaty* knows that. That's why—" She stops dead.

And I understand. All at once. The way you suddenly understand a sum you've been staring at for an hour.

*No wolf of any pack shall bond, marry, or lie with a sworn surveyor of the Reach.*

Bond.

Not *love*. Not *court* or *kiss* or *share a blanket in a goat herders' bothy*. Bond. The first verb in the clause. The one I'd read past a hundred times because everybody knows humans can't, because it's just an old word in an old law, a formality, like Voss said, with his clean hands, smiling.

*Clause Nine has never once been invoked, Miss Reed.*

"Clause Nine," I say.

Mara closes her eyes.

"The wolf shall be put to death." My voice sounds very far away. "That's you. They'd kill you."

"Yes."

"And the surveyor shall lose the hand that holds the chain." I look down at my right hand. My good hand. The one I write with. The one I hold the chain with. "And every survey she has signed shall be void."

Every survey. Not just the ones I'll sign in future. Every survey I've ever signed. And the only real survey I've ever done is the one I'm doing right now.

"If anybody finds out," I say, "the Highwood line is void. It lapses. You lose the forest. All of it."

Mara doesn't open her eyes.

"And they kill you," I say again, because it's the part I can't make go into my head. "For something we didn't even *do*."

"Clause Nine doesn't care what we did." She sounds so tired. "It doesn't say *chose*. It says *bond*. We're bonded. That's all they'd need."

---

For a long time neither of us says anything at all.

The moon moves a little way across the sky. Down in the ravine, the stream keeps up its endless roaring argument with the stones. I sit on the cold rock and feel the second heartbeat under my own, steady now, slower, and I think: *I could find her with my eyes shut. Anywhere. Forever.*

And it should terrify me. It does terrify me.

It also doesn't feel like a cage. That's the strange thing. That's the part I can't make sense of, the number that won't fit. It feels like the moment in the map room when I stepped onto the slate floor in my socks and looked down and saw the whole Reach laid out under my feet, every ridge and every river. Like finding out where I am.

"At the bottom of the ravine," I say. "You said *not you*."

She flinches. I feel it, a small hot jolt behind my own ribs.

"I thought," I say carefully, "that you meant you'd rather it was anyone else. Which is fine. I'd understand. I'm a surveyor, and you've got no reason to—"

"That's not what I meant."

I wait.

She draws in a breath, and lets it out, and when she speaks her voice is so quiet I can barely hear it over the water.

"I meant not someone they can take your hand for." She's looking down at her own hands, the burned one and the other. "Not someone who's going to walk back down to Stonebridge at first snow and stand in front of your clean-handed Registrar with gold in her eyes. Not the one person in the Reach who can't possibly keep it." A breath. "Not you. Because if it had to be anybody, I'd have—"

She stops. She doesn't finish.

She doesn't have to. I can feel the end of the sentence anyway, through the tether, warm and bright and frightened, like a lamp someone has tried and failed to cover with their hand.

My face goes hot. I'm very glad it's dark. Then I remember it isn't, really, and that she can probably feel that too.

---

"So what do we do?" I ask, much later.

Mara has stopped crying. She's sitting with her bow across her knees again, the way she does when she's thinking. In the moonlight her face is all edges.

"We hide it," she says. "We finish the survey. We file it, and the line holds, and Highwood keeps the forest." She glances at me. "And then you go back to the lowlands, and you never come back to the Reach, and nobody ever knows."

"The tether doesn't break."

"I know."

"So I'd be in the lowlands, and you'd be up here, and I'd still—"

"I *know*, Cass." She shuts her eyes, briefly. "I didn't say it was a good plan. I said it's what we do."

I think about it. I think about four nights from now, and the full moon, and a gold ring shining like a lamp in my eyes. About twenty more cairns. About someone on the rim of a ravine with a pry bar, smelling of lamp oil and paper.

"Full moon," I say. "Four nights."

"We'll be at the hold by then. I'll have to go to the hold anyway. At full moon on our own ground, we turn. All of us. We can't stop it." She looks at me. "You stay inside. With the doors shut. Nobody sees you under the moon. Nobody looks at your eyes."

"And you?"

"I'll be a wolf," she says. "Nobody looks at a wolf's eyes."

"I would."

That gets a sound out of her. Not quite a laugh. Close.

"What about your mother?" I ask. "Do we tell her?"

The almost-laugh goes out of her face.

"No," she says. "Not unless we have to." She turns the bow over in her hands. "She grew up with my grandmother and grandfather. She watched them her whole life. Every time one of them stubbed a toe, the other one swore. Every time he went hunting, my grandmother would stand at the door of the hold and look at the exact spot on the ridge where he was, before anyone could see him." A breath. "If she sees us together for more than an hour, she'll know. She won't need the moon."

"So we stay apart."

"We stay apart."

"At the hold. Where we'll be sleeping under the same roof, eating at the same table, with your entire pack watching the surveyor."

"Yes."

"That's a terrible plan too."

"I know," Mara says. "I'm having a bad night for plans."

"Strictly business," she says. "From here to Thirty-One. You measure. I hold the chain. Nobody touches anybody. Nobody—" She makes a vague, frustrated gesture at the space between us. "Nobody does anything. And maybe, if it doesn't get fed, it gets quieter."

"Does it?"

"I don't know. None of the songs say."

I hold out my left hand. My cut one, bandaged and aching.

"Strictly business," I say.

She looks at my hand. Then she reaches out and shakes it.

It's a terrible idea. I know it as soon as our palms meet. The tether goes through me like the first warm mouthful of tea in the bothy, all the way from my fingers to the back of my neck. Her hand tightens on mine, just for a second, and I feel her feel it too.

We both let go at the same time. Fast.

"Strictly business," Mara says again, rather hoarsely.

"Absolutely," I say. "Very strict. The strictest."

She stands. She picks up her bow. She looks down at me in the moonlight, and her eyes are shining gold at the edges, and there's something in her face that I've never seen there before: a kind of cracked-open softness she doesn't seem to know what to do with.

"Cass," she says. "There's something else."

"What?"

She hesitates. Then: "June asked me about it. The tether. In spring, on the line, about a week before she sent me away." Her voice drops. "She asked me whether a human could ever catch it."

The ravine roars below us.

"What did you tell her?" I whisper.

"I told her no." Mara looks out over the drop, toward the dark north, where the line runs on toward Twelve and Thirteen and the rest of the mountain. "I told her it was impossible."


# Chapter Twelve

Strictly business lasts until roughly nine o'clock the next morning.

It starts well. I'll give us that. We wake on opposite sides of the larches, and we eat on opposite sides of the cold fire, and we don't look at each other while we pack. When I take the theodolite out at Cairn Eleven to sight Twelve, Mara goes and stands at the far end of the chain, sixty-six feet away, and holds the brass handle at exactly the right height without being asked, and doesn't say a word.

Sixty-six feet. I find myself thinking about it as I take the bearing. It's the length of the chain. It's the closest we're allowed to be, and the furthest we can get, and still do the job.

"Chaining," I call.

"Fine," she calls back.

We chain. One. Two. Three. I pin and call the lengths, and she walks on, and the chain clinks over the needles between us. It's all perfectly professional. It's all perfectly, absolutely fine.

At length seven, I stub my toe on a root.

It's not a bad stub. I've stubbed my toes on every root in the Reach by now, or at least it feels that way. I hop once and say a word my mother would wash my mouth out for.

Sixty-six feet away, Mara says the same word, at the same moment, and hops.

We both freeze.

Very slowly, she turns around. She looks at me. I look at her. She's holding one foot off the ground with an expression of profound betrayal.

"Sorry," I say.

"Watch," she says through her teeth, "where you are *walking*."

"I *was*. The root moved."

"Roots don't move."

"This one did. It was a very aggressive root."

She puts her foot down, glares at me across the length of the chain, and turns back around. Her shoulders are stiff with offence. I can feel the throb in her toe, perfectly in time with mine, and something about it is so ridiculous that I have to bite the inside of my cheek very hard to stop myself laughing.

She feels that too. I know she does, because her shoulders get even stiffer.

---

By mid-morning it's worse.

It's not the pain. The pain I could manage. A stubbed toe here, a scraped knuckle there, the deep slow burn of the silver in her wrist that's always there now, under everything, like a low note under a song. Those are just facts. You can stand on facts.

It's the knowing where she is.

I can't stop. Every time she's in front of me, I know it. Every time she goes ahead to scout the next stretch of line, and I lose sight of her among the larches, the compass needle inside me swings round after her and points and won't come back. I take a bearing on Cairn Twelve and find myself looking, not at the red speck in the eyepiece, but at a stand of trees thirty degrees to the left where I know, *know*, that she's crouched by a stream filling her water bottle.

I write down the wrong angle. I have to cross it out.

I never write down the wrong angle.

"You're distracted," Mara says, when she comes back.

"I'm not."

"You've been staring at the same tree for a minute."

"It's a very interesting tree."

"You're staring at it," she says, "because I was behind it."

There's no answer to that. I don't try to make one. She looks at me with an expression I can't quite read, somewhere between exasperation and something much softer, and then she makes a small frustrated sound and turns away.

"It's not just you," she says, to the next cairn. "I keep looking back. At the theodolite. At *you*. Every time you breathe in sharp I think you've fallen." She rubs her burned wrist with the heel of her other hand. "When I drew my bow this morning, at a hare, you winced. From twenty yards. I felt you wince. I missed the hare."

"I'm sorry."

"Stop saying sorry. You didn't do it."

"Neither did you."

She turns her head and looks at me then. The daylight is clear and cold and ordinary, and there's no gold in her eyes, only amber. You'd never know. You'd never in a hundred years know.

"No," she says quietly. "Neither of us did it."

Something in the way she says it makes my chest hurt, and this time it's mine.

---

The climb to Twelve goes over a long rib of bare rock, and halfway up it, the rib narrows to a ledge with a drop on one side that I decide, firmly, not to have an opinion about.

I make it most of the way. Then a flake of slate comes loose under my boot, and my weight goes sideways, toward the drop, and my stomach goes with it.

Mara's hand closes on my wrist.

She's sixty-six feet ahead. She was sixty-six feet ahead. I don't know how she's here. I don't think she knows either; she's staring at her own hand around my wrist as if it belongs to somebody else. She must have felt it, the lurch, the moment my foot went, and moved before she'd decided to.

She hauls me back onto the ledge. I sit down hard against the rock. My heart is going like a drum, and so is hers, I can feel both of them, two drums out of step and then, slowly, settling into the same beat.

She doesn't let go of my wrist.

"Strictly business," I say, a little breathlessly.

"Shut up," says Mara.

Her thumb is on the inside of my wrist, right over the pulse. I don't think she knows it's there. She's looking at the drop. Then at me. Then at her thumb, and I watch her realise, and I feel it go through her like a flinch and a flame at the same time.

She lets go. She stands up. She walks back up the ledge and picks up the brass handle where she dropped it, and stands at the far end of the chain again, sixty-six feet away, exactly where she's supposed to be.

I sit against the rock and rub my wrist, where her thumb was.

"It's not working," I say, quietly, mostly to myself. "Whatever we're doing. It isn't working at all."

Sixty-six feet away, without turning around, Mara says, "I know." Of course she heard. Wolf ears. I should have known that, too.

---

We reach Twelve at noon, and Thirteen in the middle of the afternoon, after a long climb up a shoulder of the ridge where the larches thin out and the wind comes hard and cold off the north.

Thirteen is a big cairn: old, broad-based, much bigger than the others, built on a flat ledge of bare rock with a view so vast it makes my stomach drop. The whole Reach is spread out below. The gold forest pours down into River's green valleys. The grey gash of the ravine cuts back south behind us. And far off, at the bottom of everything, the smoky smudge of Stonebridge sits in its gorge like something at the bottom of a well.

I don't look at the view. I look at the cairn.

*13.*

"What are you doing?" Mara asks.

I'm crouched at the base of it, running my good hand over the stones. "June scratched a number on Eleven," I say. "Tiny. Next to her initials. Thirteen."

Mara's head comes up.

"I didn't tell you," I say. "Yesterday. I'm sorry. I was going to. Then the ravine happened, and—" I don't finish. "But a surveyor doesn't scratch a number on a cairn for no reason. She meant someone to come here. She meant *the next surveyor* to come here."

Mara doesn't answer. But she crouches down beside me, at the other end of the cairn, and starts running her hands over the stones too.

We go round it twice. The marks on the third stone are where they should be, the old circle and June's initials. Nothing else. No numbers. No arrows. No more scratches. Just grey slate, cold and rough and furred with lichen in the cracks.

I sit back on my heels, frustrated. "Maybe I'm wrong. Maybe it meant something else."

"No," Mara says.

She's at the north side of the cairn, the side facing away from the line, the side nobody would ever look at. She has her face close to the stones, and her nostrils are flared.

"Here," she says. "It smells different."

"Stones don't smell different."

She gives me a look.

"Right," I say. "Sorry. Paper. Go on."

"Not paper. Wax. And—" She inhales again, slowly. Something changes in her face. "Her. It smells like her. June."

I get up and go round the cairn to her, and kneel at her shoulder, and I'm close enough now that the tether hums under my skin like a plucked string. I ignore it. I look at where she's looking.

At the very bottom of the cairn, where the lowest course of slabs sits on the bare rock of the ledge, there's a stone that doesn't quite match. It's the same grey, the same lichen. But the lichen on it has been disturbed: scraped away in a thin line along one edge, then pressed back down, not quite carefully enough.

I get my fingers into the gap. My bad hand screams. I use it anyway.

The stone shifts. It's heavy, but not as heavy as it looks, and after a moment of grunting and swearing and Mara swearing in sympathy, it slides out of the cairn and thumps down onto the ledge.

There's a hollow behind it. A small dry space in the heart of the stack, just big enough for two hands.

And in it, wrapped in oilcloth and sealed along every seam with dark red wax, is a package about the size of a book.

---

I don't open it straight away.

I sit on the ledge with it in my lap, and my hands are shaking, and I'm not counting, for once. I'm not doing anything. Just holding it. It's heavier than I expected. The wax seal on the top is stamped with a little slanted *J.M.*, the same as on the cairns.

Mara is sitting beside me. Not sixty-six feet away. Right beside me, close enough that our shoulders almost touch. She doesn't seem to have noticed. I'm not going to point it out.

"Open it," she says.

I break the seal with my thumbnail. I unfold the oilcloth, layer by layer.

Inside is a field book. Not an Office one, with the stamped cover and the numbered pages. A small, battered, leather-bound notebook, the kind you'd buy in any stationer's in the lowlands. Its cover is soft with use. The edges of the pages are grey from thumbs. When I open it, the first page is covered in June's small, slanted, precise hand: bearings, distances, sketches of cairns, all in neat columns, exactly the way I'd do it. Exactly the way I *am* doing it, at the back of my own field book, under my spare socks.

Tucked inside the front cover there's a folded sheet of paper. Office paper: I'd know that grain anywhere. Surveyors on long lines send their field pages down to the Office by runner every few cairns, and keep a duplicate for themselves. This is June's duplicate. It's in her hand, and in the top corner, where the clerks number the pages, she's written the number herself.

*41. Duplicate.*

Mara draws in a sharp breath beside me.

And folded inside that, on a scrap torn from the back of the leather notebook, there's a note. Written fast, the slant leaning harder than usual, the ink blotted in places as if her hand was not quite steady.

I read the first line, and the wind on the ledge goes cold right through me.

*To the next surveyor. If you've found this, they've already tried to kill you.*


# Chapter Thirteen

I read June's note out loud, because Mara asks me to, and because I don't think I could read it silently and stay sitting up.

*To the next surveyor. If you've found this, they've already tried to kill you. I'm sorry. I didn't find a way to stop it in time.*

*The Office copy of the Highwood line is false. I don't know how long it has been false. Longer than seven years, I think. Every survey, a little more. The cairns between Nine and Fourteen have been pulled down and rebuilt further in. So have the cairns between Nineteen and Twenty-Four, and Twenty-Seven. The new ones carry the Office stamp. The old ones are where they always were, if you know where to look.*

*Trust the marks. The line through the circle always points to the true next cairn. Whoever moved the cairns didn't know that, or didn't think anyone would check. Follow the old bearings and the triangles close. Follow the Office ones and they don't.*

*I sent my reading at Nine down to Stonebridge by runner, on page 41. If page 41 is missing from my file when you get it, you will know who took it.*

My voice catches. I make it keep going.

*Do not tell the Office. Do not tell anyone who smells of the Office. Walk the old line, chain it honestly, and file it in front of witnesses, where it can't be lost. The true line runs out a quarter mile past the Office line for nearly four miles of its length. That is a great deal of forest. That is a great many wolves.*

*If they come for me before I'm done, I won't wait to be found.*

And then, at the very bottom, squeezed in, the slant so hard it almost lies down flat:

*Highwood's escort is honest. Sullen, but honest. If she's with you, tell her I'm sorry I sent her away. I didn't want her standing next to me when it happened.*

I stop reading.

Beside me, Mara has gone completely still. I can feel it through the tether: not stillness at all, but something enormous held down hard, like a hand clamped over the lid of a boiling pot.

"She sent you away," I say. Very gently. "To keep you safe."

Mara doesn't answer.

"She knew someone was coming. And she didn't want you there." I fold the note, carefully, along its creases. "It wasn't your fault, Mara. You didn't leave her. She made you go."

Mara makes a sound. It's very small. It's the sound something makes when it breaks cleanly, all the way through, after a long time under strain.

Then she puts her face in her hands.

I don't say anything else. I don't touch her. I sit beside her on the cold ledge with June's field book in my lap, and the whole of the Reach spread out below us going gold and grey and blue in the late light, and I feel her grief go through the tether into me like water into dry ground. It's huge. It's older than I am. It's been sitting in her for half a year with nowhere to go, and now it has somewhere, and it all comes out at once.

I let it. I just hold still, and let it come.

---

When it's passed, or at least when the worst of it has, I open June's field book and start reading her numbers.

It's the only thing I know how to do. It's the only comfort I've got. And it turns out to be exactly the right thing, because after a while Mara wipes her face and leans in to look over my shoulder, and I start explaining it to her the way my instructors explained it to me.

"Here. Cairn Nine. This is her bearing from Eight: forty-nine degrees, fifty-seven chains. Not fifty-two and thirty-eight." I tap the page. "That's the old cairn. The one I found under the larch."

"The toppled one."

"Yes. And here's her triangle." I show her June's neat little sums. "Closes to a tenth of a degree. Perfect. Beautiful, actually." I hear myself say it, and I can't stop. "Look at her error columns. I'd have killed for error columns like that in my second year."

Mara looks at me sideways.

"Sorry," I say. "It's just. She was very good."

"I told you that."

"You did."

I turn the page. Then the next one. And the next. Cairn after cairn, the true bearings, the true distances. June walked the whole stretch from Nine to Fourteen on the old marks, chained every length, closed every triangle. Then she'd gone ahead and scouted Nineteen to Twenty-Four, and Twenty-Seven, on her own, without chaining, just pacing and sighting. Rough numbers. Enough.

At the back of the book there's a sketch. A long, wobbling line along the ridge, with two lines running side by side: one solid, one dotted. In three places the solid line bows inward, eating into the forest. The dotted one runs straight.

*Office,* she's written beside the solid line. And beside the dotted one: *True.*

Under it, in very small letters: *About four miles of the border, a quarter mile deep. All on paper. The ground never moved.*

"Four miles," Mara says.

"Four miles of border. A quarter mile deep." I do the sum, automatically. "That's—"

"I know what it is." Her voice is rough. "That's the Long Wood. And the Pools. And the south slope above the ravine where my grandmother used to run." She's staring at the dotted line on the page. "We were told the ground slipped. Every time. Seven years ago, a rockfall. Fourteen years ago, a stream changed course. Before that, a landslide." Her hands curl in her lap. "My mother wrote letters. To the Office. Every time. And every time they wrote back very politely and told her the survey was the law."

I think of a magistrate in a lowland town, looking at my father across a desk. *Do you have a surveyor who says otherwise?*

"Mara," I say.

She looks up.

I reach into my pack. Down to the very bottom, under my spare socks. I pull out my own field book, and I open it at the back, at the page I keep for my own working, and I hand it to her.

---

She doesn't understand at first. She looks at my numbers, my small cramped figures, my sketch of a toppled cairn under a fallen larch. The old bearing. The number nineteen.

Then she does understand. I watch it happen.

"You knew," she says. "At Nine."

"I thought so."

"You said *probably*." Her voice has gone very quiet. "I asked you if it fell in the slip. You looked right at me, and you said *probably*."

"Yes."

"You *lied* to me."

"Yes." I don't look away. I make myself not look away. "I was scared. I was scared I was wrong, and I'd start a war over a bad sum on my first survey. And I was scared I was right, and that if I told you, you'd go to your mother, and Highwood would go down the mountain with bows, and someone would end up with a tag on their finger. I didn't have proof. I had a gouge in a stone and one triangle." I take a breath. "I'm sorry. I should have told you. You said *tell me when you're sure*, and I wasn't, and I hid behind that. It wasn't fair."

She stares at me. The tether is a storm. I can feel her anger, real and hot and justified. I can feel her hurt. And under both of them, slower, something else coming up through the ground like water.

"And now?" she asks.

I look down at the two field books in our laps. Mine and June's. Two sets of numbers, two surveyors, one line, and every triangle closing to a tenth of a degree.

"Now I'm sure," I say.

Mara doesn't answer. The wind fills the silence.

Then she hands my field book back to me, and her fingers rest on mine for just a second longer than they need to.

"Then tell me," she says. "All of it. From the map room."

So I do.

---

I tell her about the paler slate on the floor in Stonebridge, the recut groove bending inward between Nine and Fourteen. About Voss's clean hands. About the missing page, and the index that said forty-one. About Theo saying *measure twice*, and the hunter saying *Office told us there'd be no trouble on this stretch*, and the smell of lamp oil and paper on the rim of the ravine. I lay it all out in order, like a survey, one point to the next, and when I'm done, it doesn't look like bad luck any more.

It looks like a line.

Mara is quiet for a long time after I finish. She sits with her knees drawn up and her bow across them, looking out at the Reach. The light is going copper. Far below, a hawk turns slow circles over the River valleys, and the smoke of Stonebridge lies in its gorge like a stain.

"Theo," she says at last.

"I don't think Theo moved any cairns. I think Theo knows somebody did."

"Theo always knows." She says it without heat, like a fact about weather. "River's been taking Office favours for as long as I've been alive. Safe passage, good surveys on their side of the line. My mother used to say River would sell its own pups if the Office offered a fair price for them." She pauses. "He told you to measure twice."

"Yes."

"He didn't have to."

I think about that. About Theo's sunburned face going briefly cold, and the long look he gave me over Cairn Seven, doing sums. "No," I say. "He didn't."

"Then either he wanted you to find it," Mara says slowly, "or he wanted to see what you'd do when you did." She turns her bow over in her hands. "With Theo, it's usually both."

I unlash the pry bar from the side of my pack and lay it across my knees, the iron cold and heavy, the tip still scraped bright. Lamp oil and paper. Ink up to the wrists, or no ink at all.

"I want to know whose hands were on this," I say.

"So do I," says Mara, and for the first time since the ravine, she sounds like a wolf.

"So what do we do?" Mara asks. It's what I asked her, on the rock above the ravine. She doesn't seem to notice.

"What June said." I pick up the note again. "We walk the old line. The true one. We chain it honestly, every length, from here to Thirty-One. We rebuild the cairns that were pulled down. And then we take it to Stonebridge and we file it in front of witnesses, where it can't be lost." I look at her. "All four miles."

"They'll try to kill you again."

"They'll try to kill *us* again." I hold up my bandaged hand. "And as far as I understand it, they'll only have to manage one of us."

That should frighten her. It does. I feel it. But something else goes across her face too, fierce and bright, and when she looks at me the gold isn't in her eyes yet, the sun is still up, but it might as well be.

"The hold first," she says. "My mother needs to see this."

"I thought we weren't going near your mother."

"That was before," says Mara. She stands, and holds out her hand to pull me up, and I take it, and this time neither of us lets go quite as fast. "That was when it was just us."

Behind her, the sun touches the far ridge, and the whole long sweep of the Highwood forest goes up in gold, from the ravine all the way to the north. Four miles of it. A quarter mile deep.

I take June's field book and wrap it back up in its oilcloth, and I put it in the bottom of my pack, under the socks, with mine.

Two surveyors, I think. One line.

"Show me the way," I say.


# Chapter Fourteen

The Highwood hold isn't on any map.

I know this for certain, because I've spent six years looking at maps of the Reach, and I've walked across the biggest one in existence in my socks, and I'd remember. On the slate floor in Stonebridge, the high Highwood forest is a smooth gold slope with nothing on it but cairn numbers. No hall. No houses. No smoke.

There's smoke now. I smell it a mile off, as we come over the last rise in the late morning light: woodsmoke and roasting meat and something sweet underneath, like honey on a hot stone.

"Don't look so surprised," says Mara.

"I'm not surprised."

"You've got your mouth open."

I close my mouth.

The hold sits in a long fold of the mountain, where the ridge drops back into a sheltered bowl full of the biggest larches I've ever seen. Their trunks are as broad as wagons. Their crowns are lost somewhere far overhead in a blaze of gold. And built in among them, so close and so cleverly that at first I can't tell where the trees stop and the building starts, is the hall.

It's long and low, slate-walled, timber-framed, roofed in turf so thick that grass and small gold ferns grow along the ridgeline. The timbers are carved. Every post, every beam, every lintel over every door, covered in carving: lines, notches, swirls, long chains of small marks running round and round. Smaller buildings cluster around it, a dozen, two dozen, with smoke rising from all of them. There are goats in a pen. There are children running between the trunks. There are people everywhere, splitting wood, carrying water, sitting in doorways, mending nets.

Every single one of them stops what they're doing and looks at me.

I feel it like weather. Like walking out of a warm room into the wind. Fifty pairs of amber eyes, turning toward the chain on my shoulder.

"Keep walking," Mara says, low. "Don't stop. Don't look away."

"What happens if I look away?"

"Nothing." A pause. "They'll just think you're prey."

I don't look away.

---

The walk from the edge of the bowl to the door of the hall is about a hundred and forty steps. I know because I count them. Nobody speaks to us. Nobody moves aside, either; people simply stand where they are and let us go round them, and their eyes follow me the whole way, and the silence follows behind us like a dog.

Near the door, a young woman steps into our path.

She's about my age, broad-shouldered, black-haired like Mara, with a long knife on her belt and a split lip that's half healed. She looks at Mara. Then she looks at me, and then at the chain, and her lip curls.

"So this is the one," she says. "The Office's new pet."

"Lena," says Mara, without slowing down.

"Do we have to feed it? Or does it eat paper?"

"Move."

Lena doesn't move. She turns her eyes on me. They're very like Mara's, close up: the same amber, the same dark rim. But where Mara's eyes have always felt like a warning, Lena's feel like a promise.

"How much ground are you going to take from us this time, surveyor?" she asks softly. "A mile? Two? Or are you going to be generous, and only take the Pools?"

"None," I say.

She laughs. "That's what the last one said."

"The last one," I say, "was telling the truth."

Lena's face changes. Just for a second. Then Mara is between us, close, closer than she's been to me all morning, and she says something to Lena in a voice too low for me to hear, and whatever it is, Lena steps aside.

She watches me all the way through the door.

"Cousin," Mara says, when we're inside. "My aunt's girl. She's been angry since she was born."

"She's got your eyes."

"She's got my temper." Mara doesn't look at me. "I got the eyes."

---

The inside of the hall is one long room, warm and dim and smoky, lit by a fire that runs down the middle of the floor in a channel of stone almost the whole length of the building. The carved posts march down either side. Close up, I can see now what the carvings are: long lines of small notches and marks, grouped and spaced, running round and round each post like writing.

"The songs," Mara says, following my eyes. "Every border song we have. Every cairn. Every line." She nods at the nearest post. "That one's the Long Wood. We lost it before I was born."

I look at it. A post covered in carved music. A song about a place that doesn't exist on any map any more.

At the far end of the hall, on a wide wooden bench by the end of the fire, someone is coughing.

It's a long cough. Deep and wet and terrible, the kind that goes on past the point where you think it should stop, and then on further. The people sitting around the fire have all gone quiet, waiting for it to finish. Nobody looks. Everybody listens.

When it's over, a voice says, hoarse but perfectly clear: "Bring her here."

---

Ruth of Highwood is small. That's the first thing I notice, and it surprises me, because I'd been picturing someone like Mara only more so: tall and dark and dangerous, with a bow across her knees. Ruth is a little white-haired woman wrapped in furs on a bench by the fire, with a thick white braid over one shoulder and a face like a walnut. Her hands, folded in her lap, are thin and spotted and shake slightly.

Her eyes are not small at all.

They're amber, like Mara's, and like Lena's. But they're older and sharper and much, much harder to look into. They go over me once, from head to foot, the way I'd go over a stretch of ground before I chain it, and I feel like I've been measured to within a hand's width.

"Surveyor," she says.

"Ruth of Highwood." I bow. My instructors told us to bow to pack alphas. They didn't tell us how low. I go fairly low. "Cass Reed, Survey Office. Thank you for having me."

"I haven't had you yet." Her eyes slide to Mara. "You're late."

"We had trouble at the ravine," Mara says.

"I heard." Ruth's gaze drops, briefly, to my bandaged hand, then to Mara's forearm. Then it comes back up. It doesn't change. "Sit."

We sit. There's a bench across the fire from her, and we sit on it, and I'm aware, very aware, that Mara has sat down further from me than anyone would sit from anyone, a good arm's length away, and that this is also something a person might notice.

Ruth notices. I can tell.

She doesn't say so.

"Show me," she says instead.

I take out June's field book.

---

I don't know how long she reads. The fire burns down and somebody builds it up again. People come and go at the edges of the hall, quietly, looking at us and then away. Lena comes in and stands by one of the carved posts with her arms folded, glaring. Ruth turns June's pages slowly, one at a time, with her thin shaking fingers, and she reads every number. I can tell. I watch her eyes moving down the columns, the way mine do.

When she gets to the sketch at the back, the solid line and the dotted one, she stops.

She stays stopped for a long time.

Then she closes the book, and lays her hand flat on its cover.

"What else," she says. Not a question.

I unlash the pry bar from my pack and hold it out across the fire. Ruth doesn't take it. She leans forward instead, slowly, her furs sliding off one thin shoulder, and brings her face close to the scraped iron tip, and breathes in.

Her nostrils flare, exactly the way Mara's do.

"Lamp oil," she says. "And paper." She sits back. "And fear. Whoever held this was frightened." Her eyes go to me. "Good. Remember that, surveyor. The ones who are frightened make mistakes."

I lay the bar down on the bench beside me.

Ruth looks at June's book under her hand for a while longer. Then she starts to sing.

It's very quiet. Under her breath, almost, a low cracked hum, but there are words in it, old words, in a language I half recognise from the oldest parts of the treaty. It goes up, and down, and round, and I realise after a while that I'm hearing the shape of it as a line. Here a rise, here a fall. Here a long straight run. It's a border. She's singing a border.

Around the hall, one by one, people stop moving.

Somewhere near the door, a man's voice joins in. Then a woman's. Then a child's, high and uncertain. By the carved post, Lena's mouth is moving, her arms still folded, her eyes wet.

Beside me on the bench, Mara sings too. Very softly. I feel it through the tether before I hear it, a hum in my bones, like the vibration you feel through the chain when it's pulled taut.

I don't know the words. I don't sing. I just sit there in the warm smoky dark with the whole of Highwood singing around me, a song about a forest that the Office stole from them a quarter mile at a time, and I hold my bandaged hand in my lap and try not to cry in front of an alpha.

When the song ends, Ruth opens her eyes.

"The Long Wood," she says. "My mother taught me that one. I never thought I'd sing it over anything but a grave."

She looks at me.

"You'll walk this," she says. It isn't a question. "The old line. June's line."

"Yes."

"And you'll file it. In Stonebridge. In front of the Registrar."

"In front of everyone," I say. "That's what June said. Witnesses. Where it can't be lost."

Ruth looks at me for a long moment. Then she says, quite calmly, "They'll kill you."

"They've tried."

"They'll try harder." She coughs again, a short one this time, into a cloth she holds to her mouth. When she lowers it there's a speck of red. She doesn't look at it. "And if they kill you, the line lapses. Your clean-handed Registrar wrote it in his letter. My daughter would have shown it to me if you hadn't told me. She's been carrying that letter folded inside her coat for a week, like a stone in a boot."

I glance at Mara. Mara is looking at the fire.

"I know," I say.

"Then why?"

I think about it. I think about a stream at the bottom of a field. About a magistrate. About a post covered in carved music and a little cairn of seven stones at the bottom of a ravine.

"Because I measured it," I say. "And it's true."

Something happens in Ruth's walnut face. Not a smile. Something older than that.

"Good," she says. "Then Highwood will see you to the end of it." She looks across the fire at Mara. Then back at me. Then at the arm's length of bench between us, which is suddenly, very obviously, too wide.

"Full moon is two nights from now," she says. "We turn. All of us. Even me, old as I am. You'll stay in the guesthouse, surveyor, with the shutters closed and the door barred. For your own safety."

"Of course," I say.

"Of course." Ruth's sharp eyes rest on me. Then on her daughter. "Whatever the moon has to show me, I'd rather not see it in my hall."

She picks up June's book again, and opens it, and doesn't look at either of us.

Beside me, Mara has stopped breathing.

I sit there with my heart going too hard, and hers going harder, and I think: *she knows.*

She knows, or she's guessing. And in two nights, the moon will tell her which.


# Chapter Fifteen

The guesthouse at the Highwood hold has one room, one bed, one window with heavy wooden shutters, and one bar for the door: a beam of larch as thick as my arm, resting in two iron brackets.

"For the full moon," says the girl who shows me in. She's perhaps ten, with a gap in her front teeth and a fierce, solemn expression. "So nobody gets eaten."

"Has anybody ever got eaten?"

She thinks about it carefully. "Not *recently*."

"That's very reassuring."

"My name's Bet," she says, and then, without a pause, "Can I see your telescope?"

It's not a telescope; it's a theodolite. I tell her so. Then I spend the next hour sitting on the doorstep of the guesthouse with the instrument set up on its brass legs, while Bet and about nine of her friends take turns squinting through it at the far side of the bowl. They find a squirrel. They find a goat. They find Lena, splitting wood behind the hall, and spend a very happy five minutes watching her through the eyepiece until she feels it, somehow, and turns and glares directly at the lens, and they all shriek and scatter.

"She'll be cross," Bet says, coming back cautiously.

"She's always cross."

"Only with you," says Bet, with great authority. "She's not cross with Mara. Mara's her favourite. When Mara's alpha, Lena's going to be her second." She squints at me. "Are you going to take our forest?"

The question comes so easily, so plainly, that for a moment I don't know what to do with it.

"No," I say. "I'm going to try to give some of it back."

Bet considers this. Then she nods, once, as if it's settled, and goes back to the eyepiece.

---

I spend the rest of the day in the hall, at a long table near the fire, with June's field book open on one side of me and mine on the other and a large sheet of rough paper spread between them. I'm drawing the true line. Every cairn, every bearing, every distance, from Thirteen to Thirty-One. Where June chained it, I copy her numbers. Where she only paced and sighted, I mark it in pencil, to be checked. Where the Office cairns sit, I draw them too, in a different colour, so the two lines lie side by side on the paper the way they lie side by side on the mountain.

Ruth's people come and go around me. Nobody speaks to me directly. But they look at the paper. They lean over my shoulder, at a polite distance, and look, and I see their eyes go to the dotted line, to the places where it runs out past the solid one. The Pools. The south slope. The Long Wood.

Some of them hum under their breath when they see it. I don't know the songs. I'm starting to know the shape of them.

Once, late in the afternoon, Ruth comes and stands at my shoulder. I don't hear her come. I just feel the cold of her furs, and smell woodsmoke and old age and something bitter, like medicine, and look up to find her watching my pencil.

She doesn't say anything for a long time. She coughs once, into her cloth, and folds it away.

"You draw like her," she says at last.

"Like June?"

"Like someone who thinks a line can be true." She looks at me, those sharp old eyes, and then away across the hall, toward the door, toward the far side of the bowl, where an ache goes through my wrist and eases. Draw. Loose. "Be careful, surveyor. It's a dangerous thing to believe in, up here."

Then she goes back to her bench by the fire, and I sit with the pencil in my hand and my heart going too fast, and don't draw anything at all for a while.

Mara isn't there.

I know exactly where she is. That's the trouble. She's on the far side of the bowl, at the edge of the trees, practising with her bow. I know because every time she draws, a thin ache goes through my own right wrist, and every time she looses, it eases. Draw. Ache. Loose. Ease. Over and over, steady as a pulse, all afternoon.

She's avoiding me. It's the plan. I know it's the plan. I agreed to the plan.

I hate the plan.

---

By the next day, the day of the moon, the whole hold is restless.

You can feel it in the air, the way you feel a storm coming. People stop in the middle of what they're doing and lift their heads and breathe. Children run in packs between the great larches, shrieking, and nobody tells them to stop. Two men get into a shoving match by the woodpile over nothing at all and have to be pulled apart. The goats huddle at the far end of their pen and won't come out.

Lena passes me on the path to the hall in the late afternoon and stops. She's flushed, bright-eyed, her hands curling and uncurling at her sides.

"Shutters closed tonight, surveyor," she says. "Door barred. Don't open it for anything. Not for anything you hear."

"What might I hear?"

She smiles. It is not a nice smile. It has a lot of teeth in it.

"Us," she says, and walks on.

---

I'm in the guesthouse at dusk when the knock comes.

I've done everything I was told. I've closed the shutters and latched them. I've laid the larch bar beside the door, ready to drop into its brackets. I've lit the lamp and sat down on the bed with June's field book, and I've read the same column of figures seven times without taking in any of it, because all I can feel is Mara.

She's close. She's been getting closer all evening. Across the bowl, then at the edge of the hold, then among the buildings, moving in circles, the way you'd pace a cage. And now she's right outside.

I open the door.

She's standing on the step with her hand still raised to knock again. She looks wrong. Not hurt; she's not hurt, I'd know. But wrong in a way I've never seen her: her eyes too bright, her breathing too quick, her whole body strung tight as a bowstring at full draw. Her braid is coming undone. There are larch needles caught in it.

"I came to check the bar," she says.

"It's here." I gesture at it, lying beside the door. "I haven't put it in yet."

"Put it in."

"I will. When you're—"

"Cass. *Put it in.*" She steps inside. She doesn't seem to notice she's done it. The door swings half shut behind her, and the room is suddenly very small, the way the bothy was small, and she's standing in the middle of it with her hands clenched and her eyes on mine. "The moon's coming up. Soon. I can feel it. I have to be in the forest before it does, or I'll turn in here, and you don't want to see that close up. You don't want to be in a small room with that." Her voice drops. "*I* don't want you in a small room with that."

"Then go."

"I'm going."

She doesn't go.

She stands there, two feet from me, breathing hard. I can feel her heart going so fast it's almost a hum. I can feel the pull of the moon in her, a deep, enormous, aching drag, like the tide pulling at a boat, and I can feel her fighting it with everything she has, and I can feel something else under that.

Something that isn't about the moon at all.

"Mara," I say.

"Don't."

"I haven't said anything."

"You're going to." Her eyes are fixed on my face. "I can feel you about to."

"What am I about to say?"

"Something," she says, through her teeth, "that's going to make this worse."

I don't say anything. I just look at her. At the larch needles in her hair. At the bandage on her wrist, frayed and grey now after a week on the line, and the newer cut on her forearm, scabbed and healing. At her mouth, which is set hard, and shaking very slightly at one corner.

"Strictly business," I say quietly.

She makes a sound that might be a laugh or might be something breaking.

"It's not working," she says.

"I know."

"It's never going to work."

"I know, Mara."

"We said nobody touches anybody. We said if we didn't feed it, it would get quieter." She takes a breath, ragged. "It's not quieter. It's so loud, Cass. It's the loudest thing in my head. Louder than the moon. Louder than—" Her voice cracks. "I can't think about the line, or the forest, or my mother, or Voss, because every time I try, it's just *you*, and where you are, and whether you're cold, and whether that idiot hand of yours is hurting, and—"

I kiss her.

I don't decide to. It's like stepping in front of the crossbow. My body has already done it by the time my mind catches up. I step forward and put my good hand on her face, on her cheek, rough and warm and wet, and I kiss her.

For one long, terrible second, she doesn't move at all.

Then she does.

She makes a sound against my mouth, low in her throat, a sound I'll remember for the rest of my life, and her hands come up and fist in the front of my shirt and she pulls me in, hard, and kisses me back. Not gently. Nothing about Mara is gentle. She kisses me like she's been starving for a week and I'm the first warm thing she's found, like the storm on the turf roof, like the rockfall, like the whole mountain coming down at once. And the tether goes up like a struck match.

It's not like anything. It's not like any kiss I've ever had, and I've had a few, in the lowlands, with nice girls who smelled of soap. This is her mouth, and her hands, and her heartbeat slamming against mine, and at the same time it's *me*, feeling her feel it. Her want is in my blood and mine is in hers, and it doubles back and doubles back again, like a sound in the ravine echoing off the walls, until I can't tell whose it is or where it started. I don't care. I don't care at all.

I don't know how long it lasts. Not long. Not nearly long enough.

She tears herself away.

She stands with her back to the half-open door, breathing like she's run up the whole mountain, and her eyes are *blazing*. In the lamplight they're amber. But outside, through the gap in the door, the sky over the far ridge has gone silver, and I can feel it, I can feel the moon coming up through her like a wave about to break.

"I have to go," she says. Her voice is shredded.

"I know."

"Bar the door."

"I will."

"*Cass.*" She looks at me. Just for a second. And everything she can't say is in it, all of it, every word, so loud through the tether that I could weep.

Then she turns and runs.

I hear her feet hit the path. I hear them go off across the bowl, toward the trees, fast, faster than any human could run. And halfway there, I feel it. A wrench, deep in my chest, as if something has been turned inside out. A rush of heat and strength and wildness that isn't mine. And then, out in the dark, a sound.

Not a scream. A song.

One long rising note, high and clear and wild, from the edge of the forest. And then, all around the bowl, one after another, other voices take it up, until the whole hold is singing, the whole mountain is singing, and the moon comes up over the far ridge, round and white and enormous, and pours its light over everything.

I stand in the doorway with my fingers pressed to my mouth.

I should close the door. I should drop the larch bar into its brackets and latch the shutters and sit on the bed with the lamp lit and wait for morning, the way I promised.

I don't move.

Out among the great larches, somewhere in the silver dark, I can feel exactly where she is.


# Chapter Sixteen

I tell myself I'm only going to stand in the doorway.

That's all. Just for a minute. Just long enough to see. The bar is right there by my foot, the shutters are latched, and the second anything comes near I'll step back inside and drop the beam into its brackets and that'll be that. I'm not breaking a promise. I'm bending one. Very slightly. In a precise and measured way.

I last about a minute in the doorway.

Then I'm on the step. Then I'm on the path. Then I'm standing at the edge of the guesthouse's small patch of trampled ground, with the door wide open behind me and the lamp spilling yellow out onto the needles, and the moon pouring white over everything else.

It's the brightest night I've ever seen. Brighter than some lowland days. The great larches stand up out of the bowl like pillars in a temple, silver on one side and black on the other, every needle on every branch picked out sharp. The hall is a long dark shape, its turf roof furred with light. The smaller buildings are shut and silent. Not a lamp anywhere. Not a single human sound.

But the bowl is full of movement.

They're everywhere. Shapes, flowing between the trees. Grey, mostly, and silver-grey, and dun, and one or two nearly white, moving fast and low and silent through the shadows and then bursting out into the moonlight in long, loping strides. I see two of them meet in a clearing and rise up on their hind legs, chest to chest, snapping and tumbling, and I think it's a fight, and then I realise it's play. I see a whole knot of smaller shapes, half-grown, yipping and tearing round the trunk of one enormous larch in circles, and I think of Bet and her gap tooth and her fierce solemn face, and I bite my lip to keep from laughing out loud.

And over it all, the singing. It never quite stops. One voice goes up, then another, from the far side of the bowl, from up on the ridge, from somewhere off in the dark of the forest. It weaves in and out, rising and falling. Here a rise, here a fall. A long straight run.

It's a border. They're singing their border.

I stand at the edge of the trampled ground with my arms wrapped around myself, and I cry, a little, and I don't care.

---

And then I feel her.

She's been out there all along. Of course she has. Off to my left, a long way, up on the slope above the bowl, moving fast. I've been feeling her the whole time, the way I always do, a fixed point. But now something has changed. She's turned. She's coming back.

And through the tether, suddenly, so strongly it nearly knocks me off my feet, I feel *her*.

Not her pain. Not her heartbeat. *Her.* What it's like, right now, to be her.

It's speed. That's the first thing. Speed like I've never known, like falling and flying at once, the ground pouring away under four feet that know exactly where every stone and root is without being told. It's strength, a deep easy power in shoulders and haunches, the kind that could run all night and never tire. It's the smell of the forest, a thousand smells, ten thousand, every one of them sharp and clear and full of meaning: deer here an hour ago, a fox, water, resin, the warm living smell of the pack all round.

And it's joy. Huge, uncomplicated, overwhelming joy. The joy of being exactly what you are, in exactly the place where you're allowed to be it.

*This,* I think. *This is what the chain takes away.*

I'm still standing there, gasping, my hands pressed to my chest, when the first wolf finds me.

---

It isn't Mara.

It comes out of the shadow of the guesthouse wall, close, very close, ten feet away. It's big. Bigger than the ones I saw on the spur. Its coat is dark grey, nearly black along the spine, and its ears are flat, and its lips are drawn back from teeth as long as my fingers.

It's looking right at me. At my face.

I know those eyes. Amber, with a dark rim. I saw them this afternoon on the path to the hall, bright and flushed and full of teeth.

*Don't open it for anything. Not for anything you hear.*

"Lena," I whisper.

The wolf takes a step forward. It's growling. It's a low sound, so low I feel it more than hear it, in my ribs and the soles of my feet. Its hackles are up along its spine in a stiff dark ridge.

I don't move. I don't look away. *Don't look away,* Mara said. *They'll think you're prey.*

I'm fairly sure it already thinks that.

Another step. The growl deepens. Its eyes are fixed on my face, and I don't know if it understands, if there's anything of Lena in there at all right now, or if it's just moon and instinct and the smell of a surveyor standing where no surveyor should be.

It gathers itself to spring.

Something black hits it from the side.

---

They go down together in a storm of snarling and needles. It's over in seconds. The dark grey wolf rolls, scrambles, comes up, and the black one is already there, standing over it, teeth bared, a sound coming out of her I've never heard from any living thing. It isn't a growl. It's lower. It's older. It's the sound the mountain made in the ravine just before it came down.

The grey wolf drops. Flat. Belly to the needles, ears back, tail tucked under.

The black wolf stands over it for a long moment. Then she steps back.

The grey wolf scrambles up and bolts. Off into the trees, low and fast, and gone.

And the black wolf turns around and looks at me.

She's huge. I knew she would be. Taller at the shoulder than I am at the waist, long-legged, deep-chested, her coat black as a starless sky, gleaming silver where the moonlight touches it. There's a pale old scar across her chest, three parallel lines, where the fur grows in white. Her right foreleg is held a little off the ground, just above the paw. Where a bandage would be, if wolves wore bandages.

Her eyes are amber. And around the edge of each iris, shining in the full white light of the moon like two rings of fire, is gold.

"Mara," I say.

She walks to me. She doesn't hurry. She comes across the trampled ground slowly, one step at a time, head low, ears forward, and stops in front of me, so close I can feel the heat coming off her, smell resin and woodsmoke and something wild and warm that I would know anywhere now, in the dark, with my eyes shut.

I put my hand out. My left hand. The cut one, still bandaged.

She pushes her head into it.

Her fur is coarse on top and impossibly soft underneath. Her skull is broad and hard under my palm. She's breathing in long slow breaths, and I can feel each one through my hand and through the tether at the same time, and I'm crying again, properly this time, and she makes a small sound in her chest, low and rough, and leans her whole weight against my legs until I nearly fall over.

I put my other arm around her neck. I bury my face in her fur.

"You saved me again," I say into it. "That's twice. I'm keeping count."

She huffs. I could swear it's a laugh.

---

I don't know how long we stand like that. Long enough for my arms to go numb. Long enough for the moon to climb a little higher over the bowl. Long enough that, when I finally lift my head, we're not alone.

There's a wolf at the edge of the trees.

It's small. That's what I notice first. Small and old, its coat pure white, thick and ragged, the white of the braid that hangs over Ruth's shoulder in the hall. It's standing very still in the shadow of the nearest larch, one forepaw raised, the way you'd stand when you're too tired to walk any further but too proud to sit.

It's looking at us.

At Mara, pressed against my legs. At my arm around her neck. And then, slowly, deliberately, it lifts its eyes to my face.

I know what it sees. I can't see it myself, but I know. I've seen it in Mara's eyes twice now, and I can feel it in mine, a warmth like candlelight behind them. The full moon is pouring down on my upturned face. My eyes are shining gold.

Like a lamp, Mara said. Anyone who looks at you will know.

The white wolf looks for a long time.

Then it looks at Mara. Mara has gone completely still against me. I can feel her heart, racing. I can feel her fear, sudden and enormous.

The white wolf doesn't move. It doesn't growl. It doesn't make any sound at all.

It simply turns, and walks away into the trees, slow and stiff and silent, and doesn't look back.

Mara makes a small, broken noise against my legs.

I put my hand on her head and keep it there.

"She knows," I whisper.

Mara doesn't answer. She doesn't need to. What comes back through the tether isn't a word, but it's as good as one: a great, cold, sinking drop of dread, like a stone going down into deep water.

---

I go back inside eventually. Mara walks me to the door. She stands on the step while I go in, and she doesn't leave until I've dropped the larch bar into its brackets with a heavy wooden thud. I sit on the bed with my back against the wall and listen to her pad away across the needles, and feel her go back out into the moonlight, back to the running and the singing and the joy.

I don't sleep. Not for a long time.

I lie on the narrow bed with the lamp turned low and my bandaged hand resting on my chest, and I think about what I felt out there. The speed. The strength. Ten thousand smells. The joy of being exactly what you are, in exactly the place where you're allowed to be it.

I've spent my whole life thinking of borders as lines. Ink on paper, ochre on stone, grooves in a slate floor. Things you could measure. Things you could get right or wrong, the way you get a sum right or wrong. I became a surveyor because I wanted to be the one who got it right.

I never once thought about what it's like to be on the other side of the line. To stand at the edge of a cairn on open ground and feel all of that, the speed and the strength and the joy, waiting for you three steps away. To know that a man in a map room with clean hands has moved it a quarter mile further off, and called it a slip, and written your mother a very polite letter.

Four miles. A quarter mile deep. I'd thought of it as acres.

It's this. It's this, every full moon, for every wolf in the hold, for as long as the line stays wrong.

I turn on my side and press my face into the pillow, which smells of larch and old wool, and I make myself a promise, and I don't say it out loud, because I don't need to. It's the same promise I made in a rented room in a lowland town when I was eleven. It's just bigger now.

Some of the joy comes back to me through the tether, all night long, from somewhere out among the great trees. It lulls me, in the end. Near dawn, I sleep.

In the morning, a knock comes at the door. Not Mara's.

"Surveyor," says a voice. A child's voice: Bet. Very solemn. "Ruth says you're to come to the hall. Now. On your own."


# Chapter Seventeen

On the walk across the bowl, I have to step over three people.

They're asleep in the needles, where the moon left them. A man curled under a larch with his arm over his face. Two young women tangled together at the foot of the woodpile, snoring. There are clothes everywhere: coats, boots, shirts, flung over branches and trampled into the ground, as if a very large and very drunk wedding party had passed through. Nobody seems embarrassed. A woman walking past with a bucket of water steps neatly over the snoring pair without looking down.

I look down. I can't help it. Then I look very firmly at the hall.

The hall is empty when I come in, except for Ruth.

The long fire has burned down to a red seam of embers running the length of the floor. The carved posts stand in the grey morning light like trees in fog. The benches are bare. Somewhere outside, very faintly, I can hear the hold waking up slowly: a door, a voice, a child laughing, someone groaning in a way that sounds a lot like the morning after a festival. But in here there's nobody but the small white-haired woman on the bench at the end of the fire, wrapped in her furs, watching me walk the whole length of the room toward her.

It's sixty-one steps. I count every one.

"Sit," says Ruth.

I sit. Across the embers from her, where I sat two days ago. Mara isn't beside me this time. I know exactly where she is: in one of the small houses on the far side of the bowl, asleep, deeply, the kind of sleep you fall into after running all night. She's tired in a way I can feel in my own legs. She doesn't know I'm here.

Ruth studies me for a long time. She doesn't cough. She doesn't move. Her old sharp eyes go over my face, slowly, the way you'd go over a page you've read before, checking whether the words have changed.

"I asked you to stay inside," she says.

"Yes."

"With the door barred."

"Yes."

"You opened it."

"Yes," I say. "I'm sorry. I didn't mean to. I mean, I did mean to, I just didn't mean to *mean* to, if that—" I stop. "I'm sorry."

Ruth says nothing. She looks at my left hand, still bandaged, resting on my knee. Then she looks at my face again, and she says, very quietly, "How long?"

I could lie. I think about it. For about the length of a heartbeat, I think about looking this old woman in the eye and saying *I don't know what you mean.*

I don't.

"Five days," I say. "At the ravine. The rockfall."

Ruth closes her eyes.

---

She doesn't speak for a long time. When she does, her voice is low and tired and very steady.

"My father had it," she says. "With my mother. I watched them my whole life. When he cut his hand splitting wood, she'd drop her bowl in the kitchen. When she was sick, he couldn't eat. When he went hunting, she'd go to the door, and look up at the ridge, and say, *he's by the Pools*, or *he's above the ravine*, long before anyone could see him. They died a month apart." Her hands shift in her lap. "I wanted it. When I was a girl. I wanted it more than anything. I thought it was the most beautiful thing in the world."

"It is," I say, before I can stop myself.

Her eyes open. She looks at me.

"Yes," she says. "It is. And it is going to kill my daughter."

The embers tick. Outside, someone laughs.

"You know Clause Nine," Ruth says.

"Yes."

"Then you know what happens to her if anyone finds out."

"Yes."

"And to you."

"I know." I make myself say it. "My hand. And every survey I've signed. Including this one. The line lapses. You lose the forest."

"I don't care about the forest," says Ruth.

It's so unexpected that I just stare at her.

"I've lost four miles of forest in my life," she says. "I've written letters. I've sung songs over graves. I'll lose more before I die, and Mara will lose more after me, and the Office will write very polite letters about it until the end of the world." She leans forward, slowly, and her furs slip off one thin shoulder. "But I have one daughter, surveyor. One. And if your Registrar finds out what's in her eyes at full moon, he'll hang her from the top lock in Stonebridge, and he'll do it smiling, and he'll write me a letter about it after."

I don't say anything. There isn't anything.

"So," she says, sitting back. "Here's what you'll do."

---

She tells me.

I'm to finish the line. The true one, June's line, every length chained, every cairn rebuilt. I'm to file it in Stonebridge in front of witnesses, as June said, before first snow. Highwood will stand witness. All of Highwood, if need be.

And Mara is not to be in Stonebridge at full moon. Not ever. Not for any reason.

"And after?" I ask. "When it's filed?"

"After, you go home," says Ruth. "To your lowlands. Your flat fields. You go home, and you don't come back."

I knew she'd say it. Mara said it too, on the rock above the ravine. It still lands like a blow.

"The tether doesn't break," I say.

"No."

"So she'd—we'd—"

"You'd feel each other. For the rest of your lives. From the other end of the world." Ruth's voice doesn't soften. "My father went to Stonebridge once, for a winter. My mother stood at the door every night, facing south. She didn't eat. She didn't sleep. By spring she was a ghost." A breath. "It's not a kind thing. I'm not asking you to do a kind thing. I'm asking you to keep her alive."

"With respect," I say, and my voice comes out steadier than I expect, "that's what June did."

Ruth's eyes narrow.

"She sent Mara away. At Eight. To keep her safe." I make myself hold the old woman's gaze. "She wrote it down. *I didn't want her standing next to me when it happened.* And Mara has spent half a year thinking it was her fault. That she left. That she should have stayed." I swallow. "Sending someone away to keep them safe isn't the same as keeping them safe. It just means they don't get a say."

The silence in the hall is very loud.

"You're telling me," says Ruth softly, "how to protect my own daughter."

"No. I'm telling you what I learned from your daughter." My heart is going so hard I can feel it in my throat. "She doesn't like being sent away. Not by June. Not by me. Probably not by you."

For a long moment I'm quite sure I've gone too far. Ruth's face is like the slate on the floor in Stonebridge: grey and carved and giving nothing away.

Then, very slowly, something in it cracks. Not a smile. Something wearier and older than that.

"June didn't have my daughter's heart beating in her chest," she says. "You do. That changes the sum, surveyor. You know it does." A pause. "But you're not wrong."

"So—"

"So finish the line first," says Ruth. "Get the forest back. Get my daughter's name off any list that has a hangman at the end of it. And then we'll talk about who gets a say." Her eyes glint. "I'm old. I'm not stupid."

It isn't a yes. It isn't a no, either. I'll take it.

The fire ticks. I look down at my hands. My right hand, my chain hand, the one they'd take. My left, bandaged, the one I put out to her in the moonlight last night, and she pushed her head into it.

"Does she know you're asking me this?"

"No," says Ruth. "And you won't tell her. Not yet. She'll fight it. She'll fight me. She fights everything." A long pause. "She gets that from me."

I almost laugh. I don't. I nod, once.

Ruth watches me do it. Something in her face shifts, a very small thing, like a stone settling in a stream.

"Lena," she says.

My head comes up.

"She was close to you last night. I saw." Ruth's mouth thins. "She was far gone in the moon. Young ones are, at the full. They don't remember much the morning after." A pause. "She'll remember enough."

"Will she tell anyone?"

"Not while I'm alive." Ruth coughs then, finally, a long, deep, wet one, into her cloth. When she lowers it, there's more red than last time. She looks at it, this time. Then she folds it away. "After that, I can't promise you anything."

---

I think that's the end of it. I start to stand.

"Sit down," says Ruth. "I haven't finished."

I sit.

She reaches into her furs and brings out a letter. It's small and folded and sealed with dark red wax, and I know the seal before I see it properly. A little slanted *J.M.*

My breath stops.

"She came here," Ruth says. "In spring. At night. Three days before she sent Mara away from her at Eight." She turns the letter over in her thin fingers. "She'd camped near the hold. She came to the hall after dark, when Mara was asleep, and she sat where you're sitting, and she asked me to sing her the old songs. The ones about the tether."

"Why?"

"She didn't say." Ruth looks at the letter. "I sang them. All of them. She listened like you listen: like someone counting. When I'd finished, she gave me this, and she said: *If I don't come back, give it to the next surveyor. Not to the Office. To the surveyor.*" Ruth holds it out across the embers. "So. You're the next surveyor."

I take it. My hands are shaking. I break the seal.

It's short. Much shorter than the note at Thirteen. The handwriting is calmer, rounder, written by someone with time to sit at a table.

*To the next surveyor, from the last.*

*If you are reading this, I did not come back. Don't look for me under the stones. You won't find me there.*

*Walk the true line. Ruth will help you. Mara will too, though she'll pretend she won't.*

*And tell Ruth something for me, when she's ready to hear it. Tell her the songs are wrong about one thing.*

*J.M.*

I read it twice. Then a third time, because a third time is what you do when the first two scare you.

*The songs are wrong about one thing.*

I look up. Ruth is watching me. Her face gives nothing away.

"I never knew what she meant," she says quietly. "I've read it a hundred times since spring. I've sung every tether song I know, start to finish, looking for the mistake." Her eyes rest on mine. "Until last night."

Neither of us says anything at all.

*Humans don't catch the tether,* Mara said, on the rock above the ravine. *Everybody knows that. It's in every song.*

*Don't look for me under the stones. You won't find me there.*

"Ruth," I say. My voice comes out strange. "Did June—"

"I don't know." Ruth's voice is flat and final. "I don't know, and I won't guess, and you won't either. Not out loud. Not here." She sits back into her furs, and suddenly she looks very small and very old and very tired. "There are enough dangerous things in this hall already."

I fold the letter. I put it inside my shirt, against my skin, next to my heart.

"One more thing," says Ruth.

"Yes?"

"A runner came up from Stonebridge at dawn, while you were sleeping." Her mouth goes thin. "The Office has decided you need help. They're sending a second surveyor. To *assist* you." She says the word as if it tastes of silver. "He'll meet you at Cairn Fifteen, the day after tomorrow."

"On his own?"

"No," says Ruth. "With Theo of River."


# Chapter Eighteen

We leave the hold the next morning, and Mara and I have not talked about the kiss.

We haven't talked about anything. Not about the kiss, or the wolf at the edge of the trees, or the gold in our eyes, or what Ruth said to me in the empty hall. When I came back from the hall yesterday, Mara was awake and waiting on the step of the guesthouse, and she looked at my face and opened her mouth, and I said, "Your mother knows," and she closed it again.

That was all. She nodded once. She went very pale. Then she went to help Lena with the woodpile, and I went to my long table in the hall and drew the true line until my hand cramped, and we didn't look at each other across the fire at supper.

It's not that I don't want to talk to her. I want to so badly it's like a stone in my chest. It's that every time I look at her, I think about Ruth saying *you go home, and you don't come back*, and about nodding, and about not telling her.

She can feel it. I know she can. Not the words; just the shape of it. Guilt, sitting in me like a swallowed coin.

She hasn't asked. That's almost worse.

That first night, coming back from the hold to the line, we camp in a hollow under the last of the big larches, and she lays her blanket on one side of the fire and I lay mine on the other, and we lie down without a word. It's the bothy all over again, except backwards. Further apart, instead of closer.

I wake in the dark, deep in the night, because I'm warm.

I shouldn't be. The fire is out. The frost has come down hard; I can see it on the needles by starlight, a fine white fur over everything. My breath is smoking. But I'm warm, all the way through, and it takes me a stupid, sleepy moment to understand why.

There are two blankets on me. Mine, and hers.

I lift my head. She's on the far side of the dead fire, curled on her side on the bare needles, her coat pulled tight around her, her knees drawn up. Her eyes are closed. She isn't asleep. I can feel it: the slow careful breathing of someone pretending.

She's cold. I can feel that too. A deep ache in her hands and feet, the same ache I'd have if I were lying where she is.

She knows I can feel it. She did it anyway.

I lie there with her blanket pulled up to my chin, smelling of woodsmoke and resin and her, and something in my chest gives way like a step on scree.

I get up. I take her blanket across the dead fire and lay it over her, and tuck it round her shoulders, and she doesn't open her eyes, and she doesn't say anything. Neither do I. I go back to my side and lie down.

A long time later, out of the dark, she says very quietly, "Stubborn."

"You started it," I say.

I'm going to tell her. Everything Ruth said, and everything I said back. Not tonight. But soon. Before this turns into one more thing someone decided for her.

---

The line north of Thirteen climbs.

It's been climbing all along, really, in long slow rises and sudden steps. But past Fourteen it stops pretending. The larches thin out and shrink, gnarled and wind-bent, clinging to the rock in twisted knots. Then they stop altogether, and there's only grass, short and tough and silver with frost in the mornings, and grey rock, and sky. The air is thin. I'm breathing twice as hard as usual and getting half as much for it.

"Above the tree line," Mara says, when she sees me stop for the third time in an hour. "It's like this until Twenty."

"Lovely," I say. "Splendid. Can't wait."

She almost smiles. Almost.

It takes us the rest of that first day just to get back to the line from the hold. We chain Fourteen in the early afternoon of the second. It's a true cairn: old mark, June's initials, the line through the circle pointing exactly where June's book says it should. I stand beside it and look back down the ridge, south, toward the gold blur of the forest below, the grey gash of the ravine, and further off, out of sight, the toppled stones of the true Nine under their fallen larch.

"We'll have to go back," I say. "For Nine. And Twelve."

June's book is very clear. In the stretch from Nine to Fourteen, two cairns were moved: Nine, and then Twelve, both pulled down and rebuilt inward, both stamped *S.O.* We walked the Office Nine and the Office Twelve on the way up, because I didn't know better yet. To file a true survey, I'll have to chain the true ones.

"My mother's sending people," Mara says. "To rebuild them. Lena's going, with six others. They'll have the stones back up by the time you come down."

"I'll chain them on the way back. To Stonebridge. It's the same road." I look at her. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Thank my mother." She coils the chain handle around her fist. Then, without looking at me: "She told you to go home. Didn't she."

The wind comes hard off the high ground, cold enough to make my eyes water.

"Yes," I say.

Mara nods slowly. She doesn't look surprised. She looks like someone who's been carrying a sum in her head for a day and has just had it confirmed.

"And you said yes."

"I said I'd keep you alive." It comes out rough. "That's not quite the same thing."

She looks at me then. Her eyes are amber in the daylight, no gold, nothing anyone could see. But I can feel what's behind them. It isn't anger. It's something much more tired.

"Everybody's always keeping me alive," she says. "June. My mother. You." She turns away, toward the next stretch of line. "Nobody ever asks what I want."

"What do you want?"

She stops. Her back is to me. The wind whips the end of her braid sideways like a flag.

"Chain," she says. "We're losing the light."

---

Fifteen sits on a bare saddle of rock between two higher peaks, with a long view west over River's valleys and east over a tumbled wilderness of grey ridges and black tarns. It's the highest cairn on the line so far. It's also, I realise as we come up the last slope toward it in the cold blue afternoon, already occupied.

There's a fire. A small, neat one, sheltered behind the cairn. There are two tents, good canvas, River-made. There's a pony, shaggy and miserable, hobbled in the lee of a boulder.

And there's Theo, sitting on a folding stool by the fire with a cup in his hand, looking as if he's been waiting for us all his life.

"Mara!" he calls. "Surveyor! Come in, come in. You look frozen. Tea?"

Mara stops dead on the slope. I feel her go rigid all the way through.

"Steady," I say, under my breath.

"I'm steady."

"You're showing your teeth."

She closes her mouth.

There's someone else at the fire. He gets up as we come closer: a lean man in his forties, sandy-haired, clean-shaven, wearing a good grey Office coat buttoned all the way up. His face is narrow and pleasant and forgettable. He has a satchel across his chest just like mine, and a chain coiled at his feet just like mine, brass and silver, brand new, shining.

And his fingers are stained with ink up to the second knuckle.

"Miss Reed," he says. He holds out his hand. "Ambrose Holt. Survey Office, second class. The Registrar has asked me to assist you."

I shake it. His hand is cold. His grip is firm and dry and short.

Beside me, Mara draws in a long, slow breath through her nose.

I feel the tether hum, a sudden hard spike of alarm. I glance sideways. Mara's nostrils have flared, and her lips move, just barely, without sound. I'd know the shape of those words anywhere now. *Lamp oil.*

"Assist me with what, Mr. Holt?" I ask, smiling.

"With your survey, of course." He smiles back. It's a very thin smile. "The Highwood line is long, the season's late, and you're on your first posting. The Registrar felt it would be prudent to have a second pair of eyes. Simply to confirm your readings against the Office copy." He nods at my satchel. "You have the Office copy, I take it?"

"I do."

"Excellent. Then we're in agreement about which line we're walking." His eyes go to Mara, briefly, and then back to me, and stay there. "And the Registrar was very clear on that point. The Office line. Cairn to cairn. As recorded."

The fire crackles. Behind Holt, Theo takes a sip of his tea and watches me over the rim of the cup with bright, interested blue eyes, the way you'd watch a game of cards when you've already seen everyone's hand.

"Of course," I say.

---

We make camp on the saddle, because there's nowhere else to make it. Holt has his tent. Theo has his. Mara and I have the lee side of a boulder, a good twenty yards away, and our blankets.

I sit with my back to the rock and watch the light go off the peaks, gold and then rose and then grey. Mara sits beside me. Not sixty-six feet away. Right beside me, our shoulders almost touching, because there's nowhere else to sit.

"Is it him?" I ask quietly. "From the ravine?"

"No." She keeps her voice very low. "The one at the ravine was heavier. Bigger feet. And he was afraid." A pause. "This one isn't afraid."

"But the lamp oil."

"Same lamp oil," she says. "Same paper. The same place." She turns her head and looks across the saddle at the neat Office tent, where a lamp has been lit, and Holt's narrow shadow moves on the canvas. "He works where the other one works. He's been in the same rooms. Sat at the same desks."

"The Office."

"The Office."

Across the fire, Theo has stood up. He's stretching, yawning, a big lazy theatrical yawn. Then he strolls, unhurried, across the frozen grass toward us, and stops a polite distance off, with his hands in his pockets.

"Cosy," he says.

"What do you want, Theo?" says Mara.

"Me? Nothing. I'm just the escort." He grins. "Holt asked River to see him up the mountain. River said yes. River always says yes to the Office." His eyes rest on me. "You didn't take my advice, surveyor."

"Which advice?"

"At Seven. *Measure twice.*" He tilts his head. "Or did you?"

I don't say anything. Neither does Mara.

Theo looks at us both for a long, thoughtful moment. Then his gaze drops, just for a second, to the narrow strip of ground between our shoulders. To the place where they almost touch.

Something goes across his face too fast to read.

"Well," he says lightly. "Sleep well. Big day tomorrow. Holt's very keen to see your numbers."

He turns to go. Then he stops, the way he did on the slope at Seven, and looks back.

"Oh. And Holt asked me to pass this on. From the Registrar himself." He smiles. "He was very sorry to hear about your accident at the ravine. He hopes your hand is healing."

He walks away, back toward the fire.

I sit frozen against the boulder.

My hand. My bandaged left hand, resting in my lap.

Nobody told the Office about the ravine. No runner has gone down from the hold. Ruth wouldn't. Mara wouldn't. I haven't written a single page since Thirteen that isn't in the bottom of my pack under my socks.

There's only one way Voss could know about the accident at the ravine.

Beside me, Mara has gone so still she's barely breathing. Through the tether, I feel her understand it a heartbeat after I do.

"He knows," I whisper, "because he sent it."


# Chapter Nineteen

I count the links in my chain every morning. I have done since I was twenty-one, since my second week of training, when Agnes Bell caught me chaining a practice line with a twisted link and made me walk the whole thing again, all forty chains of it, in the rain. *Count it,* she said. *Every morning. Before you trust it with anything.*

So on the saddle at Fifteen, with frost on the grass and Holt's tent still shut and the sky behind the eastern peaks going from black to bruise to pale cold blue, I sit on my blanket and run my chain through my hands, link by link, the way I always do.

Ten. The first silver link, cold as ice.

Twenty. Thirty. Forty.

Mara is sitting a few feet away, watching me. She's been watching me do this for two weeks. She stopped asking why after the second morning.

Seventy. Eighty. Ninety.

Ninety-nine.

I stop.

I go back to the first brass handle. I start again, slower. I count out loud this time, under my breath, the way I did on the scree slope. Mara has gone still. I can feel her listening.

Ninety-nine.

"What?" she says.

"It's short." My voice is very calm. "There should be a hundred. There are ninety-nine."

"You miscounted."

"I don't miscount."

I run the chain through my fingers a third time, looking now instead of counting, looking at every join. Each link in a surveyor's chain is a length of brass wire bent into a long loop at each end, and the loops are joined with little rings. You can open a ring with pliers. You can close it again. If you're careful and you know what you're doing, nobody would ever see.

Between the forty-third and the forty-fourth link, someone wasn't quite careful enough. The little ring has been opened and closed. There are fresh tool marks on it: two tiny bright bites in the old brass, where the jaws of a pair of pliers gripped and turned.

I hold it up so Mara can see.

She looks at it for a long time. Then she looks across the frozen grass at Holt's tent.

"Last night," she says. "He came over to talk to you. About your *readings*. He sat by your pack."

"I was asleep."

"I wasn't." Her mouth thins. "I watched him the whole time. He didn't touch your pack. I'd have seen." A pause. "He touched the chain. When he picked it up, to admire it. He said it was a lovely old chain. He held it for a minute. He was talking the whole time."

I remember. Half asleep, I'd heard Holt's voice by the fire, pleasant and dull, saying something about Office standards and old brass, and I hadn't even opened my eyes.

A minute. That's all you'd need, with practice and small pliers in your pocket. One ring open, one link out, one ring closed again.

"Why?" Mara asks. "One link. What does one link do?"

"One link is eight inches," I say. "Out of sixty-six feet. It's nothing. You'd never see it in one length. But every length I chain with this, I'm calling sixty-six feet when it's really sixty-five feet and four inches. Across a mile, that's—" I do it in my head. "Fifty-three feet out. Across the four miles of the true line, it's two hundred feet. Every cairn I set would be further out than it really is."

"Further out." Mara frowns. "That's *more* forest."

"On my survey. Yes." I look down at the bright little tool marks. "And then he checks my survey against his chain, which is new and perfect and Office-standard. And all my distances are wrong. All of them. Every single one, too long, in Highwood's favour." I close my fist around the chain. "And he stands up in the map room in Stonebridge and says, *I'm afraid Miss Reed's measurements cannot be trusted. Her chain was short. And look who it favoured.*"

Mara stares at me.

"He wasn't trying to stop me walking the true line," I say. "He was making sure that if I did, nobody would believe it."

---

I have a spare link. Every surveyor carries a little tin of them, and rings, and a pair of pliers of their own. I sit on my blanket and mend the chain with cold fingers, and then I count it again, all hundred links, and then I get up and walk across the frozen grass to Holt's tent.

He's already up. He's sitting on Theo's folding stool by the remains of the fire, writing in a small black notebook, and he looks up as I come over with a pleasant, forgettable smile.

"Miss Reed. Good morning. I hope you slept—"

"My chain had ninety-nine links this morning," I say.

His smile doesn't change at all. "I beg your pardon?"

"Ninety-nine. It had a hundred last night, and this morning it had ninety-nine. The ring between forty-three and forty-four has been opened with pliers." I hold out my hand. "I'd like my link back, please."

"I'm afraid I don't understand."

"I think you do."

Behind him, Theo's tent flap opens. Theo comes out, tousled and blinking, a blanket round his shoulders, and stops dead, and his eyes go from me to Holt to me again, and he looks delighted.

"Miss Reed," says Holt patiently, "you're tired. You're on your first posting, in very difficult country, and you've been through a frightening experience at the ravine. It's perfectly natural to miscount."

"I don't miscount."

"Everyone miscounts."

"I don't," I say. "I've counted my chain every morning for six years, through rain and hail and one memorable bout of food poisoning. I counted it every morning on this line. It's the one thing I do every day without fail, and it's the one thing on this mountain I'm absolutely sure of." I keep my hand out. "So. My link."

Holt looks at my hand. Then he looks at me. For a moment something comes into his narrow pleasant face, something cold and quick and assessing. Then it's gone, and he smiles again, and shakes his head sadly.

"I'll note your concern in my report," he says.

"Do," I say. "Note it very precisely. Ninety-nine links. Between forty-three and forty-four. Fresh pliers marks on the ring." I lower my hand. "And note that from now on, I'll be counting my chain every night as well as every morning. And so will my escort."

Theo laughs out loud. He turns it into a cough very quickly.

Holt doesn't laugh. He closes his little black notebook and puts it inside his coat and looks at me with no expression at all.

"Shall we begin?" he says. "Cairn Sixteen. The Office bearing, I believe."

---

We chain Sixteen, Seventeen and Eighteen with Holt walking at my shoulder like a bad smell. He checks every length with his own chain. He writes in his notebook. He compares my bearings to the Office copy, out loud, in a mild voice, and every time they agree, he nods and says, *Very good*, as if he's surprised.

They agree because between Fourteen and Nineteen, the Office line and the true line are the same. They agree because I haven't done anything wrong yet.

Tomorrow we reach Nineteen.

Mara and I argue about it that night, in whispers, behind our boulder, while Holt's lamp burns in his tent and Theo sits by the fire pretending not to listen.

"Don't tell him," she says.

"I have to. At Nineteen, the lines split. The true cairn is a quarter mile out. If I chain to it, he'll see. He's standing *right next to me*, Mara."

"Then don't chain to it. Not with him there. Chain the Office line, let him write his little notes, let him go back down. Then we come back up on our own and do it properly."

"There isn't time. First snow—"

"Then we lose a few cairns!" Her whisper cracks. "We get back the Long Wood and the Pools and we lose Nineteen to Twenty-Four. That's still more forest than Highwood has had in my lifetime. My mother would take that. She'd *weep* for that."

"And when I file it? When I stand in the map room with a survey that's true in one place and false in another, because I didn't want to be seen?" I shake my head. "That's not a survey, Mara. That's a negotiation. That's what the man who moved our fence did. He measured what he was paid to measure and he didn't measure the rest."

"That's not the same."

"It's exactly the same. It's just in your favour." I'm shaking. I can't stop. "I'm a surveyor. I chain what's true. I chain it in front of whoever's watching. If Holt wants to see me do it, he can watch. If he runs to Voss, *let him*. Voss already knows. Voss knew about the ravine before anyone told him. Voss sent the—" I stop. I make my voice go down. "It doesn't matter what Holt sees. It only matters what I file."

She looks at me in the dark for a long time.

"They'll kill you," she says. It's what Ruth said. It's what she said, on the rock above the ravine. "You said it yourself. They'll only have to manage one of us."

"I know."

"And you'll do it anyway."

"I'd do it if you weren't here," I say. "I'd have done it if I'd never met you. That's the point. That's why it's worth anything." I swallow. "Your mother asked me to keep you alive. She didn't ask me to stop being a surveyor. I don't think she'd want me to. I don't think *you* would."

Mara makes a sound like she's been hit.

"No," she says at last, very low. "I wouldn't."

She doesn't say anything else. She lies down with her back to me, close, closer than she has since the bothy. After a while I lie down too. Our backs touch. Neither of us moves away.

---

I wake before dawn because the needle has moved.

That's how it feels. Not pain. Distance. All night the compass in my chest has pointed at the warm weight against my back, steady as north. Now it's swung right round and is pointing away, northeast, a long way off, and the space beside me is cold.

I sit up. Mara is gone. Her blanket is gone. Her bow is gone.

Across the saddle, the flap of Holt's tent is hanging open in the wind. The tent is empty. The pony is gone. And my pack has been moved. It's lying on its side, a yard from where I left it, the top flap unbuckled.

I'm on my knees beside it before I know I've moved. I tear it open.

June's book is there. Still at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, under my socks. He didn't go that deep. My Office copy of the line is gone. And my own field book, the one I keep in the outside pocket, where I write the day's numbers, and at the back, on the private pages, my working: the old bearings, the sketch of the toppled Nine, my notes about the recut groove on the map-room floor, the pry bar, *Office told us there'd be no trouble on this stretch.*

Gone.

Theo is standing at the door of his own tent, wrapped in his blanket, looking at Holt's empty one. For once, he isn't smiling.

"Where's Mara?" I say.

He looks at me. "She went after him. An hour ago. I heard her go." He hesitates. "He went northeast. Off the line. Down toward the sinkholes."

I don't need him to tell me. I've known since I opened my eyes. The needle hasn't stopped pointing: northeast, a long way off, moving fast and low, downhill.

Toward the sinkholes. Toward the country nobody holds.

"That's the Hollow," says Theo quietly. "That's all open ground."


# Chapter Twenty

"I'll come with you," says Theo.

I'm already moving. I've rolled my blanket, I've shoved June's book back into the bottom of my pack, I've slung the chain over my shoulder because I'm not leaving it anywhere near this saddle ever again. I stop with one strap half buckled and look at him.

"Why?"

"Because you'll die," he says. "You don't know the sinkholes. Nobody goes into the Hollow unless they have to, and the ones who have to don't come out the same." For once, he sounds like he means exactly what he's saying. "I know some of the paths. Not many. Some."

I look at him. At his sunburned face and his clever blue eyes, and the walking stick he's never once had to hit anyone with.

"You brought Holt up here," I say.

"The Office asked. River said yes." He doesn't look away. "That's what River does. It doesn't mean I knew what he'd do."

"Did you?"

A pause. Just a fraction too long.

"I guessed," says Theo.

I finish buckling the strap.

"Go to the hold," I say. "Tell Ruth what's happened. Tell her Holt has my field book and he's heading for the Hollow. Tell her Mara's gone after him." I pull the pack onto my shoulders. "If you do that, I'll believe you didn't know."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I'll know you did."

Theo studies me. Then he laughs, a short, unhappy sound, and shakes his head.

"Gods," he says. "She really did find the right one."

Before I can ask what that means, he's turned away and is calling for his two silent companions, and I'm already walking.

*She.* I turn it over as I go, the way I'd turn over a bad reading. Not *you found*, or *Mara found*. *She* found. As if someone had been looking. As if someone had sent a surveyor up the Highwood line on purpose, hoping.

Agnes Bell, in her office in the capital, telling me the post was mine if I wanted it. June, writing *to the next surveyor* on a scrap of paper and sealing it in a cairn. Ruth, singing the tether songs to a woman who listened like she was counting.

I don't know which *she* he meant. I put it in a column, for later. I don't have room for it now.

---

I don't follow a path. There isn't one. I follow the needle.

It's pointing northeast, steady, and I walk toward it, off the saddle, down a long slope of frost-silvered grass toward the tumbled grey country below. The sun comes up behind the eastern peaks and lights the whole wilderness in front of me red and gold, and I see the Hollow for the first time.

On the floor map in Stonebridge it's a blank. No ochre lines. No cairn numbers. Just slate, pitted with small round marks like the holes in a pumice stone, and nothing else.

From up here, it looks like the surface of the moon.

It's a vast, rumpled bowl of grey rock and brown scrub, and all across it, everywhere, there are holes. Round black mouths in the ground, dozens of them, hundreds, some no bigger than a well, some wide enough to swallow the Highwood hall. Some have steep rock sides dropping straight down into darkness. Some are funnels of loose earth and scree. Out of some of them, white steam rises in slow threads and hangs in the cold air. And the smell comes up the slope to meet me: rotten eggs, hot stone, sulphur.

Somewhere down there, in all of that, is Mara.

I can feel her. A long way off, near the middle of the bowl. She's moving. Then, as I watch, she stops.

I start down.

---

The ground in the Hollow is wrong. That's the only way I can describe it. It looks solid, grey rock and dry scrub, until you put your foot on it and feel it give, very slightly, like a floorboard over an empty room. There are cracks everywhere, some wide enough to put my hand in, and when I lean over them I can hear water running somewhere far below, and feel warm air breathing up into my face.

I give the sinkholes a wide berth. I count my steps. I test every stretch of ground with the end of my chain, swinging the brass handle ahead of me like a blind man's stick. It's slow. It's much too slow.

Mara isn't moving any more.

I don't like that. I tell myself it doesn't mean anything. She's resting. She's hiding. She's crouched behind a rock, watching Holt, waiting for her moment. She can't change out here; she knows she's slow and soft and visible, so she's being careful. That's all.

Then the pain comes.

It hits me in the side, low, under the ribs: a blow, hard and blunt, like a kick. I gasp and go down on one knee in the scrub. Before I can get up, there's another one, in the same place. Then a sharp, tearing burn across both wrists at once, tight and hot. Rope.

Then something worse. A cold, deep, sickening ache at the back of my head, and the world goes grey at the edges, and for a moment the needle in my chest goes faint, and wavers.

"No," I say out loud. "*No.* Mara—"

It steadies. She's there. She's still there. Dazed, hurting, but there. She hasn't moved. She can't move.

They've got her.

---

I stop testing the ground.

I can't afford to any more. I run. I run across the Hollow toward the needle with the chain bouncing on my shoulder and my pack thumping on my back, and I jump cracks I'd have walked a hundred yards around an hour ago. I skid down a funnel of loose scree on my backside and scramble up the other side on my hands and knees. Steam from a vent hits me full in the face, hot and stinking, and I choke and keep going. My bandaged hand is bleeding again. I can feel it, warm and wet inside the cloth. I don't care.

Then the ground runs out.

It's a sinkhole. A big one, steep-sided, its walls dropping straight down into darkness so deep I can't see the bottom. It lies across my path, a black mouth twenty yards wide, and the needle points straight across it, to the far side.

There's only one way across. A bridge.

Not a real one. It's a natural rib of rock, a ledge maybe two feet wide, arching from one lip of the sinkhole to the other. Its top is cracked and pitted and furred with moss. It has no rail. It has nothing on either side but air, and below the air, darkness, and from the darkness, very faintly, the sound of water.

I stand at the edge of it and look down.

I shouldn't. I know I shouldn't. Every instructor I ever had, every manual, every voice in my head says *don't look down*, and I look down anyway, and the dark goes on and on and on, and my knees turn to water and my stomach turns inside out and my vision closes in until there's nothing but the black.

Twenty feet below me, a stone breaks loose from the lip and falls. I listen for it to hit the bottom.

I don't hear it hit the bottom.

*I can't,* I think. *I can't. I can't do this.*

On the far side of the sinkhole, a long way off, someone hits Mara again.

I feel it in my jaw. Hard enough to make my teeth click together. Hard enough that, for a second, the tether goes white with it, and I taste blood that isn't mine.

I step onto the bridge.

---

I don't count steps. I count links.

I unloop the chain from my shoulder and hold the first brass handle in my good hand, and let the rest of it trail behind me along the narrow rock, clinking. And I count each link as it slides through my fingers. One. Two. Three. Each one is eight inches. Eight inches of brass. Eight inches is one step, if I make my steps small. I make my steps very small.

Ten. Silver. Cold against my palm.

I don't look down. I look at the chain. At the links. At my own hand. The bridge is wet. My boot slips once and I go down on one knee on the rock, and the chain rattles over the edge, and for a second I'm absolutely sure I'm going to follow it.

I don't. I stay where I am, on my knees, on the cold wet stone, with nothing on either side of me but the dark.

Twenty. Thirty.

I can feel her. On the far side. That's the thing. That's the only thing. The needle is pointing straight ahead, across the gap, steady as a struck line. I can't see where I'm going. I don't need to. I know exactly where it is.

*So you'd never lose each other in the dark,* her grandmother said.

Forty. Fifty. My legs are shaking so hard I can hear my knees knocking. I keep going. I crawl when I have to. I stand when I can.

Sixty. Seventy. The rock under my hands changes, getting rougher, drier, wider. Eighty. Ninety.

A hundred.

I'm on the other side.

I lie on my face on solid ground, the whole chain coiled in a heap beside me, and breathe. I'm sobbing. I don't care about that either.

Then I get up.

---

They're in a hollow, a quarter mile on, in the shelter of a ring of boulders around a steaming vent. I smell their fire before I see it. Pitch. Burning tar.

I come up on them the way I've watched Mara come up on things for two weeks: low, slow, keeping to the shadows, keeping downwind, not breathing. I crouch behind a boulder at the lip of the hollow and look down.

Three hunters. The same three. The grey-cropped woman, and the man whose arm I wrapped in my chain at Ten, his forearm bandaged in a filthy strip of cloth, and the third man, younger, with the crossbow. There's a fire, and a cookpot, and Holt's miserable pony tied to a stunted tree.

No Holt.

And in the middle of the hollow, by the fire, there's a post. Just a thick stake of wood driven into a crack in the rock. Mara is tied to it.

She's on her knees, her hands bound behind the post, her head down. Her braid has come completely undone, black hair hanging over her face. There's blood at the corner of her mouth. Her bow is lying on the ground ten feet away, its string cut. Her quiver is beside it, empty, red-fletched arrows scattered across the rock.

On a flat stone beside the fire, open, its pages riffling in the warm wind off the vent, is my field book.

The grey-cropped woman is standing over Mara with something in her hand. A knife. Short and broad-bladed, and as she turns it the firelight runs along the edge, and it isn't steel.

It's silver.

"Triple," she's saying. Pleasantly, conversationally, the way you'd talk about the weather. "For the heir. The Guild'll want proof. A hand, maybe. Or an ear." She crouches, and lifts Mara's chin with the flat of the blade, and Mara flinches, and I feel the cold of the silver against my own throat. "Which do you think, Highwood? Which do you need least?"

Mara lifts her head. She looks at the woman through the curtain of her hair.

Then she looks past her. Up. Straight at the boulder where I'm hiding.

She can't see me. I know she can't. But she knows exactly where I am, the same way I know exactly where she is. And her eyes, in the firelight, are wide and wet and furious and full of one single word.

*Run.*

I don't run.


# Chapter Twenty-One

I have a chain, a pry bar, a bad hand, and no plan at all.

I crouch behind the boulder and make one. Fast, the way you'd do a sum when the examiner is already walking up the aisle to take your paper.

Three hunters. Two crossbows: the young man has one in his lap, and the woman's is propped against a rock by the fire. The man with the bandaged arm doesn't seem to have one; maybe he can't draw it any more. I feel a small, mean flicker of satisfaction about that, and then I put it away. The silver knife is in the woman's hand. Mara is tied to the post. My field book is on the flat stone by the fire. And the pony is hobbled to the stunted tree on the far side of the hollow, rolling its eyes at the steam.

And above the hunters, on the lip of the hollow, there's a boulder.

Not mine. Another one, ten yards to my left. It's big, as big as a cart, and it sits right at the very edge of the slope, tilted a little forward, on a bed of loose scree and crumbling earth. It looks as if it's been thinking about falling into that hollow for about a thousand years.

I unlash the pry bar from my pack.

*Whoever held this was frightened,* Ruth said. *The ones who are frightened make mistakes.*

I am very frightened. I'm going to try not to make one.

---

I crawl to the boulder on my belly. Below me the woman is still talking. She's laughing now, crouched in front of Mara, turning the silver knife so it throws firelight across Mara's face. Mara isn't looking at her. Mara is looking at me. I can feel it through the tether: furious, desperate, pleading. *Go. Go. Go.*

I wedge the flat end of the pry bar into the crack under the boulder. I feel for the place where it rests on the scree, the place where all its weight is balanced. It's not hard to find. Rock is just numbers. Weight and angle and the place where they meet.

I lean on the bar.

Nothing.

I lean harder. My bad hand screams. Down in the hollow Mara flinches, and the woman notices and laughs again, thinking she did it.

I put my whole body on the end of the bar, and I think about a fence at the bottom of a field, and a stream, and a magistrate's desk, and my father's face. And something in the scree under the boulder gives way with a long, gritty sigh.

The boulder moves. An inch. Then another. Then it tips, all at once, and goes over the lip.

It doesn't hit anyone. I made sure of that. I aimed it at the far side of the hollow, away from the post, away from the fire. But it hits the slope with a sound like a door slamming in the side of the world, and it takes half the slope with it, and it all comes down in a roaring avalanche of scree and dust and rock straight toward the stunted tree where the pony is tied.

The pony screams and rears and tears the tree half out of the ground and bolts, dragging it, straight through the middle of the camp.

---

Everything happens at once.

The young man with the crossbow leaps up and fires at nothing and the bolt goes into the steam. The man with the bandaged arm goes over backwards into the cookpot. The pony goes through the fire and out the other side, trailing sparks and bits of tree, and the woman jumps sideways to get out of its way, and as she does it I'm already sliding down the slope on my backside in a shower of grit with the chain in my good hand.

She sees me. She turns, knife up.

I swing.

It's not like at Ten. That was instinct. This is a surveyor's throw, the one I've practised a thousand times, the long low flick that sends the chain out straight along the ground. It hisses across the rock, and the last six feet of it whip up and wrap round her wrist, and I yank.

The silver knife flies out of her hand and skitters across the stone.

I don't stop. I run past her, past the fire, to the post. Mara is already on her feet, pulling against the ropes, and I see the silver knife lying an arm's length from her boot and I grab it. My fingers close on the handle. It's horn, thank every god in the Reach, not silver. I cut the ropes on her wrists in two strokes.

She's free. She staggers, catches herself on the post, and then she's moving, and she's not slow and soft and useless, she's Mara, even out here, even in human form. She snatches up two arrows from the rock as she passes, and drives one of them point-first into the young man's thigh as he swings his crossbow round, and he goes down shrieking.

"*The book,*" I shout.

I grab it off the flat stone. It's hot from the fire. I shove it inside my shirt.

"Cass!"

The woman is up. She's got her crossbow. She's swinging it round toward us, and her face is a mask of fury, and there's nowhere to go.

Mara takes my hand, and pulls, and we run straight into the steam.

---

I don't know how long we run. I don't know where. Mara leads, and I follow, through the hot white blind fog of the vents, round the black mouths of sinkholes, down funnels of loose earth and up the other side. Crossbow bolts hiss past us twice and then not again. The shouting fades behind us. Then there's only our breathing, and the steam, and the rotten-egg stink of sulphur, and Mara's hand locked around mine like she will never, ever let go.

When we finally stop, we're in a cave.

It's not big. A low opening in the side of a rock wall, half hidden behind a curtain of steam, and inside, a single chamber, round and warm and dim. The walls glisten wet. The air is thick and hot and smells of minerals, of stone and iron and something faintly sweet. And in the middle of the floor there's a pool.

It's a hot spring. Bright blue, the blue of a summer sky, clear as glass all the way down to a pale stone bottom. Steam rises off it in slow, lazy curls. A thin stream of water trickles into it from a crack in the wall at the back, and out of it through a channel in the floor at the front, and the sound fills the little cave like a song.

Mara lets go of my hand. She sits down on the warm stone at the edge of the pool, very suddenly, as if her legs have stopped working.

"They won't follow," she says. Her voice is hoarse. "Not in here. They're afraid of the Hollow's caves. And the steam hides our scent."

I sit down beside her. I'm shaking. I think I'm going to be shaking for the rest of my life.

"You're hurt," I say.

"So are you."

"Mine's just my hand. Yours is—" I can feel it. All of it. The deep aching bruise under her ribs, two of them, where they kicked her. The swelling at the back of her skull. The split lip. The raw, burning stripes on both wrists where the rope bit. And under everything, the old slow grind of the silver burn. "Yours is everything."

She doesn't argue. She just looks at me, in the blue light from the pool, with her black hair loose and tangled all round her face and blood drying at the corner of her mouth.

"You crossed the sinkhole," she says.

"Yes."

"The bridge. I felt it. I felt you on it." Her voice cracks. "I felt you *look down*."

"I counted links."

"You *idiot*." Her eyes are wet. "You absolute—I told you to run. I told you. I thought it so hard I could feel it in my teeth—"

"I know. I felt it." I take a breath. "I didn't run."

"Why?"

And I think: *because I'd do it if you weren't here.* That's what I said on the saddle. *I'd have done it if I'd never met you.*

It was true about the survey. It isn't true about this.

"Because it was you," I say.

---

She kisses me.

It's not like the guesthouse. That was a storm, a rockfall, the whole mountain coming down at once. This is slower. This is careful. Her mouth is split and it hurts her, I can feel it hurt her, and she kisses me anyway, gently, as if I'm the one who might break. Her hand comes up and cups the side of my face. Her thumb moves along my cheekbone, the way it did at Ten, wiping away mud that isn't there.

"I want you," she says against my mouth. Very quietly. "You asked me. On the line. What I want." A breath. "That's it. That's all. I've wanted it since the gate. Since you put your hand out and I didn't take it."

I can't speak. I don't need to. She can feel it, all of it, every bit of my answer, and it goes back into her and doubles, and comes back to me doubled again.

We help each other out of our clothes. It takes a long time, because we're both hurt and both shaking and neither of us can stop touching the other long enough to manage a button. I peel the filthy bandage off her wrist and press my lips, very lightly, to the edge of the silver burn, and I feel her feel it, a shiver that goes through both of us at once. She unwinds the cloth from my palm and looks at the long cut across it, and kisses the base of my thumb.

Then she takes my good hand and leads me down into the water.

It's hot. Almost too hot. It closes over my hips and my waist and my shoulders like a long breath out, and every ache in my body loosens at once. I hear Mara make a low sound beside me, half pain and half relief, as it reaches her ribs. Steam curls round us. The blue light moves on the walls.

She pulls me in.

I've never been touched like this. I've never touched anyone like this. Her skin is hot and wet and smooth under my hands, and I find the three pale scars along her collarbone and trace them with my fingertips, and she shivers and presses her mouth to my neck. Her hands move down my back, slow, learning me, the way I'd walk a line for the first time. And everything I feel, she feels. Everything she feels comes back to me. When she touches me I feel her hand, and I feel what it's like for her to touch me. When I kiss the hollow of her throat I feel her pulse jump under my lips, and feel it jump in my own throat at the same moment. It doubles back and doubles back, like an echo in the ravine, until I can't tell where she ends and I begin.

"Cass," she breathes. "Cass. *Cass.*"

She says my name like a song. Like a border she's singing.

I lay her back against the smooth warm stone at the edge of the pool, in the shallows, the water lapping at her shoulders, and I take my time. I'm a surveyor. I'm careful. I map her. Her collarbone, her breasts, the long hard line of her stomach, every place that makes her gasp and every place that makes her laugh, low and breathless, and pull my hair. I find the places that make her arch up off the stone. I go back to them. When I move my mouth lower, she makes a sound I've never heard from her, wild and raw and helpless, and her hands fist in my hair, and the tether goes white and bright and roaring, and I feel it build in her and in me at the same time, the same wave, rising, until it breaks over both of us at once and I'm crying out against her skin and I can't tell whose voice it is.

Afterwards she pulls me up and kisses me, and laughs into my mouth, shaking, and then turns me over in the warm water with a strength that reminds me exactly what she is.

"My turn," she says.

She's not careful. She's Mara. She's slow, and then she isn't, and her mouth and her fingers find me with a certainty that makes me think she's been paying very close attention to the tether for two weeks. I come apart under her hands. Then again. The second time I'm gasping her name into the steam, holding onto her shoulders as if I'm on the bridge over the sinkhole, and she's holding me, and I'm not afraid of falling at all.

---

Later, much later, we lie in the shallows of the pool with our heads on the warm stone, tangled together, her arm across my waist and my face in her hair.

I can feel her heart. Slow now. Steady. Beating with mine, exactly in time.

"I'm not going back to the lowlands," I say into her hair.

She's quiet. The water trickles in at the back of the cave.

"I know," she says.

"Your mother—"

"I'll deal with my mother." She turns her head and presses her lips to my forehead. "Nobody's sending anybody away. Not any more."

I close my eyes. The water is warm. The steam drifts over us like a blanket. Somewhere outside, in the cold grey Hollow, three hunters are looking for us, and a man with ink-stained fingers is walking toward Stonebridge with an empty pack, and the snow is coming.

For now, I don't care. For now, the needle in my chest is pointing at the place where it always wants to point.

And she's right here.


# Chapter Twenty-Two

I wake up warm, with Mara's hair in my mouth, and for one long, slow, perfect moment I don't remember where I am.

Then I do. The cave. The pool. The steam lifting off the blue water in lazy curls. The thin grey light creeping in past the curtain of vapour at the entrance, which means it's morning, which means we've slept here all night, on a heap of our own damp clothes on the warm stone floor.

Mara is curled against my back. Her arm is across my waist, heavy and loose. Her breath is slow and warm against the nape of my neck. She's asleep, properly asleep this time, not pretending; I can feel it in the tether, a deep, still, quiet hum, like a lake with no wind on it.

I don't move. I lie there and feel her breathing, and feel my own heart beating in time with hers, and I think: *I could stay here. I could just stay here forever.*

Then my stomach growls so loudly it echoes off the cave walls.

Mara wakes up laughing.

---

We have one oatcake between us. It's crumbled to pieces in the bottom of my pack and tastes faintly of chain oil. We eat it sitting on the edge of the pool with our feet in the hot water, passing the crumbs back and forth, and it's the best thing I've ever eaten.

"Don't," says Mara.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

"I was going to say it was nice," I say. "That's all. *Nice*. A perfectly moderate word."

She bumps her shoulder against mine. Her bruises have come up overnight, livid and purple along her ribs and the side of her jaw, and I can feel every one of them in my own body, a low dull throb. My hand is aching too. But under the aches there's something else, something bright and steady and new, like the first morning light coming in past the steam. I don't know what to call it. I think it might be happiness.

I'm not used to it. I keep checking on it, the way you'd keep checking a new cut to see if it's still there.

It's still there.

After we've eaten, I rewrap her wrists.

I tear the cleanest strip I can find off the hem of my shirt and soak it in the pool, and kneel in front of her on the warm stone, and take her hands one at a time. The rope burns are bad: raw red bracelets, weeping at the edges, worse on the right where the old silver burn already was. When I lay the wet cloth on them she hisses through her teeth, and so do I, at exactly the same moment, and we both look up and catch each other's eyes and start laughing again like idiots.

"This is going to be very inconvenient," I say, wiping my eyes. "For the rest of our lives."

"Every time you stub your toe."

"Every time *you* get in a fight."

"I don't get in fights."

I look pointedly at her jaw.

"That wasn't a fight," says Mara. "That was an ambush. It's different."

"I'll make a note of the distinction."

I tie off the bandage on her left wrist and start on the right. My fingers are slow and careful. Hers are resting in my palm, loose and trusting, and I keep thinking about the gate, two weeks ago, and her looking at my outstretched hand like it might be wet. And now here she is, letting me hold both of hers, letting me hurt her a little to make it better.

I don't say any of that. I don't need to. When I look up, she's watching me with an expression I've never seen on anyone's face before, not pointed at me. Soft, and steady, and entirely unguarded.

"Done," I say, a little hoarsely.

"Thank you," says Mara.

It's the first time she's ever said it to me. I'm keeping count of that, too.

"We have to go back," Mara says.

"I know."

"To the line. To Nineteen." She looks down at her feet in the water. "And Holt's gone ahead of us, with whatever he read in your book."

I'd forgotten. Not the book; I'd never forget the book. It's lying on the stone beside me, dried out now, its pages buckled from the heat of the hunters' fire. I'd forgotten what Holt must have done with it before he handed it over. What he must have read.

I pick it up and turn to the back, to the private pages.

The sketch of the toppled Nine. The old bearings, side by side with the Office ones. *Why does River know about Nine?* My notes on the recut groove in the map room. The pry bar. *Lamp oil and paper.* And on the last page, written the night we found June's book on the ledge at Thirteen, in my own small cramped hand: *True line. File in front of witnesses. All four miles.*

"He knows," I say. "Everything. He knows I know about the cairns. He knows I know about the ravine. He knows I'm going to file the true line." I close the book. "He'll be halfway to Stonebridge by now. With a fast pony and a head start."

"He hasn't got a pony," Mara says. "You set it on fire."

"I didn't *set it on fire*. It ran *through* a fire. That's quite different."

"It was on fire."

"Briefly," I say, with dignity. "A little bit."

Mara laughs again. It's such a good sound. I want to bottle it.

Then her face changes. She's looking at me, and her eyes have gone dark and serious, and through the tether I feel something come up under the warmth like a cold current under a summer river.

"He'll tell Voss what you're going to file," she says. "But that's not the worst thing he could tell him."

I know what she means. I've been not thinking about it since I woke up.

"No," I say.

"If Holt saw anything, on the saddle. Or Theo." She swallows. "Theo looked at us, that night. I saw him look."

"I saw too."

"If Voss finds out what this is," she says, and touches her chest, and then reaches out and touches mine, very lightly, over my heart, "then the line doesn't matter. The four miles don't matter. None of it matters." Her hand stays where it is. "They'll kill me. And they'll take your hand. And everything you've done up here, every cairn, every length, it all just gets thrown away."

I put my hand over hers.

"I know," I say.

"So we should be careful. We should go back to how it was. Sixty-six feet." Her voice cracks on the number. "We should never have—"

"Mara."

She stops.

"Look at me," I say.

She does. And I look back at her, at her bruised jaw and her split lip and her black hair still damp and wild from the pool, and the faint shadow of the gold that I know is waiting there, under the amber, for the next moonrise.

"Do you regret it?" I ask.

The cave is very quiet. The water trickles in at the back and out at the front.

"No," she whispers.

"Neither do I." I tighten my hand on hers. "Not for a second. Not if they hang us both from the top lock tomorrow."

"Don't say that."

"I'm only saying it so you know." I take a breath. "There's something else. I have to tell you something. I promised myself I would, on the way up from the hold, and then everything happened, and—" I stop. Make myself start again. "Your mother asked me to go home. After the filing. Back to the lowlands, and never come back, and never tell you she'd asked. So you'd be safe."

Mara's face closes, the way a hand closes.

"I know," she says. "You told me. At Fourteen."

"I told you she asked. I didn't tell you all of it." I hold her gaze. "I told her no. Not in those words. I told her that sending someone away to keep them safe isn't keeping them safe. That it just means they don't get a say. And she said—" I almost smile. "She said *finish the line first, and then we'll talk about who gets a say.*"

For a long moment Mara doesn't move at all.

Then she makes a sound, half laugh and half sob, and drops her forehead against my shoulder.

"She said that?"

"She said she's old, not stupid."

"She *is* old," says Mara, muffled, into my collarbone. "And she's never been stupid in her life." She lifts her head. Her eyes are wet. "You said that to my mother. To her face."

"I was very polite about it."

"Nobody says that to my mother."

"Well," I say. "Somebody had to."

She kisses me. It tastes of salt and oatcake and the minerals in the water, and it's the best thing—

I don't finish the thought. She's laughing again. I can feel her feeling me think it.

---

When we finally come out of the cave, the sky over the Hollow has gone white.

Not cloudy. *White.* A low, flat, pearly white, like the inside of a shell, stretched from horizon to horizon without a single break. The air has changed too. It's colder than yesterday, much colder, and very still, and it smells different. Sharp and clean and metallic, like the air in the map room in Stonebridge, like slate. The steam from the vents rises straight up and hangs there without drifting.

Mara stops dead at the mouth of the cave and lifts her face and breathes in.

"Snow," she says.

My stomach drops. "Now?"

"Not today. Maybe not tomorrow." She keeps breathing, slow, tasting it. "Three days. Four, if we're lucky. It's on the high peaks already." She points north, at a line of grey summits I can just see over the rim of the Hollow, and I squint and see it: a thin white dusting along their crests, like flour on a baker's table.

Three days. Four if we're lucky. And we're at Nineteen, with twelve cairns left, five of them false, every one of them to be found and rebuilt and chained, and a man on his way to Stonebridge who knows exactly what I'm going to do.

"We can't make it," I say.

"We have to."

"Mara, it's twelve cairns, it's—"

"Then we don't sleep." She turns to me. Her face is set, and fierce, and under the bruises it's the face of the woman who leaned against a larch at the Highwood gate and looked at my hand like it might be wet. "We chain in the dark. We chain in the snow. We've got a chain, and a bow, and two good legs between us, and we're going to finish your survey, Cass Reed, and you're going to walk into that map room and lay it on the floor in front of Voss, and he's going to watch you do it."

I stare at her.

"That's the best plan you've had in days," I say.

"I've been having a bad week for plans."

"I noticed."

She almost smiles. Then she goes still again. Not the stillness of tasting snow. The other one, the deer-hearing-a-twig one, and her head turns slowly to the left.

I follow her look.

On the rocks above the mouth of the cave, where there was nobody a moment ago, there are people. Six of them, seven, motionless against the white sky. They're wrapped in grey, ragged coats patched and repatched, and their faces are thin and hard, and every one of them holds a spear.

The one in front is a very old woman, bent nearly double, leaning on a staff. Her hair is long and white and loose. Her eyes are amber, and they're fixed on me.

Not on Mara. On me.

She lifts her chin and breathes in, long and slow, the way Mara does, the way Ruth did over the pry bar. Her nostrils flare. Something goes across her lined face, surprise and then something much older and sadder than surprise.

"Well," says the old woman. Her voice is dry as a leaf. "Two in one lifetime. And I'd thought the first one was a miracle."


# Chapter Twenty-Three

The Hollow lives underground.

I don't understand it at first. The old woman leads us down off the rocks above the cave, slowly, leaning on her staff, and the others close in behind us with their spears held loosely, not pointed at anything, not quite pointed away. Nobody speaks. We walk for half an hour through the steaming grey wilderness. Then the old woman stops beside a sinkhole like any other, a black round mouth in the ground with a funnel of scree running down into it, and simply starts down.

There are steps. Cut into the scree, hidden under a skin of loose stones. Then there's a ledge, and an opening in the rock wall of the sinkhole, and beyond the opening, light.

It's a town. A small one, in a cave so big I can't see its roof. Lamps burn in niches in the rock. Hot springs steam in channels cut into the floor, and the air is warm and wet and smells of sulphur and woodsmoke and cooking. There are houses, built of stacked slate against the cave walls, with doors of hide. There are children. There are old people sitting by the springs. There are perhaps fifty souls in all, and every one of them stops and turns as we come in, and looks at us.

They look like Highwood. Black hair, brown skin, amber eyes. They look like Highwood the way a drawing looks like the thing it's drawn from: the same lines, but thinner, harder, scraped down to the bone.

Some of them are wrong.

I see a man by the nearest spring with a face that's almost a face: the jaw too long, the nose too broad, the ears too high and too pointed, and grey fur growing down his cheeks to his throat. I see a girl, no older than Bet, whose hands are long and dark and tipped with curved black claws. I see a woman whose spine curves forward in a long hunch under her coat, as if she's stopped halfway through bending down to the ground and never stood up again.

I remember the arm on the cart. *Halfway between one thing and another.*

"The half-shifted," Mara says, very low, beside me. Her voice is strange. "They get stuck. If they try to turn on ground that isn't theirs. Sometimes they just stay like that."

"For how long?"

"For ever."

The girl with the clawed hands is staring at my chain. When I look at her, she hides her hands behind her back.

---

The old woman sits us down by a spring at the back of the cave, on a ledge worn smooth by years of sitting. Someone brings bowls: broth, thin and hot and tasting of roots and something gamey. Someone else brings water. Nobody brings spears. They've vanished, along with most of the people who carried them.

Two things are laid at our feet, without a word.

The first is the pry bar. Its tip is still scraped bright.

The second is Mara's bow, its cut string dangling, and her quiver, refilled with every red-fletched arrow she dropped.

Mara stares at them. Then up at the old woman.

"The hunters' camp," says the old woman. "They ran in the night. They left everything." Her mouth twitches. "Your pony took most of it with him, surveyor. My grandsons found what was left."

"He's not my pony," I say faintly.

"No," she agrees. "He was the Office man's. And the Office man walked out of the Hollow yesterday at noon, south and west, toward the city, on foot, swearing." She settles herself on the ledge across the spring from us, leaning on her staff. "Not very fast. He's not a walker. But he'll get there."

"How far ahead?"

"Two days. Three, if he's as soft as he looks." She studies me. "You'll want to be quicker."

"We're going back to the line," says Mara. "To Nineteen."

"I know." The old woman's eyes haven't left me. "I'll send a boy with you. There's a way up out of the Hollow that comes out above Twenty. It'll save you half a day." A pause. "In exchange, you'll tell me something."

"What?"

"How long?"

It's what Ruth asked. The exact same words, in the exact same tone.

I look at Mara. Mara is looking at the old woman with an expression I can't read.

"Five days," I say. "No. Ten, now. At the ravine."

The old woman closes her eyes and breathes in. The same long slow breath as at the mouth of the cave.

"Ten days," she says. "And you're still alive. Both of you." She opens her eyes. "That's a good start."

---

"You knew," says Mara. "On the rocks. Before you saw us. You *smelled* it."

"Of course I smelled it." The old woman sounds almost amused. "You smell of each other right through. Like two loaves baked in the same oven." She tilts her head. "Your mother would have smelled it in a minute, Ruth's girl. Did she?"

Mara goes stiff. "You know my mother?"

"Everyone in the Hollow knows Ruth of Highwood." Something in the old woman's voice changes. It goes drier. Older. "Her grandmother put three of my family out of the hold under Clause Twelve. For marrying across packs. Did she ever tell you that?"

Mara doesn't answer.

"No," says the old woman. "I didn't think so." She looks away, across the warm dim cave, at the man by the spring with grey fur down his cheeks. "Most of us here were put out of somewhere. Under some clause or other. Twelve, or Six, or Twenty. For marrying wrong, or speaking wrong, or being born to the wrong mother. The treaty has a great many clauses, surveyor. It doesn't have any ground for us."

I think about the floor of the map room in Stonebridge. The blank grey patch with its little round pits, like pumice. No ochre. No lines. No numbers.

"I'm sorry," I say.

"Don't be sorry. Be a surveyor." She turns back to me. "Your survey. The true line. You're going to file it."

"Yes."

"In front of the Registrar."

"In front of everyone."

"Good." She nods, slowly. "Then they'll come for you. Not hunters. Not men with pry bars." Her eyes are very steady. "Law. Ledgers. Clauses. That's what they'll come with. The worst things in the world are written down."

"I know," I say.

"No, you don't. Not yet." She leans on her staff. "But you will."

---

We sleep in the Hollow that night, in a little stone house near the back of the cave, on a heap of furs that smell of smoke. Mara lies curled against me with her bandaged wrists tucked under her chin, so still and quiet that I think she must be asleep. I'm not. I lie awake and listen to the springs, and the soft sounds of fifty people sleeping in the dark, and think about clauses.

Before she sleeps, though, Mara says something.

We're lying in the dark in the little stone house, under the smoky furs, and she's quiet for so long I think she's already gone. Then she says, to the ceiling: "I didn't know."

"About Clause Twelve?"

"About any of it. My great-grandmother. The three she put out." Her voice is flat and careful, the way it gets when she's carrying something heavy. "We sing the border songs every full moon. Every cairn we ever lost. Every mile. We carved them on the posts in the hall so we'd never forget them." A breath. "Nobody ever carved a song for the ones we threw out."

I find her hand under the furs.

"That girl," she says. "By the spring. With the hands." Her fingers tighten on mine. "She's maybe nine. She'll never run. Not once. Not on any ground. Not ever. And I've been so angry, my whole life, about the Long Wood." She laughs, a short, ugly sound. "Four miles. I was angry about four miles."

"You're allowed to be angry about four miles."

"I know. I just—" She stops. "I thought we were the ones being robbed. I never thought about who we'd robbed."

I don't have an answer for that. I don't think there is one. So I just hold her hand, and after a while I feel her breathing slow, and the knot of shame in the tether loosen just a little, and she sleeps.

I lie in the dark beside her and think: *It doesn't have any ground for us.*

I'm a surveyor. Ground is what I do.

Some time in the night, I get up and go out.

The old woman is still sitting by the spring at the back of the cave. I don't think she's moved. Above her, high up, there's a crack in the roof of the cave, a long ragged slit of sky, and through it, so faint I almost miss it, the light of the moon. Waning now. Not full any more. But still bright enough to lay a thin silver stripe across the floor of the cave, right where the old woman sits.

She looks up when I come. I step into the silver stripe without thinking.

She sees my eyes.

I see her see them. I see her breath catch. And I know what she's seeing, the thin gold ring, faint now, a few nights past full, but there.

"You said two," I say quietly. "On the rocks. *Two in one lifetime.* You said the first one was a miracle."

The old woman doesn't answer for a long time. The spring talks to itself in its stone channel.

"Who was the first?" I ask.

"Sit down, surveyor."

I sit.

"I'm old," says the old woman. "I've seen a great many things. I've seen wolves stuck halfway for thirty years. I've seen children born with claws. I've seen Highwood throw its own out into the snow for loving the wrong person." She folds her hands on top of her staff. "And once, a long time ago, I saw a human catch the tether. I didn't think I'd ever see it again."

My heart is going very fast.

"With who?" I ask. "With someone from the Hollow?"

The old woman looks at me. Her face gives away nothing. It's the face of someone who has kept a great many secrets for a very long time, and knows exactly how much each one weighs.

"I don't say the names of my people to the Office," she says. "Not even to a surveyor I like."

"I'm not the Office."

"Not tonight." She almost smiles. "Ask me again after you've filed your survey. We'll see what you are then."

---

In the morning, before we leave, she gives me something.

She does it quietly, at the foot of the hidden steps, while Mara is a little way off talking to the boy who's going to guide us, a thin solemn creature of twelve or so with a sling at his belt. The old woman takes my hand, my good one, and turns it over, and presses something into my palm, and closes my fingers over it.

It's small and cold and hard. When I open my hand, it's a link.

A chain link. Brass. A long loop of wire bent at both ends, worn smooth by years of being dragged over stone, exactly like every one of the hundred links in the chain on my shoulder.

Except that one end has been opened, and never closed again.

And scratched very small along its side, in a slanted hand I'd know anywhere now, are two letters.

*J.M.*

I look up at the old woman. My mouth is open. Nothing comes out.

"Someone left that here," she says. "A while ago. For whoever came next." Her amber eyes are steady. "I think that's you."

Then she turns, and leans on her staff, and goes slowly back down the hidden steps into the dark.

The link is still cold in my fist when we climb out into the white morning. I don't put it away. I hold it the whole way up.


# Chapter Twenty-Four

The Hollow boy brings us up out of the sinkholes by a path that isn't a path at all: a crack in the rock, so narrow I have to take off my pack and push it ahead of me, that climbs for an hour through the dark and comes out, without any warning, into blinding white daylight on the side of a mountain.

I blink. I'm on a slope of frozen grass, high and bare, under that flat pearly sky. Below me, the Hollow steams in its grey bowl. Above me, a quarter mile up the slope, there's a cairn.

Not a neat little Office cairn. A real one. Or what's left of one: a scatter of old grey slabs lying in the frost, spilled out down the slope as if someone kicked them over.

"Twenty," says the boy. He points. "The true one. My gran says." And then, before I can thank him, he's gone back down the crack like a ferret down a hole.

Mara looks at the spilled stones. Then along the ridge, to where the line runs on north, toward Twenty-One and Twenty-Two and the high grey peaks with snow on their crests.

"Four cairns to rebuild," she says. "Then Twenty-Seven. Then the rest."

"And then all the way back down to Nine and Twelve."

"Three days," she says. "Maybe four."

"Before the snow." I think about it. "Mara. What *is* first snow? Exactly. In the treaty."

She frowns. "First snow. The first snow."

"Where?"

"I—" She stops. "I don't know. Nobody ever asked."

I take out my oath card, the little red-edged one Voss pressed into my hand in the map room, and turn it over. On the back, in tiny Office type, there's a list of definitions. I've never read them. Nobody reads the back.

*First snow: the first fall of snow recorded in the ledger of the Survey Office at the top lock of Stonebridge.*

I read it out loud.

"Stonebridge," says Mara slowly. "Not the mountain."

"Not the mountain." I look up at the white crests of the peaks. "Snow comes to the peaks first. Then the high line. Then the forest. Then the gorge. Stonebridge is at the bottom of everything." My heart is beating fast. "It's not three days. It's three days up *here*. Down there, it might be five. Six."

"Might."

"Might," I admit. "But it's enough to try."

Mara looks at me. Then she unslings her bow, runs her thumb along its new string, the one the Hollow gave her, and nods once.

"Then chain," she says.

---

We don't get to Twenty-One before we hear them coming.

Not hunters. A long, rising call from somewhere down the ridge to the south, high and clear, then another, answering. Then the sound of feet, a lot of feet, running.

Mara goes rigid. Then her whole face changes.

"Highwood," she says.

They come over the brow of the ridge at a run: twelve of them, fourteen, in their long patched coats, with bows and packs and coils of rope. At their head, loping easily up the frozen slope as if it's level ground, is Lena.

She stops in front of us, breathing hard. Her split lip has healed. Her eyes go to Mara: to her bruised jaw, her bandaged wrists. Something goes across her face like a cloud across the sun.

"Who did that?" she says.

"Hunters," says Mara. "It's done. I'm fine."

"You're not *fine*, you've got—"

"Lena." Mara's voice is quiet. "I'm fine. What are you doing here?"

Lena glances at me. It's not a friendly glance. But it isn't the look she gave me on the path to the hall either, all teeth and promise. It's something more complicated. Uncertain. As if she's remembering something she can't quite get hold of: a night, a moon, a black wolf standing over her.

"River's heir came to the hold," she says. "Three days ago. He told my aunt everything. The Office man. The book. The Hollow." She jerks her chin at me. "My aunt sent us. To help the surveyor *build cairns*." She says it as if it's the most ridiculous order she's ever been given.

I stare at her.

Theo kept his word.

"Nine and Twelve?" I ask.

"Rebuilt. Two days ago." Lena's eyes narrow. "We followed the old marks. Like the surveyor's drawing said." She means the big sheet I left on the long table in the hall, the true line in pencil. Somebody must have copied it.

"Thank you," I say.

She looks at me, hard. Then she shrugs, one shoulder, stiff.

"Don't thank me," she says. "Show me where to put the stones."

---

It goes faster than I could have dreamed.

I find the old marks. That's my job. At each spilled cairn, I go down on my knees in the frost and hunt through the fallen slabs until I find the one with the circle scratched into it, the line pointing true. I set up the theodolite and take the old bearing. And Highwood builds.

They don't just stack stones. They *build*, the way the old cairns were built, broad-based and fitted, each slab chosen and turned and set, and they sing while they do it. A low, steady song, the same shape over and over, a rise and a fall and a long straight run. When each cairn is finished, Lena climbs up on top of it with a pot of ochre and daubs the capstone red, and the singing stops, and for a moment the whole ridge is silent.

Then I chain to the next.

Twenty-One. Twenty-Two. Twenty-Three, as the light goes. We chain the last length to Twenty-Three by lantern, with Mara holding the brass handle and two of Lena's people walking beside the chain with torches so I can see where to set the pins. We camp beside the new cairn, under its fresh ochre, a whole pack of wolves in human shape curled up in their coats on the frozen grass, and I lie with my back to Mara's in the middle of them and feel safer than I ever have in my life.

In the morning, the frost on my blanket is thick as fur. The white on the peaks has crept lower in the night, a full hundred feet down their flanks.

We chain on.

---

Twenty-Seven is the last false cairn. We rebuild it in the early afternoon of the second day, in a cold so sharp that my breath freezes on my scarf. It's on a long exposed shoulder where the ridge narrows to a knife-edge, and when it's done, and daubed, and I've taken my bearing, I look ahead along the line and see Twenty-Eight, and Twenty-Nine beyond it.

And on Twenty-Nine, there are people.

It sits on a rocky knoll where the ridge bends east, the Highwood line on one side and, falling away to the west, a long valley that's green even now, threaded with a silver river. River ground. And around the cairn, standing very still against the white sky, there are thirty or more figures in long bark-coloured coats.

Mara stops. All around me, Highwood stops.

"River," says Lena, very low. Her hand has gone to her knife. "That's their whole high council."

And as we watch, the figures around Twenty-Nine lift their heads and begin to sing.

It's not Highwood's song. I can hear that even from here. The shape is wrong. Higher, quicker, full of little runs and turns like water over stones. It goes up, and round, and down, and it rises off the knoll into the cold air and comes rolling up the ridge toward us like smoke.

"They're singing it," Lena breathes. "On *our* cairn. They're claiming it."

*A song sung on the wrong cairn is a declaration of war.*

All around me, I feel Highwood change. It's like the moment before a storm, the moment on the slope at Seven when Theo said June's name. Every hair on my arms lifts. Lena's knife is half out of its sheath. Behind her, someone has an arrow on the string. Someone else is growling, a low, terrible, human growl.

And through the tether, from Mara, comes rage.

It's enormous. It's the rage of a wolf who has lost two miles of ground in her lifetime and four in her mother's, who has been tied to a post and kicked in the ribs on open ground, who has just spent two days rebuilding cairns the Office pulled down. It hits me like a fist. It burns in my own chest. I can feel her teeth behind her lips. I can feel her hand on her bow.

I don't say anything. I don't touch her. I just stand beside her and let her feel me there.

*They want you to see it,* she said, at Seven. *They want me to see it.*

"Mara," says Lena. "Give the word."

Mara doesn't move.

"*Mara.* They're singing on our cairn. In front of the whole pack. If we don't answer—"

"If we answer," says Mara, "we start a war."

"They've *started* it!"

"No." Mara's voice is very quiet and very level. I can feel what it costs her. Every word is a stone she's lifting off her own chest. "They want us to start it. Here. Now. Two days before the surveyor files. So that when she walks into the map room, the Registrar can say Highwood went to war with River on the line, and the survey was walked under arms, and none of it can be trusted." She turns her head and looks at Lena. "That's what this is. It's not a song. It's a trap."

Lena stares at her. Her knife is still half drawn.

"So we just *take* it?"

"No," I say.

They both look at me.

"We just chain it," I say. My voice is shaking, a little. I make it stop. "Cairn Twenty-Nine is on the Highwood line. It's been on the Highwood line for a hundred years. Nobody can change that by singing at it. Nobody can change it by pulling it down. The only thing that changes a line is a survey." I lift the chain off my shoulder. "So I'm going to walk up there, and I'm going to chain it, and I'm going to take my bearing off it, in front of River's whole high council. And then we're going to walk on."

There's a long silence.

"And if they stop you?" Lena asks.

"Then they've interfered with a sworn survey," I say. "In front of thirty witnesses. And I'll put that in my report too."

Mara looks at me. I feel it through the tether: the rage still there, still burning, but something else underneath it now. Something like pride.

Then she takes the brass handle out of my hand.

"Chain," she says.

---

We walk up to Twenty-Nine together. Just the two of us. Highwood stays on the ridge behind us, bows down, knives sheathed, silent.

River's council parts as we come, slowly. The song falters. Stops.

At the front of them, I see Theo. He's standing a little apart, his walking stick in his hand, and his face is white and set, all the easy charm wiped off it. When his eyes meet mine, he gives the smallest shake of his head.

*Not mine,* it says. *Not my choice.*

I believe him. I don't know why.

I set up the theodolite over Cairn Twenty-Nine, in the middle of River's high council, with thirty pairs of amber eyes on me and River's song still hanging in the cold air like smoke. I level the bubble. My hands are perfectly steady.

I take my bearing on Thirty.

"Chaining," I say.

And Mara walks on, the brass handle in her fist, out past the cairn, past the silent ranks of River, along the ridge toward Thirty, and the chain unrolls behind her across the frozen ground, brass and silver and brass, and nobody stops her.

Behind me, very softly, somebody on the Highwood side begins to sing.

I look up. Far to the north, over the last grey peaks, the white sky has turned grey, and something is drifting down out of it, slow and silent and soft as ash.

The first flakes.


# Chapter Twenty-Five

Cairn Thirty-One is the top of the world, and it's snowing on it.

We reach it in the last grey hour of the afternoon, after chaining Thirty through flakes that come down thicker and faster with every length. It sits on a bare summit of shattered slate, the highest point of the whole Highwood line, with the land falling away on every side into white. I can't see the Hollow any more. I can't see River's valleys. I can barely see Thirty behind us, a red smudge in the swirl. There's just the cairn, old and broad and furred with frost, and the snow, and the wind, and us.

I set up the theodolite over it with numb fingers. The bubble won't settle. I talk to it. I beg it. Snow lands on the eyepiece and melts and blurs the cross-hairs, and I wipe it with my sleeve, and wipe it again, and take my sight back along the line to Thirty, and close the angle.

It closes. A tenth of a degree.

I write it in my field book. My hand is shaking so hard the figures come out like a child's. I don't care. I write it down, and underline it, and then I sit down in the snow beside Cairn Thirty-One and laugh until I can't breathe.

Mara sits down beside me. She doesn't ask what's funny. She can feel it.

"Two to go," she says.

"Two to go," I say. "And they're both thirty miles away. At the bottom of the mountain."

"Twenty," she says. "If we go straight down the ridge."

"Oh, well, *twenty*. That's nothing. Why didn't you say?"

She bumps her shoulder against mine. Snow is caught in her eyelashes, in her hair, on the bruise along her jaw. She looks happier than I've ever seen her.

"We'll run," she says.

---

Before we can run, Theo comes.

He's followed us up from Twenty-Nine. I didn't hear him; nobody ever hears him. He's just suddenly there, at the edge of the summit, a shape in the snow with a walking stick, and Highwood bristles all along the ridge behind us, and Lena's knife comes half out again.

"Let him," says Mara, without looking round.

Theo comes over, slowly, his boots crunching. He stops a polite distance off and looks down at us sitting in the snow by the cairn.

"Congratulations," he says. "Thirty-One."

"You told Ruth," I say. "About Holt. And the Hollow."

"I said I would."

"I wasn't sure you'd do it."

"Neither was I." He smiles, a little crookedly. "My mother wasn't pleased. Nor was the council. Hence"—he waves his stick back down the ridge toward Twenty-Nine—"the singing. That was my mother's idea of a reply."

"And yours?"

"Mine," says Theo, "is to come up a mountain in a snowstorm and tell you something you won't like." He crouches in the snow, so his face is level with ours. His blue eyes are serious for once. "Holt wasn't sent up here to check your numbers."

"I know. He was sent to make sure nobody would trust them."

"That too." He hesitates. "But that's not why the Registrar sent him. Not really." He looks at Mara. Then at me. Then, very briefly, at the narrow strip of snow between our shoulders, where they're touching. "He was sent to look."

The wind goes cold right through me.

"Look at what?" I ask.

Theo doesn't answer that. He doesn't have to.

"I don't know how Voss knew," he says quietly. "Maybe someone in the Hollow talks. Maybe someone at the hold. Maybe he just guessed, from the way you walked into his map room. He's very good at guessing." He stands up and brushes the snow off his knees. "But he sent a man with a notebook up the Highwood line to see what was in your eyes. And then you set fire to his pony."

"I didn't *set fire*—"

"I know, I know. It ran through a fire. You've said." Theo almost smiles. "File your survey, surveyor. Do it fast. Do it in front of as many people as you can find." He turns to go. "And then I'd think very carefully about where you sleep at the next full moon."

He walks off into the snow. In a few steps he's a shape. In a few more he's gone.

Beside me, Mara hasn't moved a muscle since he spoke.

"He knew," she says. "On the saddle. When he looked."

"He knew before that," I say. "I think he knew at Seven."

"And he didn't say."

"No." I watch the place where he vanished. "Neither did we."

---

We run.

Down off the summit, down the ridge, in the snow and then out of it. The flakes thin as we drop, then turn to sleet, then stop altogether, though the sky stays grey and low and heavy and the cold doesn't lift. Highwood runs with us, all fourteen of them. Not as wolves; a wolf can't carry a torch, or a coil of rope, or a surveyor's pack, and Ruth sent them to carry things. They go down the mountain the way water goes down it, choosing every line without seeming to think, never stumbling. I stumble constantly. Two of them take my pack. Lena takes the theodolite case, with a look that says she'll drop it off a cliff if I say a word, and then carries it more carefully than I would. I let her.

We don't stop when it gets dark. Someone lights torches. We run by torchlight, a long bobbing chain of flames down the black ridge, past Twenty-Seven and its fresh red cap, past Twenty-Three and Twenty-Two and Twenty-One. We sleep for three hours in a hollow above Nineteen, piled together like puppies for warmth. Then we run again.

I count. I can't stop. Steps, cairns, hours. My legs stop being legs and become two numbers I'm carrying. Mara is always beside me, or just ahead, and when I stumble I feel her catch her breath before I've even started to fall.

At dawn on the second day, we reach Thirteen.

And from the ledge where we found June's book, I see the snow line.

It's below us. It's come down in the night. A clean white edge running across the whole face of the mountain, from the ravine in the south to the high peaks in the north, as straight as a surveyor's line. Above it, white. Below it, gold. The larches are still gold below it, the whole long sweep of the forest still glowing. But at the edge, where the white meets them, the gold is going grey.

It's moving. Slowly. Downhill.

"Twelve," says Mara.

---

We chain Twelve in an hour. The true Twelve, rebuilt by Lena's people from its own old stones, a quarter mile out from the Office cairn, in a clearing where three great larches stand in a ring. I find the old mark on its third stone, a little crooked where Lena set it back, and lay my pencil along the line, and it points true. I chain from Eleven to Twelve and from Twelve to Thirteen, and close both triangles, and they close.

We run again.

The snow comes down behind us. I can feel it, the way you'd feel someone walking a few paces behind you in the dark. Every time I look back up the ridge, the white edge has crept lower. Past Thirteen. Past Twelve, an hour after we leave it. The air is so cold now it hurts to breathe.

Past Eleven. Down into the ravine by the goat path, to the little cairn of seven stones by the stream.

Mara stops there. Everyone stops, because she does.

She doesn't say anything. She bends and picks up a flat grey stone from the stream bed, smaller than the seventh, and sets it carefully on top of the little stack. Then she takes the brown larch twig off the top, the one she laid there in spring, and puts it in her coat, and lays a fresh one in its place: gold, from the trees above, still bright.

"Eight," she says quietly.

I don't understand at first. Then I do. Eight stones. Eight, for the last cairn where she saw June. For the place she was sent away from. Not a grave any more. A marker. A place on a line.

*Don't look for me under the stones,* June wrote. *You won't find me there.*

I take my pencil out of my pocket, and crouch, and on the flat face of the eighth stone I scratch a small circle, with a line through it, pointing north. Toward the hold. Toward Thirteen, and the ledge, and her book.

Mara looks at it. Then she touches my shoulder, once, and we go on.

Up the other side. Past Ten. Across the narrow neck of open ground, where Mara dims and Lena dims and all of Highwood dims together, and we run the five chains in silence, and nobody is waiting for us in the bracken.

And then the forest, and the slope, and a fallen larch, and under it a cairn.

The true Nine. Rebuilt. Standing tall and broad and fitted in its old place, a quarter mile out from the neat little Office cairn, its capstone daubed red. Lena's people built it two days ago. They built it well.

It's afternoon. The light is going. Above us, so close I could almost throw a stone into it, the white edge of the snow is sliding down through the gold.

I set up the theodolite over Eight. Mara takes the brass handle. I take my bearing on Nine: forty-nine degrees. June's bearing.

"Chaining," I say.

And we chain it.

One length. Two. Ten. Twenty. Mara walks, and I follow, and I pin, and I call. All of Highwood walks beside us, silent, through the trees. Thirty. Forty. The snow is in the treetops now. I can see it falling through the gold, a few flakes, then more. Fifty. It lands on the chain. It lands on Mara's hair. It lands on my outstretched hand.

Fifty-seven.

Mara stops beside the true Nine with the chain drawn taut between us.

I walk the last few feet to her. I set the last pin.

"Fifty-seven," I say.

My voice comes out strange. Very small. I check it. I check the tension and the pin, and I take my sight to Ten and close the triangle, and it closes, a tenth of a degree, the same as June's, exactly the same, and I write it in my field book with my numb shaking hand.

Then I look up.

Snow is falling all around us. Through the larches, through the gold, soft and silent and enormous. It's falling on Nine and on the fallen tree and on the gouge in the old stone where someone once drove an iron bar. It's falling on all of Highwood, standing silent among the trees.

It's done.

The whole line. Thirty-one cairns. Every length chained, every triangle closed. Four miles of border, a quarter mile deep, all of it pushed back out to where it belongs.

"It's done," I say.

Nobody moves.

Then Lena starts to sing.

It's the Long Wood. I know it now. I know the shape of it. Here a rise. Here a fall. A long straight run. She sings it with her face lifted into the falling snow and tears running down her cheeks, and one by one, all around us, Highwood joins her.

Mara drops the brass handle in the snow.

She turns to me. Her face is wet. Her eyes are shining. And she doesn't care, she doesn't care at all, who's watching. She takes my face in her hands, there in front of her whole pack, and kisses me.

Behind us, the Long Wood goes on singing.

Below us, at the bottom of the mountain, the snow is moving down through the gold toward Stonebridge.


# Chapter Twenty-Six

Ruth comes down the mountain in a litter.

I don't know she's coming until we reach the Highwood gate at dawn, the two larches and the red cairn, and find her there waiting for us. She's sitting in a chair made of bent larch poles and lashed hide, wrapped in so many furs that only her face shows, small and brown and fierce as a walnut. Four of her people stand around her, holding the poles. Behind them, all along the path, there are more. Thirty. Forty. Half the hold, at least, in their long patched coats, silent in the cold grey light.

"Mother," says Mara. Her voice cracks. "You can't. The cough—"

"I can do what I like," says Ruth. "I'm alpha." She looks past her daughter, at me. "Witnesses, surveyor. Wasn't that what she wrote? Where it can't be lost?"

I can't speak. I nod.

"Then let's go and be witnessed," says Ruth of Highwood, and coughs, and waves a thin hand at her bearers. "Down."

---

We go down the cart track into Stonebridge like an army.

Not an army with weapons. Ruth was very clear about that, at the gate: bows unstrung, knives left with the youngest at the cairn. An army of people. Of wolves in human shape, fifty of them now, walking in a long silent column down the switchbacks past the quarries and the guild stakes, past the yellow flags snapping on the east spur, with their alpha carried at the front and a surveyor walking beside her with a chain on her shoulder.

The quarrymen stop work to stare. A cart driver pulls his horse off the track and sits with his mouth open. At the first houses, people come to their doors. At the top lock, the woman who sold me chestnuts on my first morning stands up behind her brazier and watches us go by, and as I pass, she lifts one hand, very slightly, as if she's not sure she should.

I lift mine back.

The sky over the gorge is white and low and heavy. The air smells of slate and coal smoke and something sharper underneath. Snow. It hasn't started yet. It's very close.

Two hundred and twelve stairs to the Survey Office. I count them all. So, I think, does Mara, walking beside me, although she'd never admit it.

---

The doors of the Office are shut when we reach the top. A clerk comes out, the young nervous one with ink to his wrists, the one who showed me in on my first day. He sees the column of wolves on the stairs below him, fifty pairs of amber eyes, and goes the colour of paper.

"I'm here to file a survey," I say. "The Highwood line. Walked and chained by Cass Reed, sworn surveyor, first class." I hold up my field book. "With witnesses."

The clerk looks at the book. Then at Ruth, in her litter. Then at me.

"The Registrar," he says faintly, "is in the map room."

"Good," I say.

---

The bench outside the double doors still has its row of shoes. The polished black ones are there, too expensive to be a clerk's. Voss is waiting.

I sit and unlace my boots.

Behind me, there's a pause. Then a rustle, and a creak, and a thud, and another, and another, as fifty people from the Highwood hold sit down on the floor of the corridor and take off their boots.

Mara sits beside me on the bench. She unlaces her boots without a word and sets them beside mine. Then she looks down at her own feet, in thick grey wool socks with a hole in one heel, and up at me, with an expression of deep and quiet outrage.

"No boots on the map," I say.

"This is ridiculous."

"I know."

"I'm walking on my own forest in my *socks*."

"I know," I say. "Come on."

I push open the doors.

---

The map room is just as I left it. The great slate floor, carved with the whole Reach. The little rivers trickling in their channels. The ochre lines. The cold coming up through my socks.

There are more people than I expected. Clerks, a dozen of them, lining the walls. Two men in the grey coats of the Office whom I don't know. A man in a fine dark coat with a gold chain across his waistcoat whom I'd guess, from the way he's standing, owns a quarry. Over by the window, leaning on his walking stick and watching everything, Theo.

And by the window, beside the gold crown of the Highwood ridge, Registrar Voss.

Holt is standing at his shoulder.

He looks tired. His Office coat is buttoned to the throat, his narrow pleasant face drawn and grey, and when he sees me come through the door his mouth goes thin. When he sees Mara come through behind me, in her socks, and then Ruth carried in her chair, and then the whole of Highwood filing in barefoot and stocking-footed and silent behind her, until they line the whole southern wall of the map room three deep, his face does something I'll treasure for the rest of my life.

Voss doesn't change at all.

"Miss Reed," he says pleasantly. "You've brought friends."

"Witnesses," I say. "Under the treaty."

"Of course." He walks toward me across the map, stepping over rivers and ridges without looking down, exactly the way he did on my first morning. "Mr. Holt tells me you've had a difficult survey."

"Mr. Holt removed a link from my chain," I say, "and stole my field book, and gave it to hunters on open ground in the Hollow."

The room goes very quiet.

"Those are serious accusations," says Voss.

"Yes."

"Mr. Holt informs me that your chain was short when he examined it. By one link. That your distances throughout the survey are therefore inflated." He shakes his head, sadly. "In Highwood's favour, I'm afraid. Your escort's favour." His eyes rest on Mara for a moment. "I'm sure you understand the difficulty."

"I understand it very well." I lift the chain off my shoulder. "Which is why I'd like it measured. Here. Now. Against the Standard."

For the first time, something flickers behind his spectacles.

The Standard is set into the north wall of the map room, under the tall windows: a bar of dark bronze, sixty-six feet long, let into the slate at waist height, with a brass stud at each end. Every chain in the Reach is supposed to be checked against it once a year. I've read about it in every manual. I've never seen anyone use it.

"Mr. Holt," I say. "Would you like to hold the other end?"

---

They measure it. Two clerks, white-faced, while the whole room watches. One brass handle against the stud at one end. The chain stretched taut along the bronze bar, link by link. The other handle at the other end.

It meets the stud exactly.

"Sixty-six feet," says the older of the clerks. His voice shakes. "Exact."

"Count the links," I say.

He counts them. Out loud. One. Two. All the way to a hundred. When he's done, I hold up the ring between forty-three and forty-four. The new one, from my repair tin, bright against the old brass. I ask him to compare it to the one next to it.

He does.

"This one's new," he says slowly. "Replaced. Recently."

"At Cairn Fifteen," I say. "The night Mr. Holt admired my chain by the fire. I counted it the next morning, as I do every morning. It had ninety-nine links. River's heir was present when I said so." I don't look at Theo. "I replaced it before I chained another length. Every length on this survey, from Cairn One to Cairn Thirty-One, was chained with a hundred links."

All eyes turn to Theo by the window.

He doesn't move. Then he sighs, very quietly, like a man stepping off a ledge he's been standing on for a long time.

"She said it," says Theo of River. "Ninety-nine. I heard her. Holt didn't deny it." He looks at Holt. "He said he'd *note her concern*."

Holt's face has gone the colour of the slate.

---

I lay the survey on the floor.

All of it. My field book, open. June's leather book beside it, open at the sketch, the solid line and the dotted one. Her duplicate of page forty-one. The note from Thirteen. I set them down on the carved Highwood ridge, right on top of the paler slate, the recut groove between Nine and Fourteen. Then I lay the pry bar beside them, its tip still scraped bright.

"This is the Highwood line," I say. "Walked and chained by June Marsh, sworn surveyor, from Cairn One to Cairn Fourteen, in spring. Walked and chained again by me, Cass Reed, sworn surveyor, from Cairn One to Cairn Thirty-One, this autumn. Every triangle closes to a tenth of a degree. Every old cairn carries its old mark. Seven cairns on the Office copy carry an Office stamp from this summer. They were built on ground that never slipped, in places where the old marks never pointed." I take a breath. "The true line runs a quarter mile beyond the Office line, for four miles of its length. I'm filing it. In front of fifty witnesses from Highwood, and River's heir, and every clerk in this room."

Nobody speaks.

"And," I say, "I'd like it carved."

Voss looks down at the books on the floor at his feet for a very long time.

I watch him think. I watch him do the sum. Fifty wolves along the south wall. Ruth in her litter. Theo by the window. A dozen clerks with ink to their wrists who have just watched a chain meet the Standard, and heard the word *Holt*. The man with the gold watch chain, who has gone quite still. And on the floor, in two hands, the same numbers. Two surveyors. One line.

Then Voss lifts his head and smiles.

It's a very good smile. Warm, and regretful, and perfectly clean.

"Mr. Holt," he says, without turning round, "you're dismissed from the service of the Office. Effective immediately. You'll answer to the magistrate for the chain."

Holt makes a sound like a man being hit.

"Registrar—"

"Effective *immediately*, Mr. Holt." Voss doesn't look at him. He's looking at me. "The Office regrets the conduct of one of its officers. The Office is grateful to Miss Reed for her diligence." He nods to the clerks along the wall. "Carve it."

---

It takes them all afternoon.

They come with chisels and mallets and pots of ochre, six of them, and they kneel on the slate floor in their socks and recut the Highwood line. They fill the old groove between Nine and Fourteen with slate dust and resin, and cut the new one a quarter mile further out. Then the same between Nineteen and Twenty-Four. Then at Twenty-Seven. Chip by chip. The sound fills the room like rain.

Highwood watches. All of them. Nobody leaves. Nobody speaks. When the last clerk sits back on his heels and wipes his brow, and another comes with the ochre pot and paints the new groove red, Ruth makes a small sound in her litter.

She's crying. She doesn't wipe her face.

The forest is back. Four miles of it, carved in slate.

I stand on it, in my socks, and feel the cold come up through my feet, and Mara's hand finds mine, there in the middle of the map room, in front of everyone.

I don't let go.

Outside the tall windows, the white sky over the gorge has finally broken. The first flakes are drifting down past the glass, soft and slow and enormous, onto the roofs of Stonebridge.

Somewhere down at the top lock, a clerk will be writing it in the ledger now. *First snow.*

The Highwood line has held.


# Chapter Twenty-Seven

The innkeeper at the top lock has opinions about wolves, and he shares them with me over the bar whether I want them or not.

"Fifty," he says. "*Fifty.* In my common room. Dripping snow on my floor. Eating my bread." He slaps a tankard down in front of me so hard it slops. "One of them asked if I had any raw liver. *Raw.*"

"Did you?"

"Of course I had raw liver," he says, with enormous dignity. "I'm not an amateur."

Behind me, the common room of the inn at the top lock is louder than I've ever heard any room be. Every bench is full. Every table. People are sitting on the floor and on the stairs and on the bar itself, Highwood in their long patched coats with snow melting on their shoulders. Someone has found a fiddle. Someone else has found a drum, or a pot that can be used as one. Lena is dancing on a table with a tankard in each hand, and two of her friends are trying to pull her down and failing, and the whole room is singing something that is definitely not a border song and is, if I'm understanding the words correctly, about a goat.

The innkeeper looks at it all with an expression of profound suffering. Then he looks at the money box behind the bar, which is fuller than I suspect it's been in years.

"Raw liver," he mutters again, and goes off to find more.

Someone slides onto the stool beside me. I know who before I look. Nobody else in the Reach arrives so quietly.

"Surveyor," says Theo.

"You came."

"River was represented in the map room. River is represented at the party." He lifts a finger at the innkeeper, who ignores him. "Also, I'm told there's raw liver."

I look at him. He looks tired, under the easy smile. There's a shadow along his jaw where he hasn't shaved. He said a lot of things in the map room this afternoon, in front of the Registrar and a dozen clerks and a man with a gold watch chain, that River's heir probably wasn't supposed to say.

"Thank you," I say. "For telling them about the chain."

"It was true."

"That's never stopped you before."

He laughs. It's a real one, short and surprised. "No," he admits. "It hasn't." He turns his tankard round on the bar, slowly. "My mother's going to be furious with me for a year."

"Will she forgive you?"

"Probably. Eventually. She'll want something for it." He glances sideways at me. "River always does."

"On the saddle," I say. "When you left to go to the hold. You said *she really did find the right one*." I hold his gaze. "Who's *she*?"

Theo looks at me over his tankard. Then he smiles, the old easy one, the costume.

"Ask me in the spring," he says, and drinks, and doesn't say anything else.

---

Ruth is by the fire.

They've carried her litter in and set it down in the warmest corner, and she sits in her furs with a cup of something hot and watches her people with an expression I can't read. Every few minutes someone comes and kneels by her chair and says something, and she nods, or puts a thin hand on their head for a moment. She's coughing less tonight. Or she's hiding it better. I can't tell which.

When she sees me looking, she crooks one finger.

I go and sit on the floor beside her chair.

"Surveyor," she says.

"Ruth."

"You did it."

"We did it."

"Mm." She drinks. Her eyes are on the dancing. On Lena, who has now been joined on the table by Bet's older brother. "Four miles. I'll be dead before I've walked half of it." She says it calmly. "But Mara will walk all of it. And her children. And theirs."

I don't say anything. I don't think she wants me to.

"I was wrong about you," says Ruth, after a while.

"Which part?"

"I thought you'd break." She looks down at me. "On the line. Under all that. I thought you'd go back down the mountain in a week with your chain between your legs and tell the Office the escort was sullen." Her mouth twitches. "June's word. Not mine."

"I nearly did," I say. "Break, I mean. More than once."

"But you didn't."

"No."

"Why?"

I think about it. The scree slope. The crossbow. The bridge over the sinkhole, link by link in the dark. Every time, the same reason.

I look across the room.

Mara is by the door. She's been by the door all night. She's standing with her back against the wall, a full tankard in her hand that she hasn't touched, and she looks the way she looked on the narrow neck of open ground between Nine and Ten. Dimmed. Like a lamp turned down.

Ruth follows my look. Her face changes. Not much. Enough.

"The city," she says quietly. "No pack holds it. It's no one's ground. She can't change here. None of us can." She turns her cup in her hands. "Most of us drink until we stop noticing. Mara's never been able to."

"Is it bad?"

"It's like being half asleep," says Ruth, "and knowing you'll never wake up properly until you leave."

I'm on my feet.

"Surveyor."

I stop.

Ruth looks up at me from her furs, with her sharp old eyes, and for a moment she looks very like her daughter.

"Up in the hall," she says, "you said sending someone away to keep them safe just means they don't get a say." She takes a slow breath. "So. I'm listening. When she wants to say it."

I can't speak. I nod.

"Go on," says Ruth of Highwood, and waves me off with a thin hand. "Before I change my mind."

---

"Come with me," I say to Mara.

She looks at me. "Where?"

"Upstairs." I take her hand. Nobody's watching, or everybody is; it doesn't matter. "I've got a room with a window."

It's the same room. The slanted ceiling, the sagging bed, the window that looks straight up at the Highwood ridge. It's dark outside now. The snow has been falling since the afternoon, steady and soft, and the roofs of Stonebridge are white, and the river is a black ribbon below, and up on the ridge, invisible in the dark, the larches are holding the snow on their branches like cupped hands.

I open the window.

Cold comes in. And with it, so faint I can barely smell it under the coal smoke, the smell of larch. Resin and frost and needles, blowing down off the mountain.

Mara goes to the window like someone walking to a fire. She puts both hands on the sill and leans out into the snow and breathes in. A long breath. Then another. I watch her shoulders come up, half an inch. Not much. A lamp turned up a little, in a room that's still too dark.

"Better?" I ask.

"Yes." She doesn't turn around. "It's so loud here. Everything. Coal and horses and the river and so many people. I can't think." A pause. "I could hear your heart from the bottom of the stairs, though."

"That's because it's very loud."

"It is," she agrees. "It's always very loud."

I come and stand beside her at the window. Snow lands on my face. It lands on hers. We look up together at the dark shape of the ridge, at the place where I know, without needing to see it, that the Highwood gate is: two larches the width of a cart apart and a cairn daubed so red it looks freshly bled on.

"Your mother says she's listening," I say. "When you want to say it."

Mara's breath catches.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you want." I keep my eyes on the ridge. "She said I was right. That sending someone away just means they don't get a say. So she's giving you one."

For a long time Mara doesn't say anything at all.

"I want you to stay," she says at last. Very quietly. To the snow. "That's what I want. I want you to stay in the Reach. Not in the lowlands. Not anywhere else. Here." She takes a breath. "I want you at the hold. At the long table, drawing your lines. On the ridge with your chain. Talking to your telescope."

"Theodolite."

"*Theodolite.*" She almost laughs. "I want to know where you are because you're three steps away, not because you're four hundred miles south and I can feel you missing me." Her hands tighten on the sill. "I want you to stay. And I want to not die for it. And I want you to keep your hand." Her voice cracks. "That's what I want. All of it. I know it's impossible. I'm saying it anyway."

I turn to look at her.

She's not looking at me. She's looking at the ridge. Her face is wet, from the snow or from something else. I can't tell. I can feel it, though. All of it, every word, pouring through the tether like water over a weir.

"It's not impossible," I say.

"Clause Nine—"

"Clause Nine is a line." I hear myself say it, and I know, as soon as it's out, that it's true. "It's a line someone drew. A hundred years ago, in a room like the one downstairs, with a quill and a ledger. Someone sat down and decided where it went." I take her hand off the sill and hold it. "And I've just spent four weeks proving that lines get drawn wrong. And that if you walk them, and measure them, and you're stubborn enough to stand in a room full of people and say *no, that's wrong, here's the number*—"

"You can move them," says Mara.

"You can put them back where they should have been."

She turns. She looks at me. In the dark, with the snow behind her, her eyes are just amber, no gold. It's the wrong time of the month for gold. But I can see it anyway. I can see all of it.

"Then survey it," she says.

"Clause Nine?"

"All of it. The whole treaty." Her fingers tighten on mine. "Find where it's wrong. Find the number. And stand in a room full of people, and say it."

"That might take years."

"I'm a wolf. I'm good at waiting." She pulls me in. Her forehead comes down against mine, cold from the window, her breath warm on my mouth. "And you're a surveyor."

"I am."

"The one nobody can pay."

"The one nobody can pay," I say.

"Then I'll wait," says Mara. "And I'll hold the chain."

---

Later, much later, when the singing downstairs has finally stopped and the fire in the common room has burned low and the snow has stopped falling and the whole of Stonebridge lies white and silent under a sky full of stars, we lie together in the sagging bed under the slanted ceiling.

The window's still open. We're both too stubborn to close it. The cold comes in and the larch smell comes in with it, and Mara lies with her face turned toward it, toward the ridge, breathing it in, and I lie with my face in her hair.

I can feel her heart. Slow and steady, beating in time with mine.

"Promise me," she says into the dark.

"What?"

"That you'll find a way."

I think about Clause Nine. About the oath card in my pocket, with its tiny definitions on the back that nobody reads. About a hundred years of ledgers stacked floor to ceiling down a long corridor, and somewhere in them, perhaps, the original treaty, the first version, the one someone wrote down before anyone started moving lines.

"I promise," I say.

She sighs, a long, slow, contented sound, and settles back against me. And within a minute, for the first time since I've known her, she's asleep in a city.

I'm not. I lie awake, and look at the slanted ceiling, and make a list in my head of every ledger I want to read, in order, with the dates.

It's a very long list. I don't mind.

Outside, far up on the ridge, something begins to sing.


# Chapter Twenty-Eight

I wake up in Stonebridge, in the sagging bed under the slanted ceiling, with snow on the windowsill and Mara asleep beside me, and for a long time I just lie there and let myself be happy.

It's very quiet. The whole city is muffled under white. No carts. No river noise; the snow swallows it. Just, now and then, the soft whump of a load sliding off a roof somewhere, and the far-off clank of the first lock gate opening for the day.

Mara is sleeping on her front, one arm flung across my stomach, her face turned toward the open window. Her hair is everywhere. There's a larch needle caught in it, which must have come all the way down the mountain with her. Her breathing is slow and deep, and through the tether I can feel how deep. She's not dimmed. Not this morning. Not asleep.

I watch her for a while. I don't count anything.

Then I ease out from under her arm, and dress quietly, and go out into the snow.

I don't mean to go far. Just to the top lock, a hundred steps from the inn door. I want to see it. I want to see the place where it's written down.

The lock-house is a little slate hut on the quay beside the great black gates, with one window and a chimney trailing smoke. Through the window I can see a clerk at a high desk, an old man in fingerless gloves, bent over a ledger as thick as a paving stone. He's writing. Very slowly, very carefully, the way you'd write something that's going to be read for a hundred years.

I don't go in. I stand outside in the snow and watch him through the glass, and I know exactly what he's writing, because it's the only thing anyone in this city will write today.

*First snow.*

And under it, somewhere in another ledger, in another building at the top of two hundred and twelve stairs, there's a line carved in slate that wasn't there yesterday. A quarter mile further out than it was. Four miles long. Daubed fresh red.

"You again," says a voice.

It's the chestnut woman. She's setting up her brazier on the quay, stamping her feet against the cold, her breath smoking. She looks at me, and then up at the ridge, white now from top to bottom, and then back at me.

"I saw you come down yesterday," she says. "With all of them. The whole mountain behind you."

"Yes."

"My mother's mother was Highwood," she says abruptly. "Put out, under one of their clauses. Married a lock-keeper. Never went back." She shrugs, and pokes at her coals. "Never could change, after. Not in the city. Used to stand right here on the quay on full-moon nights and look up at that ridge like it owed her money."

I don't know what to say.

She fishes a paper twist of hot chestnuts out of her brazier and holds it out to me.

"On the house," she says. "For the forest."

I take them. They burn my fingers through the paper. I eat them on the walk back to the inn, slowly, and they're the best thing I've ever tasted, and this time there's nobody beside me to tell.

I go in to find breakfast.

---

The common room looks like a battlefield. Highwood is asleep everywhere, on the benches, on the floor, under the tables, in heaps. Lena is asleep on the bar with her head on her arms and a tankard still in one hand. Ruth's litter is empty by the cold fire; someone must have carried her up to a bed. The innkeeper is sweeping round the sleeping bodies with a broom and an expression of martyrdom.

"Porridge," he says, when he sees me.

"Please."

"You'll get it when I've swept." He sweeps. Then he stops, and looks at me, and says gruffly, "That was a good thing you did. Yesterday. With the map."

I stare at him.

"My grandfather was a carter," he says. "Used to take the Office men up the line, forty years back. He always said there was something wrong with the way they came back down." He goes back to sweeping. "Took a lot of stones with them, he said. For people who were only meant to be counting."

He doesn't say anything else. I sit at the end of the nearest bench, between two sleeping wolves, and wait for my porridge, and feel something warm settle in my chest.

---

I'm halfway through the porridge when the door opens.

Cold air comes in, and snow, and a young man in an Office coat, his face red with cold, ink to the wrists. It's the clerk. The nervous one, the one who took me down the ledger corridor on my first morning and told me to take off my boots.

He stands in the doorway and looks at the common room full of sleeping wolves, and goes very pale.

Then he sees me. He comes across the room toward me, picking his way between the sleepers, holding something in both hands against his chest as if it might bite him.

"Miss Reed," he says.

"Good morning."

"I've been sent," he says. "From the Office. The Registrar sent me." He swallows. "I'm very sorry."

"Sorry for what?"

He holds it out.

It's a paper. Folded in three, the Office way, and sealed with red wax stamped with the Office mark: a square, with *S.O.* inside. My name is on the front, in the neat upright hand I last saw at the bottom of a letter Mara read at the Highwood gate. Voss's hand.

I take it. My hands are perfectly steady. I break the seal.

---

*SURVEY OFFICE OF STONEBRIDGE*

*SUMMONS*

*Cass Reed, sworn surveyor of the Reach, first class, is hereby summoned to appear before the Registrar of the Survey Office in the map room at noon this day, to answer a charge laid under Clause Nine of the Cairn Treaty: namely, that the said surveyor has bonded with a wolf of the Highwood pack, in breach of that Clause.*

*Under Clause Nine, every survey signed by the said surveyor is suspended pending judgment, including the survey of the Highwood line filed yesterday.*

*A WARRANT has been issued under the same Clause for the arrest of the wolf, MARA of Highwood, heir of that pack.*

*The charge is laid upon the sworn statement of a witness, given before the Registrar yesterday evening, which is attached.*

Behind it, folded once, there's a second sheet. A witness statement. Short, formal, in a clerk's tidy hand. *I, the undersigned, being a sworn surveyor of the Reach, do solemnly state that I have observed...*

I don't read the rest. I can't. My eyes have gone straight to the bottom of the page, to the signature.

It's not a clerk's hand. It's small and slanted and precise, every letter crossed and dotted. The kind of hand I've spent my whole life trying to have. I've read it in a battered leather field book on a ledge at Thirteen. I've read it on a scrap of paper sealed in a cairn. I've read it in a letter Ruth kept in her furs for half a year. I've read it scratched in tiny letters on the side of a brass link that's sitting in my coat pocket right now.

*June Marsh.*

---

I don't know how long I sit there.

The common room is very quiet. Somewhere a sleeping wolf snores. The innkeeper has stopped sweeping. The young clerk is still standing beside me, shifting from foot to foot.

"This is a mistake," I say. My voice sounds far away. "June Marsh is dead. She died in the spring. In the ravine below Cairn Eleven. There's a report."

"I know, miss." The clerk's voice is very small. "I filed it."

I look up at him.

"She came in last night," he says. "After the carving. After everyone had gone. I was the only clerk still there, finishing the ledger. *First snow.*" He swallows. "She came in out of the snow, by herself, in an old coat, and she asked for the Registrar. And he came down, and he looked at her like—" He stops. "Like he'd seen a ghost, miss. I've never seen him look like that. Not once."

"And she signed this."

"In front of him. In front of me." The clerk's eyes are wet. "Then she left. Out into the snow again. She didn't say where she was going."

*Don't look for me under the stones,* June wrote. *You won't find me there.*

*If they come for me before I'm done, I won't wait to be found.*

I put my hand in my coat pocket and close my fingers around the brass link. It's cold. Its open end bites into my palm.

Above me, through the ceiling, through the floor of the slanted room, I feel Mara wake.

It goes through the tether all at once, like a door slammed open in a storm. She's awake, and she's sitting up, and she can feel what I'm feeling: the cold, and the dread, and something underneath both that I don't have a name for yet. I feel her confusion. I feel her fear, sudden and enormous. I feel her start to move, toward the door, toward the stairs, toward me.

"Miss Reed?" says the clerk. "Are you all right?"

I look down at the summons in my hand. At the word *WARRANT*, in capitals. At the name underneath it.

*MARA of Highwood, heir of that pack.*

At noon, I'm to walk into the map room in my socks and stand on the Highwood line I carved yesterday, and answer for loving her. And whatever happens to me there, they've already decided what happens to her.

*The wolf shall be put to death.*

On the stairs behind me, I hear her feet.

I fold the paper in three, very precisely, along its creases. I stand up. The clerk steps back. The innkeeper is watching me over his broom. Around me, one by one, the wolves of Highwood are waking up, lifting their heads, breathing in, smelling something in the air that wasn't there a minute ago.

Fear. Mine.

Mara comes down the last of the stairs and stops at the bottom, her hair wild, her coat half on, her eyes on my face.

"Cass," she says. "What is it?"

I hold out the paper.

She takes it. She reads it. I watch her read it, standing at the foot of the stairs in her half-buttoned coat with snow-light from the window falling across her face, and I feel every word land in her. *Clause Nine. Suspended. WARRANT.* Her own name, in capitals.

Then the signature.

She looks at it for a very long time. Her hand has started to shake. I've never seen her hand shake, not on the scree, not at the post in the Hollow with a silver knife at her throat.

"That's her hand," she whispers.

"Yes."

"That's *June's* hand. That's how she wrote *sullen*." Her eyes come up to mine, wide and dark and full of something worse than fear. "She's not dead."

All around us, Highwood is on its feet now. Lena has slid off the bar with her knife already out. Someone is running up the stairs, calling for Ruth. Somebody else has put their back against the door.

I can't make my mouth say the rest of it. So I say the only thing I can make sense of, the one number in all of this that doesn't fit, the one I keep coming back to.

"June Marsh is alive," I say.

And I don't say the rest. I don't need to. Mara can feel it through the tether, as clearly as if I'd shouted it across the room.

The woman who sent her away at Cairn Eight, to keep her safe, has just walked out of the snow and sworn, in front of the Registrar, to the one thing in the Reach that can get her killed.

---

*End of Book One*
