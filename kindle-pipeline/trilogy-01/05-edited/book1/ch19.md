# Chapter Nineteen

I count the links in my chain every morning. I have done since I was twenty-one, since my second week of training, when Agnes Bell caught me chaining a practice line with a twisted link and made me walk the whole thing again, all forty chains of it, in the rain. *Count it,* she said. *Every morning. Before you trust it with anything.*

So on the saddle at Fifteen, with frost on the grass and Holt's tent still shut and the sky behind the eastern peaks going from black to bruise to pale cold blue, I sit on my blanket and run my chain through my hands, link by link, the way I always do.

Ten. The first silver link, cold as ice.

Twenty. Thirty. Forty.

Mara is sitting a few feet away, watching me. She's been watching me do this for two weeks. She stopped asking why after the second morning.

Seventy. Eighty. Ninety.

Ninety-nine.

I stop.

I go back to the first brass handle. I start again, slower. I count out loud this time, under my breath, the way I did on the scree slope. Mara has gone still. I can feel her listening.

Ninety-nine.

"What?" she says.

"It's short." My voice is very calm. "There should be a hundred. There are ninety-nine."

"You miscounted."

"I don't miscount."

I run the chain through my fingers a third time, looking now instead of counting, looking at every join. Each link in a surveyor's chain is a length of brass wire bent into a long loop at each end, and the loops are joined with little rings. You can open a ring with pliers. You can close it again. If you're careful and you know what you're doing, nobody would ever see.

Between the forty-third and the forty-fourth link, someone wasn't quite careful enough. The little ring has been opened and closed. There are fresh tool marks on it: two tiny bright bites in the old brass, where the jaws of a pair of pliers gripped and turned.

I hold it up so Mara can see.

She looks at it for a long time. Then she looks across the frozen grass at Holt's tent.

"Last night," she says. "He came over to talk to you. About your *readings*. He sat by your pack."

"I was asleep."

"I wasn't." Her mouth thins. "I watched him the whole time. He didn't touch your pack. I'd have seen." A pause. "He touched the chain. When he picked it up, to admire it. He said it was a lovely old chain. He held it for a minute. He was talking the whole time."

I remember. Half asleep, I'd heard Holt's voice by the fire, pleasant and dull, saying something about Office standards and old brass, and I hadn't even opened my eyes.

A minute. That's all you'd need, with practice and small pliers in your pocket. One ring open, one link out, one ring closed again.

"Why?" Mara asks. "One link. What does one link do?"

"One link is eight inches," I say. "Out of sixty-six feet. It's nothing. You'd never see it in one length. But every length I chain with this, I'm calling sixty-six feet when it's really sixty-five feet and four inches. Across a mile, that's—" I do it in my head. "Fifty-three feet out. Across the four miles of the true line, it's two hundred feet. Every cairn I set would be further out than it really is."

"Further out." Mara frowns. "That's *more* forest."

"On my survey. Yes." I look down at the bright little tool marks. "And then he checks my survey against his chain, which is new and perfect and Office-standard. And all my distances are wrong. All of them. Every single one, too long, in Highwood's favour." I close my fist around the chain. "And he stands up in the map room in Stonebridge and says, *I'm afraid Miss Reed's measurements cannot be trusted. Her chain was short. And look who it favoured.*"

Mara stares at me.

"He wasn't trying to stop me walking the true line," I say. "He was making sure that if I did, nobody would believe it."

---

I have a spare link. Every surveyor carries a little tin of them, and rings, and a pair of pliers of their own. I sit on my blanket and mend the chain with cold fingers, and then I count it again, all hundred links, and then I get up and walk across the frozen grass to Holt's tent.

He's already up. He's sitting on Theo's folding stool by the remains of the fire, writing in a small black notebook, and he looks up as I come over with a pleasant, forgettable smile.

"Miss Reed. Good morning. I hope you slept—"

"My chain had ninety-nine links this morning," I say.

His smile doesn't change at all. "I beg your pardon?"

"Ninety-nine. It had a hundred last night, and this morning it had ninety-nine. The ring between forty-three and forty-four has been opened with pliers." I hold out my hand. "I'd like my link back, please."

"I'm afraid I don't understand."

"I think you do."

Behind him, Theo's tent flap opens. Theo comes out, tousled and blinking, a blanket round his shoulders, and stops dead, and his eyes go from me to Holt to me again, and he looks delighted.

"Miss Reed," says Holt patiently, "you're tired. You're on your first posting, in very difficult country, and you've been through a frightening experience at the ravine. It's perfectly natural to miscount."

"I don't miscount."

"Everyone miscounts."

"I don't," I say. "I've counted my chain every morning for six years, through rain and hail and one memorable bout of food poisoning. I counted it every morning on this line. It's the one thing I do every day without fail, and it's the one thing on this mountain I'm absolutely sure of." I keep my hand out. "So. My link."

Holt looks at my hand. Then he looks at me. For a moment something comes into his narrow pleasant face, something cold and quick and assessing. Then it's gone, and he smiles again, and shakes his head sadly.

"I'll note your concern in my report," he says.

"Do," I say. "Note it very precisely. Ninety-nine links. Between forty-three and forty-four. Fresh pliers marks on the ring." I lower my hand. "And note that from now on, I'll be counting my chain every night as well as every morning. And so will my escort."

Theo laughs out loud. He turns it into a cough very quickly.

Holt doesn't laugh. He closes his little black notebook and puts it inside his coat and looks at me with no expression at all.

"Shall we begin?" he says. "Cairn Sixteen. The Office bearing, I believe."

---

We chain Sixteen, Seventeen and Eighteen with Holt walking at my shoulder like a bad smell. He checks every length with his own chain. He writes in his notebook. He compares my bearings to the Office copy, out loud, in a mild voice, and every time they agree, he nods and says, *Very good*, as if he's surprised.

They agree because between Fourteen and Nineteen, the Office line and the true line are the same. They agree because I haven't done anything wrong yet.

Tomorrow we reach Nineteen.

Mara and I argue about it that night, in whispers, behind our boulder, while Holt's lamp burns in his tent and Theo sits by the fire pretending not to listen.

"Don't tell him," she says.

"I have to. At Nineteen, the lines split. The true cairn is a quarter mile out. If I chain to it, he'll see. He's standing *right next to me*, Mara."

"Then don't chain to it. Not with him there. Chain the Office line, let him write his little notes, let him go back down. Then we come back up on our own and do it properly."

"There isn't time. First snow—"

"Then we lose a few cairns!" Her whisper cracks. "We get back the Long Wood and the Pools and we lose Nineteen to Twenty-Four. That's still more forest than Highwood has had in my lifetime. My mother would take that. She'd *weep* for that."

"And when I file it? When I stand in the map room with a survey that's true in one place and false in another, because I didn't want to be seen?" I shake my head. "That's not a survey, Mara. That's a negotiation. That's what the man who moved our fence did. He measured what he was paid to measure and he didn't measure the rest."

"That's not the same."

"It's exactly the same. It's just in your favour." I'm shaking. I can't stop. "I'm a surveyor. I chain what's true. I chain it in front of whoever's watching. If Holt wants to see me do it, he can watch. If he runs to Voss, *let him*. Voss already knows. Voss knew about the ravine before anyone told him. Voss sent the—" I stop. I make my voice go down. "It doesn't matter what Holt sees. It only matters what I file."

She looks at me in the dark for a long time.

"They'll kill you," she says. It's what Ruth said. It's what she said, on the rock above the ravine. "You said it yourself. They'll only have to manage one of us."

"I know."

"And you'll do it anyway."

"I'd do it if you weren't here," I say. "I'd have done it if I'd never met you. That's the point. That's why it's worth anything." I swallow. "Your mother asked me to keep you alive. She didn't ask me to stop being a surveyor. I don't think she'd want me to. I don't think *you* would."

Mara makes a sound like she's been hit.

"No," she says at last, very low. "I wouldn't."

She doesn't say anything else. She lies down with her back to me, close, closer than she has since the bothy. After a while I lie down too. Our backs touch. Neither of us moves away.

---

I wake before dawn because the needle has moved.

That's how it feels. Not pain. Distance. All night the compass in my chest has pointed at the warm weight against my back, steady as north. Now it's swung right round and is pointing away, northeast, a long way off, and the space beside me is cold.

I sit up. Mara is gone. Her blanket is gone. Her bow is gone.

Across the saddle, the flap of Holt's tent is hanging open in the wind. The tent is empty. The pony is gone. And my pack has been moved. It's lying on its side, a yard from where I left it, the top flap unbuckled.

I'm on my knees beside it before I know I've moved. I tear it open.

June's book is there. Still at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, under my socks. He didn't go that deep. My Office copy of the line is gone. And my own field book, the one I keep in the outside pocket, where I write the day's numbers, and at the back, on the private pages, my working: the old bearings, the sketch of the toppled Nine, my notes about the recut groove on the map-room floor, the pry bar, *Office told us there'd be no trouble on this stretch.*

Gone.

Theo is standing at the door of his own tent, wrapped in his blanket, looking at Holt's empty one. For once, he isn't smiling.

"Where's Mara?" I say.

He looks at me. "She went after him. An hour ago. I heard her go." He hesitates. "He went northeast. Off the line. Down toward the sinkholes."

I don't need him to tell me. I've known since I opened my eyes. The needle hasn't stopped pointing: northeast, a long way off, moving fast and low, downhill.

Toward the sinkholes. Toward the country nobody holds.

"That's the Hollow," says Theo quietly. "That's all open ground."
