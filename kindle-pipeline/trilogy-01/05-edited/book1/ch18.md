# Chapter Eighteen

We leave the hold the next morning, and Mara and I have not talked about the kiss.

We haven't talked about anything. Not about the kiss, or the wolf at the edge of the trees, or the gold in our eyes, or what Ruth said to me in the empty hall. When I came back from the hall yesterday, Mara was awake and waiting on the step of the guesthouse, and she looked at my face and opened her mouth, and I said, "Your mother knows," and she closed it again.

That was all. She nodded once. She went very pale. Then she went to help Lena with the woodpile, and I went to my long table in the hall and drew the true line until my hand cramped, and we didn't look at each other across the fire at supper.

It's not that I don't want to talk to her. I want to so badly it's like a stone in my chest. It's that every time I look at her, I think about Ruth saying *you go home, and you don't come back*, and about nodding, and about not telling her.

She can feel it. I know she can. Not the words; just the shape of it. Guilt, sitting in me like a swallowed coin.

She hasn't asked. That's almost worse.

That first night, coming back from the hold to the line, we camp in a hollow under the last of the big larches, and she lays her blanket on one side of the fire and I lay mine on the other, and we lie down without a word. It's the bothy all over again, except backwards. Further apart, instead of closer.

I wake in the dark, deep in the night, because I'm warm.

I shouldn't be. The fire is out. The frost has come down hard; I can see it on the needles by starlight, a fine white fur over everything. My breath is smoking. But I'm warm, all the way through, and it takes me a stupid, sleepy moment to understand why.

There are two blankets on me. Mine, and hers.

I lift my head. She's on the far side of the dead fire, curled on her side on the bare needles, her coat pulled tight around her, her knees drawn up. Her eyes are closed. She isn't asleep. I can feel it: the slow careful breathing of someone pretending.

She's cold. I can feel that too. A deep ache in her hands and feet, the same ache I'd have if I were lying where she is.

She knows I can feel it. She did it anyway.

I lie there with her blanket pulled up to my chin, smelling of woodsmoke and resin and her, and something in my chest gives way like a step on scree.

I get up. I take her blanket across the dead fire and lay it over her, and tuck it round her shoulders, and she doesn't open her eyes, and she doesn't say anything. Neither do I. I go back to my side and lie down.

A long time later, out of the dark, she says very quietly, "Stubborn."

"You started it," I say.

I'm going to tell her. Everything Ruth said, and everything I said back. Not tonight. But soon. Before this turns into one more thing someone decided for her.

---

It takes us the rest of that first day just to get back to the line from the hold. We chain Fourteen in the early afternoon of the second. It's a true cairn: old mark, June's initials, the line through the circle pointing exactly where June's book says it should. I stand beside it and look back down the ridge, south, toward the gold blur of the forest below, the grey gash of the ravine, and further off, out of sight, the toppled stones of the true Nine under their fallen larch.

"We'll have to go back," I say. "For Nine. And Twelve."

June's book is very clear. In the stretch from Nine to Fourteen, two cairns were moved: Nine, and then Twelve, both pulled down and rebuilt inward, both stamped *S.O.* We walked the Office Nine and the Office Twelve on the way up, because I didn't know better yet. To file a true survey, I'll have to chain the true ones.

"My mother's sending people," Mara says. "To rebuild them. Lena's going, with six others. They'll have the stones back up by the time you come down."

"I'll chain them on the way back. To Stonebridge. It's the same road." I look at her. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Thank my mother." She coils the chain handle around her fist. Then, without looking at me: "She told you to go home. Didn't she."

The wind comes hard off the high ground, cold enough to make my eyes water.

"Yes," I say.

Mara nods slowly. She doesn't look surprised. She looks like someone who's been carrying a sum in her head for a day and has just had it confirmed.

"And you said yes."

"I said I'd keep you alive." It comes out rough. "That's not quite the same thing."

She looks at me then. Her eyes are amber in the daylight, no gold, nothing anyone could see. But I can feel what's behind them. It isn't anger. It's something much more tired.

"Everybody's always keeping me alive," she says. "June. My mother. You." She turns away, toward the next stretch of line. "Nobody ever asks what I want."

"What do you want?"

She stops. Her back is to me. The wind whips the end of her braid sideways like a flag.

"Chain," she says. "We're losing the light."

Past Fourteen, the line stops pretending.

It's been climbing all along, really, in long slow rises and sudden steps. The larches thin out and shrink, gnarled and wind-bent, clinging to the rock in twisted knots. Then they stop altogether, and there's only grass, short and tough and silver with frost in the mornings, and grey rock, and sky. The air is thin. I'm breathing twice as hard as usual and getting half as much for it.

"Above the tree line," Mara says, when she sees me stop for the third time in an hour. "It's like this until Twenty."

"Lovely," I say. "Splendid. Can't wait."

She almost smiles. Almost.

---

Fifteen sits on a bare saddle of rock between two higher peaks, with a long view west over River's valleys and east over a tumbled wilderness of grey ridges and black tarns. It's the highest cairn on the line so far. It's also, I realise as we come up the last slope toward it in the cold blue afternoon, already occupied.

There's a fire. A small, neat one, sheltered behind the cairn. There are two tents, good canvas, River-made. There's a pony, shaggy and miserable, hobbled in the lee of a boulder.

And there's Theo, sitting on a folding stool by the fire with a cup in his hand, looking as if he's been waiting for us all his life.

"Mara!" he calls. "Surveyor! Come in, come in. You look frozen. Tea?"

Mara stops dead on the slope. I feel her go rigid all the way through.

"Steady," I say, under my breath.

"I'm steady."

"You're showing your teeth."

She closes her mouth.

There's someone else at the fire. He gets up as we come closer: a lean man in his forties, sandy-haired, clean-shaven, wearing a good grey Office coat buttoned all the way up. His face is narrow and pleasant and forgettable. He has a satchel across his chest just like mine, and a chain coiled at his feet just like mine, brass and silver, brand new, shining.

And his fingers are stained with ink up to the second knuckle.

"Miss Reed," he says. He holds out his hand. "Ambrose Holt. Survey Office, second class. The Registrar has asked me to assist you."

I shake it. His hand is cold. His grip is firm and dry and short.

Beside me, Mara draws in a long, slow breath through her nose.

I feel the tether hum, a sudden hard spike of alarm. I glance sideways. Mara's nostrils have flared, and her lips move, just barely, without sound. I'd know the shape of those words anywhere now. *Lamp oil.*

"Assist me with what, Mr. Holt?" I ask, smiling.

"With your survey, of course." He smiles back. It's a very thin smile. "The Highwood line is long, the season's late, and you're on your first posting. The Registrar felt it would be prudent to have a second pair of eyes. Simply to confirm your readings against the Office copy." He nods at my satchel. "You have the Office copy, I take it?"

"I do."

"Excellent. Then we're in agreement about which line we're walking." His eyes go to Mara, briefly, and then back to me, and stay there. "And the Registrar was very clear on that point. The Office line. Cairn to cairn. As recorded."

The fire crackles. Behind Holt, Theo takes a sip of his tea and watches me over the rim of the cup with bright, interested blue eyes, the way you'd watch a game of cards when you've already seen everyone's hand.

"Of course," I say.

---

We make camp on the saddle, because there's nowhere else to make it. Holt has his tent. Theo has his. Mara and I have the lee side of a boulder, a good twenty yards away, and our blankets.

I sit with my back to the rock and watch the light go off the peaks, gold and then rose and then grey. Mara sits beside me. Not sixty-six feet away. Right beside me, our shoulders almost touching, because there's nowhere else to sit.

"Is it him?" I ask quietly. "From the ravine?"

"No." She keeps her voice very low. "The one at the ravine was heavier. Bigger feet. And he was afraid." A pause. "This one isn't afraid."

"But the lamp oil."

"Same lamp oil," she says. "Same paper. The same place." She turns her head and looks across the saddle at the neat Office tent, where a lamp has been lit, and Holt's narrow shadow moves on the canvas. "He works where the other one works. He's been in the same rooms. Sat at the same desks."

"The Office."

"The Office."

Across the fire, Theo has stood up. He's stretching, yawning, a big lazy theatrical yawn. Then he strolls, unhurried, across the frozen grass toward us, and stops a polite distance off, with his hands in his pockets.

"Cosy," he says.

"What do you want, Theo?" says Mara.

"Me? Nothing. I'm just the escort." He grins. "Holt asked River to see him up the mountain. River said yes. River always says yes to the Office." His eyes rest on me. "You didn't take my advice, surveyor."

"Which advice?"

"At Seven. *Measure twice.*" He tilts his head. "Or did you?"

Neither of us answers.

Theo looks at us both for a long, thoughtful moment. Then his gaze drops, just for a second, to the narrow strip of ground between our shoulders. To the place where they almost touch.

Something goes across his face too fast to read.

"Well," he says lightly. "Sleep well. Big day tomorrow. Holt's very keen to see your numbers."

He turns to go. Then he stops, the way he did on the slope at Seven, and looks back.

"Oh. And Holt asked me to pass this on. From the Registrar himself." He smiles. "He was very sorry to hear about your accident at the ravine. He hopes your hand is healing."

He walks away, back toward the fire.

I sit frozen against the boulder.

My hand. My bandaged left hand, resting in my lap.

Nobody told the Office about the ravine. No runner has gone down from the hold. Ruth wouldn't. Mara wouldn't. I haven't written a single page since Thirteen that isn't in the bottom of my pack under my socks.

There's only one way Voss could know about the accident at the ravine.

Beside me, Mara has gone so still she's barely breathing. Through the tether, I feel her understand it a heartbeat after I do.

"He knows," I whisper, "because he sent it."
