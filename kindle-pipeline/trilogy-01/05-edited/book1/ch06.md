# Chapter Six

Cairn Eight is the last place June Marsh was seen alive, and Mara won't go within twenty yards of it.

She doesn't say so. She doesn't have to. We chain up to it from Seven through a long morning of thin cold sunshine, and with every length the brass handle in her hand gets a little lower, and her steps get a little shorter, until on the last length before the cairn she stops dead with the chain only half-taut and won't walk on.

"Mara."

"Here's fine."

"It's twelve feet short."

"Then measure the twelve feet."

I look at her. She's staring at the cairn, which is just a cairn: a grey heap on a rocky step above a long drop, daubed with tired red, a few tufts of grass growing out of the gaps in the stones. There's nothing about it to frighten anyone. But Mara looks at it the way I'd look at the scree slope, if the scree slope had personally killed someone I loved.

I don't argue. I walk the last twelve feet myself, with the chain's end trailing behind me, and pin the length, and set up the theodolite.

There's a surveyor's mark scratched into the third stone down, the same as on every other cairn. A circle with a line through it, the date seven years ago. And under it, *J.M.*, this spring.

I've been looking at these marks for three days. I've touched every one. It's only now, crouched beside Cairn Eight with my nose almost against the stone, that I really *see* one.

The line through the circle isn't decoration. It's not a flourish. It's straight and deep and deliberate, and it points.

I turn and look back down the ridge. From here I can just see Cairn Seven, a red speck far below on its knuckle of rock. The line on the previous surveyor's mark on Seven had pointed here. I'm almost sure of it. I never thought to check.

I check now. I lay my pencil along the scratched line on Cairn Eight's stone and sight along it, out across the slope. It points northeast, out over a long gold shoulder of forest toward the next spur.

Then I open the Office copy of the line, find Cairn Nine, and read off the bearing and distance from Eight.

*Bearing: 52 degrees. Distance: 38 chains.*

I sight it with the theodolite. Fifty-two degrees puts Cairn Nine not on the far spur but in a little dip much closer in, deeper among the trees.

The line on the old mark points somewhere else.

I sit back on my heels.

"What?" says Mara, from twenty yards away.

"Nothing." It comes out too fast. "Just checking the angle."

---

We chain toward Nine.

I follow the Office bearing, because that's what the Office copy says, and that's what I'm here to walk. Thirty-eight chains: a little under half a mile. I count every length out loud, and Mara holds the handle steady, and we go down off the rocky step into the forest and along a gentle slope where the larches grow close together and the light goes the colour of weak tea.

At thirty-eight chains, almost exactly, there's a cairn.

It's not like the others. It's smaller, for a start, and much neater, the slabs squared and fitted together as if somebody took care. The ochre on its capstone is red but not wet-bright like River's paint on Seven. It's a season old, maybe. There's no lichen on the stones. There's no grass growing out of the gaps. There's a surveyor's mark scratched into the face, but only one, and it isn't June's.

It's an Office stamp: a little square with the letters *S.O.* inside, and a date from this summer.

"Cairn Nine," says Mara.

"Is it?"

She frowns at me. "The Office moved it. After the rockfall." She nods northward, vaguely, toward the far spur. "There was a notice. My mother got a letter. The ground had slipped, they said. The old cairn went down with it. They sent a man up in summer to build this one."

"A surveyor?"

"A man with a hammer." She shrugs. "We weren't invited."

I look at the neat little cairn. At the letters *S.O.* and the summer date. At the absence of lichen.

*When you get to Nine, measure twice.*

"I want to close the angle," I say.

"What does that mean?"

"It means I'm going to take a sight back to Seven and Eight from here, and see if the triangle adds up." I'm already setting up the theodolite, my hands moving faster than my thoughts. "Three angles in a triangle have to make a hundred and eighty degrees. Always. If they don't, something's in the wrong place."

"Something like what?"

"Like me," I say. "Probably. It's probably me."

It isn't me.

I take the sight to Eight. I take the sight to Seven, which I can just catch through a gap in the trees, a red speck on the far knuckle. I do the sums in my field book, carefully, twice. Then I do them a third time, because a third time is what you do when the first two scare you.

The triangle doesn't close. It's out by more than three degrees. Over these distances, three degrees isn't an error. It's a different place.

I do some more arithmetic. It's not hard arithmetic. I could do it in my sleep. I just don't want to.

If the line through the old mark on Cairn Eight is right, then Cairn Nine, the real Cairn Nine, ought to be about a quarter of a mile further out. Northeast. Toward the far spur. Toward the edge of the forest, and the open slope beyond it.

A quarter of a mile. Twenty chains.

The same distance the line bends inward on the floor of the map room.

---

"I'm going to take a look at something," I tell Mara.

"At what?"

"Just the ground. Up there." I wave vaguely northeast. "For the ravine. I want to see where the slip happened."

It's not even a lie. I do want to see where the slip happened. I want to see it very much. I want to see it because I'm almost certain I won't find it.

She looks at me for a long, measuring moment. Then she slings her bow over her shoulder and follows.

I walk on the old bearing. I don't chain it; I just pace, counting under my breath, compass in one hand. The trees thin. The ground rises gently, firm and dry under the needles. There's no slip. There's no scar, no raw earth, no tumble of fallen rock. There's just more forest, gold and quiet, and the far spur getting closer.

At about nineteen chains, I nearly fall over it.

It's lying on its side under a fallen larch, half buried in needles and moss. A heap of grey slabs that used to be a stack, spilled out across the ground like dice from a cup. Moss has grown over most of them. Lichen, yellow and grey, crusts the rest. And on the largest, wedged under the trunk of the fallen tree, the faded rust-red of very old ochre.

I crouch. I brush away moss with my sleeve.

There's a mark scratched in the stone. A circle with a line through it, seven years old. Under it, *J.M.* and this spring's date.

And beside the mark, low on the stone where the slabs meet the earth, there's a fresh, pale gouge, about the width of my thumb. The kind of gouge you get when someone drives the point of an iron bar between two stones, and leans on it until the stack goes over.

I put my thumb in it. It fits.

Behind me, needles crunch.

I stand up, fast, and turn, and Mara is right there, three paces off, looking past me at the spilled stones. I can't tell how much she's seen. I can't tell what she's thinking. Her face is very still.

"That's old," she says. "That's from before the slip."

"Yes," I say.

"So it fell in the slip."

I look at the gouge. I look at the forest around us, dry and whole and untouched, for a quarter mile in every direction. I look at Mara, whose people have lost two miles of ground in her lifetime. Who sat by a fire two nights ago and told me she didn't think the clean-handed Registrar was going to give any of it back.

And I think: *if I tell her, what does she do?*

She goes back to her mother. Highwood marches down to River's cairns, or to Stonebridge, or straight to the Survey Office. And I'm a surveyor with three days' experience and one bad triangle, and no proof of anything except a gouge in a stone that anyone could have made at any time, and the line still lapses at first snow, and the hunters come up onto the ridge, and Aled is not the last person with a tag on his finger.

Or I'm wrong. I'm wrong, and I've just started a war over a bad sum.

"Probably," I say.

It's the worst thing I've ever said. It sits in my mouth like the iron taste of the stream at the gate.

Mara looks at me. Then she looks at the stones again, for a long time. Then she turns and walks back toward the neat little Office cairn, and I follow her, and neither of us says anything at all.

---

We pin the Office cairn as Nine. I write the Office bearing in my field book, and the Office distance, and the Office everything. And then, on a fresh page at the back, the page I keep for my own working, I write the old bearing, and the number nineteen, and a small careful sketch of a cairn spilled on its side under a fallen tree.

I don't show it to anyone.

It's not the same as lying, I tell myself. A survey isn't filed until it's carved. Between now and first snow I can walk this line a dozen ways, and keep a dozen pages of working, and the only page that becomes law is the one I lay on the slate floor in Stonebridge at the end. So I'll walk it both ways. I'll keep the Office's numbers and my own, side by side, the way you keep two columns of a sum you don't trust. And when I've got enough, when I've got more than one gouge and one bad triangle, I'll decide.

*A surveyor belongs to no one,* Voss said, with his clean hands folded. *Only to the line.*

The trouble is that I'm no longer sure which line he meant. The one on the floor of his map room, or the one scratched into a fallen stone under a larch, pointing northeast.

I close the field book and put it in the bottom of my pack, under my spare socks, where nobody would think to look. Then I feel ridiculous, and leave it there anyway.

By late afternoon the sky behind the far spur has gone a strange, bruised yellow, and the wind is coming the wrong way, hard and cold out of the north. Mara keeps looking up at it. She keeps looking at me, too, when she thinks I'm busy with the theodolite. I can feel it, every time, like a draught on the back of my neck.

We're packing up the chain when she finally says it.

"You found something."

"I found a fallen cairn."

"You found something else."

"Mara—"

"You count when you're frightened," she says. Her voice is low and level and absolutely certain. "I told you that. I didn't tell you the rest." She takes the brass handle, coils the last few links around it, and holds it out to me, and her eyes are the colour of the sky behind her, gold and darkening. "You count when you lie, too."

I take the handle.

I don't answer.

Above us, somewhere over the far spur, the first long roll of thunder comes rumbling down the ridge.
