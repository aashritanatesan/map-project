# Chapter Five

We chain Cairns Four, Five and Six in a single long day, and neither of us says a word about the spur.

It's not that we're avoiding it. It's more that it's there, between us, the way the ravine was there between Cairns One and Two: something we have to keep crossing, over and over, without looking down. Mara holds the brass handle. I hold the other end. I don't flick the chain once. When it kinks, I walk the whole length of it and untwist the links with my fingers, and if she notices she doesn't say anything, but she stops holding her right wrist so far from her body.

The ridge bends south after Cairn Five. The ground falls away on our left into long green valleys, softer than anything I've seen in the Reach, with a river threading silver through the bottom of them and smoke rising from somewhere far down where I can't see the houses.

"River ground," Mara says, when she catches me looking.

"It's pretty."

"So's a snake."

"Everything up here is pretty in a way that wants to kill me." I take my bearing on Cairn Six. "It's a theme."

We eat at Cairn Six, sitting on opposite sides of it with our backs to the stones, like two people who have been seated at the same table at a wedding and are trying to make the best of it. Mara has more venison. I have the end of the innkeeper's bread, which has gone hard enough to drive pins with.

I'm working my way through it when she says, without looking around the cairn, "Why do you count?"

I stop chewing. "What?"

"Everything. Steps. Trees. You counted the stones in the stream yesterday. I heard you." A pause. "You count under your breath when you're frightened."

I hadn't known she could hear that. I'm not sure I'd known I did it out loud.

"It helps," I say, after a moment.

"With what?"

"With the—" I wave my bread at the general mountain. "The everything. When I was little, things kept changing without anyone telling me. Where we lived. Who was in charge of what. Whether the fence at the bottom of our field was ours or someone else's." I turn the crust over in my hands. "Numbers don't do that. Forty is forty on Tuesday and forty on Friday and forty on a scree slope with a wolf watching you. You can stand on a number."

Silence, from the other side of the cairn.

"That's stupid," Mara says eventually.

"Thank you."

"No." I hear her shift against the stones. "I mean it's stupid that it works. It shouldn't. A number's not a floor." Another pause, longer. "My mother sings when she's frightened. Under her breath. The old border songs. All the verses, start to finish, even the ones about cairns we lost before I was born." A breath. "I used to think that was stupid too."

I don't say anything. I don't think she wants me to. I sit with my back against the cold slate and listen to the wind come up out of the River valley, and on the other side of the cairn, so quietly I'm not sure I'm meant to hear it, someone hums three notes and stops.

Then she stands, and the brass handle clinks, and we go on.

---

Cairn Seven is wrong.

I see it from half a mile off, before I've even set up the theodolite. Every other cairn on the line has been daubed the same tired rust-red, the ochre weathered and cracked by seven winters. Cairn Seven glows. It sits on a knuckle of rock where the ridge splits, Highwood forest on one side, River valley on the other, and the red on its capstone is so fresh and bright it looks wet.

"Mara."

"I see it."

We walk to it together. Up close it's even stranger. The ochre isn't just fresh on top. It's been painted down the sides, in long careful strokes, and in the wet-looking red someone has drawn a mark I don't recognise: three wavy lines, one above another, like water.

"That's not a survey mark," I say.

"No." Mara's voice is very flat. "It's River's."

"Why would River paint—"

"Because they want you to see it." She's not looking at the cairn. She's looking past it, down into the valley, and her hand has gone to her bow. "Because they want *me* to see it."

"Very good," says a voice behind us. "Ten points to Highwood."

I turn so fast I nearly drop the theodolite.

There are three of them. They've come up out of the valley side so quietly that I didn't hear a thing, which shouldn't surprise me by now, but does. Two are big and silent and wear long fur-trimmed coats the colour of wet bark. The third is out in front, and he's smiling.

He's maybe thirty, red-blond, with a short beard and the kind of open, sunburned face that makes you want to tell it things. His coat is better than the others', with silver buttons. He has a pack on his back and a walking stick, not a bow, and he's holding the stick loosely, as if he's never once had to hit anyone with it.

"Theo," says Mara. It isn't a greeting.

"Mara. Always a joy." He doesn't come closer. He's very careful, I notice, to stay on the valley side of the cairn. Then he turns the smile on me, and it brightens, like a window catching the sun. "And you must be the new surveyor. Theo of River. I'd shake your hand, but I suspect Highwood would shoot me for it."

"I wouldn't waste the arrow," Mara says.

"See? Charming. She's always like this." He sweeps a hand at the cairn. "Do you like it? We tidied up. It was looking very shabby."

"You're not supposed to touch the cairns," I say. "Only a sworn surveyor marks a cairn."

"Oh, we didn't *mark* it." He looks wounded. "We'd never. We just decorated. It's a border cairn, half of it's ours. We thought it deserved a bit of colour." His eyes flick to Mara, and for just a second the warmth in them goes thin as ice. "Seeing as Highwood can't be bothered to look after its own."

Mara doesn't move. But I'm standing close enough to her to feel something change: a tightening in the air, like the moment before a thunderstorm when every hair on your arms lifts at once.

"Theo," I say, loudly and brightly, "it's very nice to meet you. Is there something we can do for you? Because we've got a lot of mountain left to measure."

He laughs. It's a good laugh, warm and easy, and I hate that I like it.

"Straight to business. I like her, Mara. Where did you find her?"

"Stonebridge," says Mara. "Same place your orders come from."

That lands. I watch it land. The smile stays on Theo's face, but it stops reaching his eyes.

"I'm here," he says to me, ignoring her, "to make you an offer. Purely neighbourly. You've got, what, twenty-four cairns left? Some of them are in terrible country. Sinkholes. Sheer drops. Hunters." He shrugs. "And your escort is, let's be kind, a little *distracted* these days. Her mother's ill. The pack's nervous. Highwood isn't what it was."

"Get to it," Mara says.

"River would be honoured to take over." He spreads his hands. "Three of our best, to see you safely to Cairn Thirty-One. Proper beds, down in the valley, every few nights. Hot food. Decent wine. Not"—he glances at Mara's camp-stained coat, my needle-studded hair—"whatever this is."

I'll be honest. For one second, the words *proper beds* go into me like a hook into a fish.

Then I think of a heap of slate on a mountain, painted with three wavy lines, on a cairn that is half Highwood's.

"That's very kind," I say. "But the treaty says the surveyor's escort comes from the pack whose line is being walked. And this is the Highwood line."

"The treaty says lots of things."

"Yes. That's sort of the point of it."

Theo studies me. His eyes are very blue and very sharp, and suddenly I'm aware that the easy smile and the walking stick and the sunburned face are all a sort of costume, and under it is someone who has been doing sums in his head since before I opened my mouth.

"Fair enough," he says lightly. "Offer stands, though. Any time." He turns to Mara, and his voice drops into something almost gentle, which is somehow worse than the ice. "She's better than the last one at saying no. I'll give her that."

Mara's jaw tightens.

"June said yes, you know," Theo goes on, softly. "To the beds. Twice. Lovely woman. Very curious." He tilts his head. "The last time I saw her, she was walking with you, Mara. Up toward Cairn Eight. And then she wasn't walking with anybody."

The silence after that has teeth.

Mara's fingers are white on her bow. I don't think she's breathing. I don't think, for a moment, that she's entirely in her own body. There's that door again, behind her eyes, and this time it isn't opening a crack. It's straining at the hinges, and whatever's behind it is not small, and it is not calm.

"Thank you, Theo," I say. I step between them, which is a stupid thing to do, which I do anyway. I keep my eyes on his face and my voice very polite. "We'll think about your offer. Good luck with your decorating."

For a moment he just looks at me. Then the smile comes back, real or not, and he sketches a little bow.

"Surveyor."

His two silent companions turn and start back down the valley side. Theo follows, unhurried, the walking stick swinging. Ten yards down the slope, he stops and looks back over his shoulder.

"Oh," he says. "One more thing. Professional courtesy." He nods at the theodolite in my arms. "Your Registrar asked me to keep an eye on you. Lovely man. Very clean hands." He smiles. "When you get to Nine, measure twice."

Then he's gone, down among the green, and the valley swallows him.

---

I turn around.

Mara is standing exactly where she was, by the bright-red cairn. She hasn't moved. Her bow is still in her hand. She's staring down at the place where Theo disappeared, and her face is the colour of the slate.

"Mara."

Nothing.

"Mara, what did he mean? About June?"

"Nothing." It comes out rough. "He meant nothing. He wanted to see what I'd do."

"He said you were walking with her. Up toward Eight."

She turns. And the look she gives me isn't angry. It's much worse than angry. It's the look of a person who has just been told that the thing she's been trying very hard not to think about is standing right behind her.

"Measure your cairn," she says. "We're losing the light."

I want to push. Everything in me that counts and checks and corrects wants to push. But I look at her hand on the bow, at the bandage on her wrist, at the bright-red ochre drying on the cairn behind her, and I think: *not now.* Not with Theo's voice still hanging in the air like smoke.

So I set up the theodolite over Cairn Seven. I level the bubble. I take my bearing on Cairn Eight. My hands are shaking, a little, and I have to do it twice.

*When you get to Nine, measure twice.*

Behind me, I hear Mara walk to the edge of the ridge and stand there, facing the valley. She doesn't hum this time. She doesn't make any sound at all. When I glance back, she's pressed the heel of her burned hand against her mouth, hard, as if there's something in there she can't afford to let out.

I look away. I give her that.

I write the angle in my field book. And underneath it, very small, in the margin where June used to write about the wind, I write:

*Why does River know about Nine?*
